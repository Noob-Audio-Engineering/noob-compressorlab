# The dbx 160: research notes for the true-RMS VCA side of `noob-compressorlab`

Research dossier for the dbx 160 model of the `noob-compressorlab` example plug-in of
noob-vst-webgui-framework. The example is a humorous, affectionate spoof of the dbx 160 family. It is not a
product, it is not a parity replacement for anything, and it does not use the dbx, 160, 160A, OverEasy,
Infinity+ or Blackmer names as its own name. Trademarks below belong to their owners and are used only to
identify the devices discussed. This model sits behind the same per-instance `model` switch that already
selects the 1176, LA-2A, LA-3A, CL 1B, Distressor and Neve behaviours; see [[Distressor]], [[Neve-33609]],
[[1176]] and [[LA-2A]].

Conventions, kept the same as the other dossiers so they all read alike:

- Citations are `[n]`; the numbered list in section 13 gives the URL for every source, and reference-style
  link definitions at the very end make the `[n]` markers clickable.
- Numbers that come from a manufacturer specification, a manual, a schematic or a datasheet are attributed.
  Numbers that are my own arithmetic are labelled **derived**; numbers that are my own guess are labelled
  **estimate**. Nothing labelled a measurement was invented. Where sources disagree, both figures are given
  and the disagreement is stated rather than resolved.
- "GR" is gain reduction. "THD" is total harmonic distortion. "VCA" is a voltage-controlled amplifier.
  dBu is 0.775 V RMS, dBV is 1 V RMS, dBFS is digital full scale. dbx's own documents use "dBm" and "dB"
  loosely for what is almost always dBu into a bridging load; I quote them as printed and say so where it
  matters.
- Where I read something off a schematic I say which drawing and where on it. Where the scan defeated me I
  say that instead of guessing, and section 4.6 is a list of exactly what I could not resolve.

**Why this unit is worth the trouble, in one paragraph.** Every detector in the lab so far is a rectifier
followed by a time constant. The 160 is not. It is a **true-RMS** detector of David Blackmer's own
invention, feeding a **log-domain** gain cell of his own invention, in a **feedforward** topology, with no
attack or release controls at all — because the RMS detector's attack and release are two faces of one time
constant and cannot be separated. That single architectural fact generates the whole published ballistics
table, the whole published low-frequency distortion behaviour, and most of what people mean when they say the
box sounds "musical". Section 5 is where that argument is made, and section 6 is where the knee is turned
from an adjective into a function.

**The headline result, stated up front so the rest of the file can be checked against it.** dbx published a
program-dependent attack table (15 ms for 10 dB, 5 ms for 20 dB, 3 ms for 30 dB), a release rate
(120 dB/s on the 160, 125 dB/s on the 160A), a release table (8 ms for 1 dB, 80 ms for 10 dB, 400 ms for
50 dB) and a low-frequency third-harmonic law ("at 100 Hz, half the value at 50 Hz") [1][2][8]. **All four
fall out of one number**: the log-domain filter time constant of the RMS detector, τ ≈ 34.5 ms. And that
number falls out of two components I can read on dbx's own schematic, R35 at 909 kΩ and C15 at 22 µF, which
the drawing marks as a **factory-matched pair** [4]. Section 5.5 does the arithmetic. If one thing in this
file survives, it should be that.

## 1. What the dbx 160 is, and where it came from

### 1.0 Where the documents are, and how I found them

WebSearch was exhausted before I started, so nothing below was found by searching the web. Everything came
from three places: the Internet Archive's `advancedsearch.php` JSON endpoint queried with `curl`, URLs I
constructed by hand, and links followed out of documents I had already fetched. That worked better here than
it had any right to. The Internet Archive holds a remarkably complete dbx shelf, including two things the
lab's survey did not know existed:

- **the original 160/161 instruction manual**, `dbx_dbx_160`, with a full specification page for the 160 and
  a labelled front-panel drawing [1];
- **a dedicated schematic item**, `dbx_160-161-162_Schematic`, whose page scans are 5100 × 6592 pixels —
  exactly twice the resolution of the same drawings inside the service manual, and enough to read four-digit
  resistor values off a thirty-year-old blueprint [6].

The survey's honest caveat was that the service manual is "a test and alignment manual, not a full schematic
set" [44]. That was true of the OCR text, which is why the survey believed it. It is not true of the
document: **leaves 17 and 18 of that item are the complete 160 and 161 schematics**, title block and all, and
the schematic-only item is the same two drawings at double resolution. So the survey's central worry about
this candidate — that it could be modelled on behaviour but only partly on circuit — turned out to be
unfounded. I read the circuit.

The one document I could not reach is the Mix *TECnology Hall of Fame* entry for the 1976 dbx 160VU, which
Wikipedia cites through a Wayback snapshot [36]. `web.archive.org` returned HTTP 429 to every request I made
across two attempts spaced well apart, so that snapshot is cited in section 13 as **unread**, and the 1976
date it supports is attributed to Wikipedia rather than to Mix.

### 1.1 David Blackmer, and the two inventions this box is made of

**David E. Blackmer** was born 11 January 1927 and died 21 March 2002 [37]. He started in audio at Lafayette
Radio in Boston in the 1940s, studied electronics in the US Navy and then at Harvard and MIT, and worked at
Trans-Radio Recording Studio, Epsco, Hi-Con Eastern and Raytheon, where he designed telemetry for the Mercury
space programme. He founded **dbx, Inc. in 1971**, sold it to BSR in 1979 and stayed on for several years,
then founded Earthworks in the late 1980s. He was a life member of the IEEE and a fellow of the Audio
Engineering Society from 1976, and he had ten children [37][38].

The company name comes from decibel expansion, and its stated goal was "to get closer to the realism of a
live performance" [36]. Its first fame was the dbx noise-reduction system, a decibel companding system that
competed with Dolby in the early 1970s [36].

Two of Blackmer's patents are the whole of this compressor, and both were filed within fifteen months of each
other while he was starting the company:

| Patent | Title | Filed | Granted | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **US 3,681,618** | RMS circuits with bipolar logarithmic converter | 29 Mar 1971 | 1 Aug 1972 | The **RMS detector** [23] |
| **US 3,714,462** | Multiplier circuits | 14 Jun 1971 | 30 Jan 1973 | The **VCA**, the "Blackmer gain cell" [24] |

The 160's own manual lists five patents on the schematic page: "3,681,618; 3,714,462; 3,789,143; 4,101,849;
4,097,767" [1]. The other three are US 3,789,143, a compander whose control signal is logarithmically related
to the instantaneous RMS value of the input [25]; US 4,101,849, an adaptive filter for a compander [27]; and
US 4,097,767, an **operational rectifier** [26]. That last one matters more than it looks, and section 6.2
explains why: the operational rectifier is the block that makes a threshold, and moving one diode inside or
outside its feedback loop is the difference between a hard knee and OverEasy.

Blackmer's abstract for the RMS patent is worth quoting because it states the design goal in his own words,
and the goal is *exactly* the thing an emulation has to reproduce:

> "another principal object of the present invention is to provide a circuit which provides rapid response to
> transient signals and a slower, rate-limited response to falling signal levels in an output which is
> logarithmically related to the rms value of the input. This results in a measurement or control function
> which is **more nearly like the human ear** in response to complex waveforms than peak or average
> detectors." [23]

"Rapid response to transient signals and a slower, rate-limited response to falling signal levels" is a
one-sentence specification of the ballistics, and "rate-limited" is not a figure of speech. Section 5.5 shows
that the falling response really is rate-limited, in the strict sense of a fixed number of decibels per
second, and that dbx's published release figures are that one rate restated three times.

The VCA patent's stated goal is equally specific: "an analog multiplier which has excellent gain control over
at least a ±50 decibel range with very low distortion and noise and a **constant decibels per volt control
characteristic**" [24]. Constant decibels per volt is the property the whole control law in section 6 rests
on.

### 1.2 The 160 itself: dates, and what the sources actually say

Wikipedia says "in 1976 dbx introduced the dbx 160 compressor" and cites the Mix TECnology Hall of Fame entry
for the **1976 dbx 160VU** [36]. I could not read that entry (1.0), so I record 1976 as Wikipedia's date
resting on a source I did not verify. dbx's own current copy says only "since its introduction in the mid
1970s" [13], which is consistent and no more precise. The original manual carries no date at all. The service
manual's 160/161 initial procedure is stamped **"Revised 4-20-77"** [3], which puts a factory alignment
procedure for a shipping product in April 1977 and is the earliest hard date I have for the 160 in
production.

The company address on the original manual is **dbx, Inc., 71 Chapel Street, Newton, MA 02195**, with the
repair department at 224 Calvary Street, Waltham [1]. By the time of the 160A manual it is **dbx Professional
Products, 8760 South Sandy Parkway, Sandy, Utah**, "A Harman International Company", copyright 1995 [2]. The
160 schematic's title block reads "**dbx, a division of AKG Acoustics**" [4], and the 160X manual is stamped
"Used with the permission of AKG ACOUSTICS, INC." [9]. Three owners appear on the documents of one product
line, and the panel legend barely changed.

dbx's own account of the family order, from the current product page, is worth having in their words because
it is the only place any of them is ranked:

> "Since its introduction in the mid 1970's, the dbx 160 series has been the world's most popular line of
> audio compressors. After the classic 160 (which is still used daily in many world class studios), came the
> 165, the 160X and the 160XT. **The 160X/XT were the models that truly became the industry standard.**" [13]

That is a manufacturer marketing its current product, so treat the ranking as a claim rather than a fact. But
it is a useful corrective to the plug-in market, which models the original almost exclusively.

### 1.3 What dbx claimed for it, in 1977 and in 1997

The introduction to the original manual is the clearest statement of the design thesis anywhere, and it names
all three of the things that make the box unusual:

> "Unique circuit designs, such as **true rms level-detection** and **feed-forward gain reduction**, place the
> 160 and 161 in a completely different class from conventional compressor/limiters." [1]

and it explains *why* feedforward, in an argument that is technically correct and that I have not seen a
competitor make:

> "Traditional gain reduction is accomplished by sensing signal level at the device's output and applying a
> correction signal, via a feedback loop. At progressively higher compression ratios, the feedback loop's gain
> increases, distortion increases, and eventually instability or oscillation occurs. To avoid this problem,
> many conventional units restrict the maximum amount of feedback, thus restricting the maximum compression
> ratio to some lower ratio, such as 10:1 or 20:1. The dbx 160 and 161 are free of the instability of
> excessive loop gain, and can provide **infinite compression (approximately 120:1)**." [1]

Two things to take from that. First, it is the correct explanation for why the 1176 tops out at 20:1 and the
160 does not — and the lab already models that 1176 as a feedback loop whose ratio emerges from loop gain
([[1176]] 3.7), so the contrast is live rather than academic. Second, **dbx published the number**: the
"infinity" setting is approximately **120:1**, not infinity. Section 6.5 takes that seriously.

And on the detector:

> "Most compressor/limiters use some form of peak detection, with fast response characteristics that can have
> a disturbing effect on program material. True rms level detection closely simulates the response of the
> human ear. Even at high compression ratios, the gain changing action of the 160 Series is highly listenable
> and natural sounding." [1]

The third claim is the one an emulation is most likely to get wrong, because it is about the *absence* of
controls:

> "In conventional compressor/limiters, the attack and release times depend on feedback loop gain, which means
> they constantly must be readjusted for optimum results at different compression ratios. Since the attack and
> release times of dbx units **vary automatically with the rate of level change in the program material** (the
> envelope shape), operation is simplified; no manual attack/release adjustments are required." [1]

Twenty years later the 160A manual is saying the same things in the same order — "True RMS Level Detection —
senses the power in the program in a musical manner, much as the ear does, giving results superior to peak or
average detection" [2] — which is unusual consistency for marketing copy across two changes of ownership, and
suggests the engineering claim was believed internally rather than written by a copywriter.

### 1.4 Why it is famous

Three reasons, and only one of them is the sound.

**It was early, and it was different.** Universal Audio call it "the first solid-state VCA compressor ever
made" [40]. I have not verified that priority claim and I would not repeat it as fact; what is verifiable is
that Blackmer's VCA patent was granted in January 1973 and that the dbx 202 "black can" VCA built from it
"exceeded by far the performance of other VCAs of the time" and "were used in most early automated mixing
boards" [36]. The gain cell that made automated console faders possible is the same gain cell in this box.

**It is trivially easy to use.** Three knobs and no time constants. dbx made that a selling point rather than
an omission (1.3), and it is the reason the 160 turns up on drum kits in rooms where nobody has time to dial
in an attack.

**It does something no other classic does.** Past the infinity mark the ratio goes *negative*: louder in,
quieter out. dbx trademarked it as **Infinity+** and shipped it on the 160X, 160XT and 160A [8][9][10][2].
The original 160 does not have it (2.2), which is a distinction almost every secondary source gets wrong.

## 2. The variants, and what genuinely differs

### 2.1 The family in one table

Everything in this table is from a dbx document; the "sources" column says which.

| Model | What it is | Knee | Ratio range | Metering | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **160** | The original. Balanced transformerless input on a Jones barrier strip, ground-loop-compensated single-ended output, FET turn-on/turn-off transient protection, wood cabinet. | **Hard only** | 1:1 to ∞ (≈120:1) | Illuminated VU, switchable input / output / gain change | [1][4] |
| **161** | The 160 with the professional parts removed: unbalanced RCA in and out, no ground-loop compensation, no transient protection, lower output ceiling. | Hard only | same | same | [1][5] |
| **162** | The two-channel version, with matched components and close-tolerance parts specifically so that a stereo pair *does* track. | Hard only | same | two VUs | [1][3] |
| **160 VU** | The name Mix's Hall of Fame uses for the original, distinguishing it from the LED-metered successors. Not a separate model number in any dbx document I read. | — | — | — | [36], and see 2.4 |
| **165** | "The top of the line", strappable, and the one that adds **manual attack and release** as an alternative to automatic. | OverEasy | 1:1 to ∞:1 | 30 dB analogue RMS meter | [8][16][17] |
| **160X** | The rack-mount redesign. Adds **OverEasy**, **Infinity+**, the 19+12 LED display, stereo strapping, a detector input on the barrier strip and a bypass switch. | **Switchable** | 1:1 to ∞:1 to **−1:1** | 19-LED level + 12-LED GR | [8][9][11] |
| **160XT** | The 160X with TRS jacks and the panel that became the 160A's. | Switchable | same | same | [10][12][21] |
| **160A** | The current-generation version: XLR and TRS, floating balanced output, detector input on its own jack, stereo strapping. Discontinued as of dbx's own page. | Switchable | same | same | [2][13] |

### 2.2 The single most important difference: the original 160 has no soft knee

Almost every secondary source, including Wikipedia, says the 160 introduced OverEasy [36]. It did not, and
three dbx documents say so.

- The 160A manual lists as a feature: "OverEasy®/Hard Knee Selection — allows selection between our famous
  OverEasy compression curve and **the classic 'Hard Knee' curve popularized by the original dbx 160, 161 and
  162**" [2]. The same sentence appears verbatim in the 160XT manual [10].
- The 160X manual, describing hard-knee mode: "**the 160X will perform in the manner of its predecessor, the
  160**" [9].
- dbx's own OverEasy patent, US 4,182,993, uses the 160 as the *prior art it is improving on*, and prints its
  transfer curve as Figure 1 with a corner in it: "the input-output characteristic curve shown, is typical of
  the Model 160 Compressor Limiter manufactured by DBX, Inc. of Newton, Mass. ... **Once the compression ratio
  is determined, it remains fixed throughout the compression region. This latter feature, however, does not
  provide optimum performance**" [28].

That patent was filed on **2 November 1978** by **Leslie B. Tyler**, assigned to dbx Inc., and granted 8
January 1980 [28]. So OverEasy is a 1978 invention by a different engineer, arriving on the 163 and the 160X
around 1980–81, three or four years after the 160 shipped. A model that gives the original 160 a soft knee is
modelling the wrong box, and a model that gives the 160A only a hard knee is modelling the wrong box in the
other direction.

The second-most-important difference follows the same line: **the original 160 has no negative ratios**. Its
specification says "continuously variable from 1:1 to 120:1 (infinity)" and its panel legend stops at ∞ [1].
Infinity+ is listed as a 160X feature in the 1981 brochure — "Infinity+™ compression provides negative gain
control for 'dynamic reversal' effects" — and appears on every later model [8][9][10][2][13].

### 2.3 What is the same across all of them

This is the more useful half of the comparison, because it is what a shared engine can cover.

- **The gain element.** A Blackmer VCA throughout. The 160's schematic calls it out as a plug-in module,
  "**VCA (200)**", reference designator M1 [4]. The 1981 brochure says of the 164 that "Blackmer VCAs assure
  precise stereo tracking over the entire Over Easy curve" [8], so the name is dbx's own.
- **The detector.** A true-RMS module throughout. The 160's schematic calls it "**RMS (208)**", reference
  designator M2 [4]. The 160X and 164 copy talk about "matched RMS detectors" for strapping [8][9].
- **The topology.** Feedforward throughout: the detector listens to the *input*, never to the output. The
  160A manual makes this explicit — "the 160A actually responds to the DETECTOR INPUT which is wired
  internally in parallel to the INPUT" [2].
- **The ballistics.** The published attack table (15 ms / 5 ms / 3 ms for 10 / 20 / 30 dB) is **identical**
  across the 160, the 160X, the 160XT, the 160A and even the 163 [1][8][9][10][2]. That is a strong hint that
  the detector's time constant was never changed, and section 5.5 uses it as one.
- **The output gain range.** ±20 dB on every model [1][8][9][2].

### 2.4 The "VU" and "X" names, said plainly

There is no dbx document in front of me that prints the model number "160VU". The original manual's cover
reads "**dbx Model 160/161 compressors/limiters**" and the panel silkscreen reads "**dbx**" and
"**COMPRESSOR/LIMITER**" with no suffix [1]. "160 VU" appears to be a retronym — the name the market gave the
original once the LED-metered 160X arrived, and the name Mix's Hall of Fame used in 2010 [36]. Universal
Audio's product image for their plug-in is filed as `uad-vcavu.jpg` [40], which suggests the same usage
inside a modern vendor. I use "the 160" for the original throughout, and say "the one with the VU meter"
where the distinction matters.

"X" is not a retronym: 160X is a dbx model number printed on the panel [8]. "XT" likewise [10].

### 2.5 Which one to model, and why

**The original 160**, with the 160A's OverEasy and Infinity+ available as switches.

The case for the original is that it is the one the plug-in market means by "dbx 160", it is the one with a
readable schematic, it is the one with the best-documented specification page, and its VU meter and wood
cheeks make a far better faceplate for a spoof than a black rack panel does. The case for adding the 160A's
two features is that they cost almost nothing on top of the architecture the original forces on us — OverEasy
is one function swap in the rectifier (6.2) and Infinity+ is letting one coefficient exceed 1.0 (6.4) — and
that leaving them out would throw away the two most distinctive behaviours in the family.

So the model is the 160 by default, with `dbx_knee` and a ratio control whose range runs past infinity. That
is a deliberate composite, it is not any single piece of hardware, and section 10.1 says so on the tin.

## 3. The controls, and enough of the front panel to draw a faceplate

Two panels are described here, because the plug-in's face is the original 160's and the two later features

- **Image A**, `ref/dbx-160-frontpanel-fig.jpg` and its enlargement `ref/dbx-160-panel-zoom.png`: dbx's own
  Figure 1, "Front Panel", from the 160/161 instruction manual, leaf 3 [1]. A line drawing, not a photograph,
  so it is authoritative for legend and layout and only indicative for proportion.
- **Image B**, `ref/dbx-160a-front-big.jpg` and its halves `ref/dbx-160a-panel-left.png` and
  `ref/dbx-160a-panel-right.png`: dbx's own 160A product photograph at 1200 × 118, from `adn.harmanpro.com`
  [14]. A real photograph, so it is authoritative for colour, and it is the only colour reference I have.

I also saved `ref/dbx-brochure-1981-p1.jpg`, the 1981 brochure page carrying a **160X** product photograph
and the full 160X specification table [8], and `ref/dbx-160x-p7.jpg`, the 160X manual page whose section 4.6

**A note the framework insists on.** None of this belongs in the framework. The framework is headless and
uncoloured; every colour, legend and proportion below lives in the example plug-in's own web layer. This

### 3.1 The original 160 panel, from dbx's own drawing

Read left to right off Image A. The chassis is a **wood-cheeked cabinet**: a plain panel with a visibly
grained wooden end-block at each side, drawn with wood-grain hatching, and the manual confirms "handsome
cabinets" with "self-adhesive rubber feet ... for protection of delicate finishes that might otherwise be
marred by the compressor/limiter's **wood cabinet**" [1]. Two units side by side fit a 19-inch rack in
2½ inches of panel space with the RM-150-C kit; a single unit uses the RM-150-D [1].

Along the top of the panel, above the knobs:

| Position | Legend | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| far left | **THRESHOLD**, with **BELOW ○** and **○ ABOVE** beneath it | Two LEDs flanking the word, one either side |
| left of centre | **COMPRESSION** | Over the middle knob |
| right of centre | **OUTPUT GAIN**, on two lines | Over the right knob |

The three knobs sit in a row below those legends, and the VU meter occupies the right-hand third.

**THRESHOLD** knob, scale marks arranged anticlockwise-to-clockwise: **10mv, 30mv, 100mv, 300mv, 1V, 3V**.
Six marks, all lower-case "mv" and upper-case "V" exactly as drawn. Each step is a factor of about 3.16, so
the scale is **log-linear at 10 dB per mark**, five intervals covering 49.5 dB. The manual's specification
agrees: "continuously variable from 10mV(−38dB) to 3V(+12dB)" [1]. The factory procedure confirms the
marks are calibration points rather than decoration: "Step the oscillator up and down in 10 db steps
verifying that the threshold level matches the input signal at successive calibration marks on the threshold
dial" [3].

**COMPRESSION** knob, nine marks, clockwise from the bottom-left: **1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 10, 20, ∞**. The "∞"
is the last mark and there is nothing past it. Note what is *not* printed: no "1" after a colon anywhere. dbx
wrote bare numbers on the original and switched to "3:1", "4:1" style on the 160A (3.2).

**OUTPUT GAIN** knob, five marks: **−20, −10, 0, +10, +20**, with 0 at twelve o'clock. The manual's advice is
"for normal operation, OUTPUT GAIN be set at 12 o'clock (0dB) position" [1].

Along the bottom of the panel, left to right:

| Legend | What it is |
|---|---|
| **POWER** | A rectangular rocker or push switch at the far left, under the threshold knob |
| **INPUT  OUTPUT  GAIN CHANGE**, bracketed underneath by **METER** | Three rectangular push buttons in a row, with a drawn bracket joining them to the word METER |
| **dbx** | The logo, large, lower-case, bold, to the left of the meter |
| **COMPRESSOR/LIMITER** | Under the logo, smaller, upper case |

The **VU meter** is a rectangular illuminated movement in a rectangular bezel occupying roughly the
right-hand third of the panel. It is "factory set for 0VU=+4dB" and recalibratable from the rear [1]. A
zero-set screw sits beneath the meter face.

**Proportions, and how much to trust them.** Measured off Image A as fractions of the panel width between the
inside faces of the wood cheeks (**estimate**, from a line drawing, ±3% at best):

| Feature | Centre, as a fraction of panel width | Diameter or width |
|---|---|---|
| THRESHOLD knob | 0.115 | 0.075 |
| COMPRESSION knob | 0.315 | 0.085 |
| OUTPUT GAIN knob | 0.510 | 0.075 |
| Meter bezel | 0.815 (centre), spanning 0.66 to 0.97 | — |
| POWER switch | 0.115 | 0.055 wide |
| Meter buttons | 0.300 to 0.485 | three buttons |
| BELOW / ABOVE LEDs | 0.105 and 0.170 | small |

Panel height is 1 rack unit's worth of usable face; the manual gives no dimensioned drawing, and the original
160's outside dimensions are **not published in its manual** — the specification page stops at power
requirements [1]. So panel aspect ratio is a guess and I have not made one.

**Colours on the original.** I have no colour photograph of a 160, and the manual drawing is monochrome, so
I will not state a panel colour. What the drawing does establish, and the text confirms, is the LED
behaviour, and here the original differs from every later model:

> "When the **amber** indicator is illuminated, the input level to the compressor/limiter is below the
> threshold level ... When the **red** indicator is illuminated, the input level to the compressor/limiter is
> above the threshold level. The L.E.D.'s do not measure output level." [1]

So on the 160 it is **amber for below, red for above**. On the 160X the specification prints
"BELOW/threshold/ABOVE (**green, yellow, red**)" [8][9], and the 160A photograph shows the same green /
yellow / red [2][14]. The original had two LEDs; the OverEasy models have three, the middle one lighting only
in the OverEasy region. That middle LED is the cheapest possible way for a plug-in to show a knee, and I
would keep it.

One more behaviour worth reproducing because it is charming and specified: "A steady-state, sine-wave tone
**exactly at the threshold voltage** causes both L.E.D.'s to remain **dimly illuminated**" [1]. The factory
procedure uses it as the threshold calibration point: "Turn front panel 'Threshold Control' until both led's
are off ... (When both leds are off the input signal is at threshold level.)" [3].

### 3.2 The 160A panel, from dbx's own photograph

Read off Image B, which is the panel dbx still shows on its product page. Black brushed-aluminium face,
white silkscreen, and **thin blue vertical rules dividing the panel into five sections**. Hex-socket screws
at the corners, rack ears with rounded-rectangle slots.

Section by section, left to right:

1. **Identity.** "**dbx**" in white, large, bold, lower case, above "**160A**" in white, letterspaced, in a
   lighter weight.
2. **Buttons.** Two small round black push buttons: **BYPASS** with a **red** LED above it, and **SLAVE**
   with a **yellow** LED above it.
3. **Threshold.** A **red** knob with a white pointer line. Scale marks, clockwise from lower left:
   **−40dBu, −30, −20, −10, 0, +10, +20dBu** — seven marks at 10 dB spacing, matching the published
   "−40dBu to +20dBu" range [2]. The word **THRESHOLD** is printed below, with a leader line running right to
   the OverEasy group. To the right of the knob: three LEDs labelled **BELOW** (green), **OVEREASY** (yellow)
   and **ABOVE** (red), with a small round black **OverEasy** button below the yellow one.
4. **Ratio.** A **blue** knob with a white pointer. Scale marks, clockwise from lower left:

### 3.5 The detector input, and why it earns a place in the model

The detector input is more interesting on this box than on most, because the manual's list of uses is a list
of things the model should be able to do:

frequency-weighted compression with an equaliser ahead of the detector; de-essing with a parametric boosted
at 4–6 kHz; sustain by boosting the instrument's dominant range; pre-emphasis for broadcast; multi-way
speaker protection weighted to the fragile driver; and — the one I like best — **anticipated compression**,
where you feed the programme straight to the detector and delay the audio, so that "the effect can be that
of zero attack time at a given frequency ... Additional signal delays beyond this zero time will then cause
the compressor to finish reducing the gain **before the leading edge of the loud passage even enters the
signal input**" [2].

That last one is free in a plug-in and impossible in most hardware, and the manual draws it as Figure 5. A
`dbx_lookahead` control is therefore not a modern liberty; it is a feature dbx documented in 1995.

make-up" is bit-for-bit unity. That is worth reproducing exactly rather than approximately.

The **four KE4858 JFETs** are the "FET circuits [that] prevent power turn-on or turn-off transients from
reaching the output" in the specification [1]. They are a mute, not a bypass — the 160 has no bypass switch
at all. The 161 drawing omits them, which matches the manual's statement that the 161 "lacks turn-on/turn-off
transient protection, and ground-loop compensation" [1][5].

T1 (drawing 110083) from a 117 V AC primary, F1 3AG ¼ A, S1 POWER, C1 0.005 µF 3 kV across the mains. Raw

The rail tolerance matters, because the rail *is* the reference for both panel controls (4.3) and, I will

the negative one drift four times as far. That asymmetry is a clue that the positive rail is the one
something depends on.

### 4.5 The trims, and what each one tells us about the topology

The factory procedures name the trims, and a trim exists only where a real parameter has to be forced into
tolerance. This is the cheapest structural information in the whole file. From the 160/161 procedure [3][7]:

| Trim | Procedure | What it therefore is |
|---|---|---|
| R14 (selected) | set +15.000 V ±50 mV | supply reference |
| RMS trim, "the two RMS trim pins" | null the offset at the RMS module's input pin to 0 ±15 mV | detector input offset |
| R36 | with 1.228 V RMS at 100 Hz dropped to −60 dB, adjust for "a symmetrical waveform on comp pin" | **the RMS module's rectifier symmetry**, watched on the timing capacitor |
| R27 and R34 | at 1.228 V RMS, 100 Hz, compression "3", output +20, trim both for minimum distortion | **VCA symmetry and RMS symmetry**, trimmed against distortion |
| R63 | set the voltage at the far end of R79 to −0.011 V DC | level calibration |
| R33 (161) / threshold pot | set so both LEDs are just off, then check 10 dB dial steps | threshold calibration |

Three things follow. First, 100 Hz is dbx's chosen distortion-measurement frequency in production, not 1 kHz;
section 7.4 explains why that is the honest choice for this circuit. Second, **1.228 V RMS is exactly
+4 dBu** (0.775 × 10^0.2 = 1.2277 V), so the factory reference level is +4 dBu, matching the meter's factory
calibration [1][3]. Third, both symmetry trims are adjusted *against distortion*, which tells us the residual
distortion of both the cell and the rectifier is a symmetry error — an even-order term whose magnitude is set
by how well two junctions match. That is the right way to model it (10.4).

The 163's procedure, printed in the same service manual, names its trims more explicitly still — "Set R25
(RMS Sym), R61 (VCA Sym), and R68 (Log) to midrange", then "Alternate between R61 (VCA Sym) and R25 (RMS Sym)
as you adjust for minimum distortion. Two passes is usually enough", then "Reduce oscillator output by about
20 dB. Adjust R68 (Log) for minimum distortion", and finally "Adjust R61 (VCA Sym) for distortion **less than
0.056 %**" [3]. Three trims, two of them symmetry and one of them the log conformance, and a numeric
production limit. The 163 is not the 160, but it is the same two modules in the same roles, and 0.056 % is
the only production distortion limit dbx printed anywhere I looked.

## 5. The VCA and the RMS detector

This is the section the dossier exists for. Everything the 160 does that no other model in the lab does comes
from these two modules and from the fact that they are the *same* circuit trick used twice, in opposite
directions.

### 5.1 Which parts, exactly

The 160's schematic names them, in a legend headed "PHYSICAL CONFIGURATIONS, BOTTOM VIEW" [4]:

| On the drawing | Designator | Pins shown | What it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **VCA (200)** | M1 | IN, GND, P0, N1, N0, M, +V, −V, OUT | The **dbx model 200 VCA**, a Blackmer gain cell |
| **RMS (208)** | M2 | IN, OUT, TRIM, SYM, OFFSET, COMP CAP, GND, +V, −V | The **dbx model 208 RMS detector** |

Both are potted modules with their own trim pins, and dbx supplied a matched transistor with the VCA — the
drawing marks Q7, a 2N2263, "**SUPPLIED WITH VCA**" [4], sitting on the module's GND pin with a 22 µF
bypass. Wikipedia's account of the family, describing the 202, says these were "built using discrete
transistors that were **hand matched while running at an elevated temperature in an oven**" [36]. That is
what the supplied transistor is for: it tracks the cell's temperature so the control law does not drift.

I have not opened a dbx 200 or a dbx 208, and no datasheet for either is reachable. What I have instead is
better than a datasheet in one way and worse in another: **Blackmer's patents describe the circuits exactly**
[23][24], and **THAT Corporation still manufactures their direct descendants and publishes full datasheets
for them** [30][31]. THAT's own copy is explicit about the lineage: the 2252 RMS detector is "**Based on dbx
technology**" and "Matches 2180 and 2181 Series VCAs" [31], and the 2180 is "Pin-Compatible with
2150-Series" [30], the 2150 being the successor to the dbx 202. So every number I take from a THAT datasheet
is a number for the modern part, cited as such, used as the best available proxy for the module dbx potted in
1976. Where that proxy is load-bearing I say so.

### 5.2 The Blackmer gain cell, in Blackmer's own terms

From US 3,714,462, the structure is this [24]:

- An input operational amplifier with **two feedback paths, through the collector–emitter circuits of
  opposite-conductivity-type transistors**. Because a junction's voltage is the log of its current, this pair
  converts the input current to a log voltage — and because there are two of opposite polarity, it does so
  **bilaterally**, for both halves of the waveform, without rectifying.
- A **second pair** of transistors, each connected to derive the antilogarithm of the output of a
  corresponding transistor of the first pair.
- A **control voltage summed with the log signal by applying it to the bases** of the log and antilog
  transistors, "thereby controlling the gain between the two operational amplifiers".
- A second op amp as an output buffer, recombining the two halves.

Two consequences follow directly, and both matter for the model.

**The gain is exponential in the control voltage, and exactly so.** Adding a voltage in the log domain
multiplies in the linear domain. Blackmer's stated objective was "a **constant decibels per volt** control
characteristic" over "at least a ±50 decibel range" [24], and THAT's modern part delivers **6.1 mV/dB
(6.0 to 6.2)** measured over a −60 dB to +40 dB gain range, on either control port, with the negative port at
−6.1 mV/dB [30]. In the model this means gain reduction is computed and applied in **decibels**, not in
linear gain, and there is no approximation involved in doing so. Every VCA compressor in the lab already
works this way ([[Distressor]] 3.2); the difference here is that for the 160 it is a documented property of a
named part rather than a convenient choice.

**The cell's residual distortion is a matching error, and it is even-order.** The two halves of the signal go
through different transistors. If those two paths are not identical the two halves are not amplified
identically, and an asymmetric transfer curve is an even-order one. That is why the part has a **symmetry
trim pin** — pin 4 on the THAT 2180 [30], the P0 port fed by R27 (50 kΩ) through R28 (300 kΩ) on the 160 [4]
— and why dbx's factory procedure adjusts it "for minimum distortion" [3]. The 163's procedure puts a number
on the result: after trimming, "distortion **less than 0.056 %**" at +2 dBV, 100 Hz [3]. THAT's modern part
claims "< 0.01 % (2180A)" [30].

The tempco is the last property worth carrying: **+0.33 %/°C** on the control constant, referenced to a chip
temperature of 27 °C [30]. A 10 °C rise makes every decibel of the control law 3.3 % bigger, which shifts
both the threshold and the ratio. That is what Q7 is compensating and it is why an emulation with a
temperature knob would not be absurd — though I am not going to build one.

### 5.3 The Blackmer RMS detector, in Blackmer's own terms

From US 3,681,618, and confirmed in far more detail by THAT's datasheet for the descendant part [23][31]:

1. **Bilateral log conversion.** An op amp with "a pair of diode junction feedback loops of **opposite
   polarities**", exactly as in the VCA. The input is a *current*, and pin 1 is held at virtual ground.
2. **A pair of fixed-gain stages of +2 and −2**, and "a corresponding pair of rectifiers for respectively
   half-wave rectifying" their outputs. In the modern part the mirror is done with transistors rather than
   gain stages, but the result is identical: the current in the summing transistor equals **|I_in|**, the
   absolute value of the input current [31].
3. **Squaring, for free.** "By using **two** diode-connected transistors in the feedback loop of OA2, the
   2252 produces a voltage proportional to **twice the log** of I_C4" — and twice the log of x is the log of
   x², so the log-domain voltage is the log of the instantaneous *square* [31]. No multiplier is used.
4. **The mean, taken in the log domain.** "The outputs of the rectifiers are summed with one another ... a
   **source of constant current and a capacitor** coupled to the source and to the rectifier outputs so that
   the voltage across the capacitor will tend to bring the **long term average** sum of the output currents
   from the rectifiers into equality with the current from the source" [23]. On the 160 that capacitor is
   **C15, 22 µF tantalum**, on the module's **COMP CAP** pin [4].
5. **The square root, also for free.** "The square root portion of the Root-Mean Square is implied by the
   constant of proportionality for the output voltage: **it is not computed explicitly**. This is because, in
   the log representation, taking the square root is equivalent to division by two. The voltage at pin 6 is
   proportional to the mean of the square at approximately 3 mV/dB, and proportional to the **square root of
   the mean of the square at approximately 6 mV/dB**" [31].

So the whole of *root mean square* is done with junctions and one capacitor. There is no squarer, no
averager and no square-rooter. That is why the thing has a dynamic range of eighty-odd decibels and costs
almost nothing, and it is why Blackmer could open his patent by dismissing thermocouples, hot wires, square-
law curves, FETs, segmental diode approximations, Esaki diodes and analogue multipliers in one paragraph as
"limited to a dynamic range between [40] and 60 decibels" with "very limited crest factor tolerance" [23].

**The output scale factor is 6.1 mV/dB (6.0 to 6.2)**, the same number as the VCA, measured over
31.6 nA < I_in < 1 mA [31]. dbx built the box out of two parts with the same log constant, so a volt in the
sidechain means the same number of decibels everywhere in it. That is the single most useful fact for writing
the model, and section 6 leans on it throughout.

### 5.4 The scaling constant of the whole box: 124.5 to 1

The panel controls swing the full ±15 V rails (4.3). The VCA needs ±120 mV or so. So there must be an
attenuator of about 125:1 between them, and the schematic contains that ratio twice:

| Where | Resistors | Ratio |
|---|---|---|
| VCA control port | **R47 24.9 kΩ 1 %** and **R48 200 Ω 1 %** | 124.5 : 1 |
| Meter path | **R49 1.24 MΩ 1 %** and **R50 10 kΩ 1 %** | 124.0 : 1 |

**Derived.** Taking R47 as the series element and R48 as the shunt, a ±15 V wiper voltage becomes

> 15 V × 200 / (200 + 24 900) = **119.5 mV**

and at the Blackmer constant that is

> 119.5 mV / 6.1 mV/dB = **19.6 dB**, or 19.9 dB if the cell is at the 6.0 mV/dB end of its tolerance.

dbx publish the output gain range as **±20 dB** on every model in the family [1][8][9][2], and mark the ends
of R80's track "−20db" and "+20db" on the drawing itself [4]. So the derivation lands within 2 % of the
published figure, and it does so using only values printed on the schematic and a constant from the
descendant part's datasheet.

**The honest caveat**, promised in 4.6: I could not resolve from the scan which of R47 and R48 is the series
element. The other reading — R48 in series, R47 shunting — gives a divider of 0.992 and a gain range of
±2470 dB, which is absurd, and a reading where R30 parallels R48 gives ±9.8 dB, which contradicts the
printed panel legend. Only one reading survives contact with dbx's own number, so that is the one I adopt,
and I record that I adopted it by elimination rather than by reading it.

The same arithmetic run backwards gives the **threshold range**. R69 (5.49 kΩ 1 %) brings the detector
voltage to the summing node and R71 (523 kΩ 1 %) brings the threshold wiper there [4], so a ±15 V wiper
swing balances a detector swing of

> 15 V × 5.49 / 523 = **157.5 mV**, which is 157.5 / 6.1 = **±25.8 dB**, a span of **51.6 dB**  (**derived**)

against a published threshold range of **10 mV to 3 V, a span of 49.5 dB** [1]. Within 4 %, using two printed
1 % resistors and one datasheet constant. R72 (909 kΩ 1 %) also enters that node and will shift the centre of
the range; I did not resolve what it connects to, so the *span* is derived and the *centre* is not.

### 5.5 The one time constant that generates the entire published ballistics table

Here is the argument the whole file rests on.

**The mechanism.** The averaging capacitor is charged through a transistor whose emitter current is the
antilog of the difference between the log-domain signal and the capacitor voltage, and discharged by a
constant current source. THAT state the resulting time constant in closed form [31]:

> **τ = C_T · V_T / I_T = 0.0259 · C_T / I_T  seconds, at 300 K**

This is a **log-domain filter**: one pole whose effective resistance is the incremental resistance of a
junction, 1/g_m = V_T/I_T. Two behaviours follow that no linear RC has.

- **Falling signals are rate-limited, not exponential.** When the input drops, the charging transistor turns
  off and the capacitor is discharged by I_T alone. The voltage falls **linearly in volts**, and because
  volts are decibels here, it falls **linearly in decibels**: a constant dB/s. THAT put it plainly: "the
  straight-line decay follows from the fact that the natural release of the exponential time weighting is a
  decaying exponential in the linear world. This maps to a **straight line in the log representation**" [31].
- **Rising signals attack faster the bigger the step.** "for sudden increases in the input current I_in, the
  current available to charge the capacitor C_T is proportional to the **square of the short-term increase**
  in input current" [31]. A bigger step opens the charging transistor harder, so the same capacitor charges
  in less time.

And there is no way to separate the two: "How fast the 2252 acquires a signal (the 'attack'), and how fast it
returns to rest following a signal (the 'release'), are **locked in relationship to each other** by the nature
of the exponential time-weighting imposed by this log-domain filter. **Separate attack and release
adjustments are not possible within the constraint of rms response**" [31].

That sentence is why the dbx 160 has no attack knob and no release knob. It is not a simplification for the
user. It is a property of true-RMS detection.

**The equation.** Writing the log-domain error as e (volts, positive when the signal exceeds the stored
level), the capacitor obeys

> τ · dv/dt = V_T · ( exp(e / V_T) − 1 )

For a step of Δ decibels, e starts at K·Δ where K = 6.1 mV/dB. Solving and asking for the time to reach
**63 % of the level change** — which is precisely how dbx define attack time, "time required to reduce signal
by 63 % of level increase (above threshold)" [8][1] — gives a closed form (**derived**):

> t(Δ) / τ = ln [ ( 1 − e^(−u) ) / ( 1 − e^(−0.37 u) ) ],   with u = K·Δ / V_T

**The components.** On the 160's drawing, R35 is **909 kΩ 1 %** and C15 is **22 µF tantalum**, and the note
on the drawing reads "**△ R35 AND C15 ARE MATCHED PER DWG #164001**" [4]. dbx matched a resistor to a
capacitor at the factory. The only quantity a resistor–capacitor *pair* can set here is τ, and taking R35 as
the current-source resistor from the +15 V rail:

> I_T = 15 V / 909 kΩ = **16.50 µA**
> τ = 22 µF × 0.0259 V / 16.50 µA = **34.5 ms**   (**derived**)

**The test.** Feed that one number back into everything dbx published:

| Published quantity | dbx's figure | Model at τ = 34.5 ms | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Release rate, 160 | **120 dB/s** | **123.0 dB/s** | [1] |
| Release rate, 160A | **125 dB/s** | 123.0 dB/s | [2] |
| Release, 1 dB | 8 ms | 8.1 ms | [2] |
| Release, 10 dB | 80 ms | 81 ms | [2] |
| Release, 50 dB | 400 ms | 407 ms | [2] |
| Attack, 10 dB step | **15 ms** | **15.3 ms** | [1][8][9][2] |
| Attack, 20 dB step | 5 ms | 6.3 ms | [1][8][9][2] |
| Attack, 30 dB step | 3 ms | 2.6 ms | [1][8][9][2] |

Seven published numbers, one free parameter, and that parameter is not free either — it is fixed by two
components printed on the schematic. The worst error is the 20 dB attack point at 27 %; every other row is
inside 10 %, and dbx's own figures are prefixed "typically" and given to one significant figure.

**Two further observations that fall out of the table.**

First, **the 160A's release specification is one number written three times**. 1 dB ÷ 125 dB/s = 8 ms;
10 dB ÷ 125 = 80 ms; 50 dB ÷ 125 = 400 ms. All three of dbx's release figures are exactly the quoted rate,
to the digit [2]. That is not a criticism — it is confirmation that the release really is a constant dB/s
slew and that dbx knew it — but a model that implements three separate release times has misread the
specification.

Second, **each published figure implies its own τ, and they agree**:

| Published figure | Implied τ | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Attack, 10 dB = 15 ms | 33.9 ms | [1] |
| Attack, 20 dB = 5 ms | 27.3 ms | [1] |
| Attack, 30 dB = 3 ms | 39.8 ms | [1] |
| Release 120 dB/s | 35.4 ms | [1] |
| Release 125 dB/s | 34.0 ms | [2] |
| R35 × C15 on the drawing | **34.5 ms** | [4] |

Five independent published quantities and one pair of components, spread over 27 to 40 ms, clustering at 34.
I take **τ = 34.5 ms** as the model's constant and label it **derived**, not measured.

**Sensitivity, stated so nobody over-trusts it.** The 15 V in the I_T calculation is the rail, and the real
drive is the rail plus or minus whatever sits across the source. THAT's own design procedure for the same
block computes its timing resistor as **(|V_EE| + 2·V_d)/I_T**, because the capacitor pin sits two diode
drops off the rail [33], which for dbx's parts would mean 16.4 V, τ = 31.6 ms and 134 dB/s. At 14 V it is
37.0 ms and 115 dB/s. So the honest range is **τ = 31 to 37 ms and 115 to 134 dB/s**, against dbx's published
120 to 125. The agreement is real, it brackets both published figures, and it is not to three figures. I take
34.5 ms because it sits in the middle of the published rates and it fits the attack table best.

**And an independent confirmation of the rate itself.** THAT Corporation's design note for a soft-knee
compressor built from the descendants of these two modules gives the identical design equation —
C = I_T / (K · rate) — and then says, in their own words:

> "Then set the detector's release rate. **We have found over the years that a value of approximately
> 120 dB per second provides the best sound without introducing noticeable distortion.** So, defining
> RelRate := 120 dB/second, the rate of change required of the detector's output voltage becomes
> V_C = K_GAIN × RelRate = (6.1 × 10⁻³) × 120 = 732 mV/s. The timing capacitor, C4, becomes
> C4 = I_t / V_c ≈ 10 µF" [33]

That is the successor company, thirty years later, arriving at **exactly the number dbx printed on the 160's
specification page in the 1970s**, and calling it the value that sounds best. It is the strongest external
corroboration in this file that 120 dB/s is not an arbitrary figure but the right one for this architecture.

### 5.6 The ripple, and why dbx's distortion footnote is the same equation

A true-RMS detector run at audio frequencies does not produce a clean DC. It produces DC plus ripple at twice
the input frequency, and THAT give the amplitude [31]:

> V_R ≈ V_T / (4 π f_in τ)   for f_in ≫ 1/(4πτ)

That ripple modulates the VCA's gain, and gain modulation at 2f on a carrier at f produces sidebands at f and
**3f**. So a true-RMS feedforward compressor has a **third-harmonic distortion that is inversely proportional
to frequency**, is proportional to how much of the detector's output reaches the cell (the ratio setting),
and falls as τ rises.

Now read dbx's own footnote to the 160's specification page:

> "3rd-harmonic distortion in the 160 Series **decreases linearly as the frequency rises: at 100 Hz
> 3rd-harmonic distortion is 1/2 the value at 50 Hz**, etc." [1]

and the 1981 brochure's footnote to the 160X's:

> "2nd harmonic is **relatively unaffected** by compression ratio, time constants and frequency, while **3rd
> harmonic decreases with slower time constants, higher frequencies and lower compression ratios**" [8]

And then, twenty years later, dbx name the mechanism outright. Their 1998 white paper on the 160SL opens by
explaining what an automatic mode is for:

> "'Well behaved' means that pumping and breathing are minimized and that **dynamic distortion, caused by low
> frequencies modulating the control line feeding the VCA**, is not audible." [45]

*Low frequencies modulating the control line feeding the VCA.* That is the ripple, named by the
manufacturer, identified as the source of distortion, and identified as a low-frequency phenomenon. I derived
the equation before I found that sentence, and finding it afterwards is the closest thing to a confirmation
this file contains.

Every clause of both footnotes is that one equation. Inversely proportional to frequency: V_R ∝ 1/f.
Decreasing with slower time constants: V_R ∝ 1/τ. Decreasing with lower compression ratios: the ripple
reaching the cell is scaled by the ratio coefficient α (6.1). And the second harmonic being independent of
all three, because the second harmonic is not ripple at all — it is the symmetry error of the cell and the
rectifier (5.2), which no amount of averaging touches.

**Putting a number on it (derived).** At τ = 34.5 ms and 100 Hz, V_R = 0.098 dB rms, so 0.138 dB peak. A
sinusoidal gain modulation of that depth has a modulation index m = 0.0159, and the third harmonic is m/2 =
**0.80 %** at infinite compression. dbx publish "**0.5 % 3rd harmonic typical at infinite compression
ratio**" for the 160, without naming a frequency [1] — and their production distortion trim is performed at
**100 Hz** [3], which is where this mechanism is worst and therefore where a sensible manufacturer measures.
Predicted 0.8 % against published 0.5 %: the mechanism is right, the frequency law is right, and the
magnitude is within a factor of 1.6. I would not claim better.

The corresponding second-harmonic figure is **0.075 % at infinite compression at +4 dBm output** on the 160
[1], and the 160X's table gives 0.07 % second harmonic both below and above threshold, unchanged [8][9] —
consistent with a fixed cell asymmetry rather than anything dynamic. The 161's specification prints
"**0.75 %** 2nd harmonic" in the same slot [1], ten times the 160's, on a page otherwise identical. I believe
that is a typographical error in the 161 column rather than a real difference, because nothing else in the
161's circuit would multiply its cell asymmetry by ten and the 161 uses the same modules [5]. I record it as a
discrepancy rather than resolving it silently.

### 5.7 Crest factor, and what the detector does not hear

The last property of a true-RMS detector that an emulation must carry is that it is **wrong about peaks, on
purpose, by a specified amount**. THAT publish the error against crest factor at a 1 ms pulse repetition rate
[31]:

| Crest factor | Reading error |
|---|---|
| 3.5 | 0.2 dB |
| 5 | 0.5 dB |
| 8 | 1.0 dB |

A sine wave has a crest factor of 1.41; a snare hit can be 8 or more. So the 160 under-reads a spiky source
by up to a decibel relative to its true RMS, and it under-reads it by *vastly* more than that relative to
what a peak detector would say. This is the whole reason the box sounds the way it does on drums: it lets the
transient through and grabs the body. A model that quietly substitutes a peak detector with a fast attack
will get the gain-reduction meter roughly right and the sound completely wrong.

There is a second, subtler level dependence. The detector's bandwidth falls with signal current [31]:

| Input current | Frequency for 1 dB extra error |
|---|---|
| ≥ 100 µA | 80 kHz |
| ≥ 10 µA | 74 kHz |
| ≥ 1 µA | 30 kHz |
| ≥ 100 nA | 4 kHz |

On the 160 the detector's input resistor is **R38, 23.2 kΩ** [4], so +4 dBu (1.228 V) at the detector is
1.228 / 23 200 = **52.9 µA** (**derived**), comfortably inside the widest band, while −50 dBu is about 100 nA
and the detector stops hearing above roughly 4 kHz. In practice that means the 160 loses high-frequency
sensitivity on very quiet material — which is below any threshold anyone would set, so I note it and do not
model it.

### 5.8 What the two modules do **not** explain

Honesty about the boundary. The following are real behaviours of the hardware that neither patent nor
datasheet accounts for, and that I am therefore not going to model as physics:

- **The output stage's own character.** Q8/Q9/Q14 with 22 Ω emitter resistors driving a ground-compensated
  single-ended output into 600 Ω is a class-AB discrete amplifier that will have its own crossover behaviour
  near clipping [4]. dbx publish only "+26 dB (15.5 V)" bridging and "+24 dBm (12.3 V)" into 600 Ω [1], with
  no distortion figure at those levels, so anything I did here would be invented.
- **The ground-loop compensation**, which "senses any ground-loop current (hum)" and "applies a precise
  correction signal ... to reduce hum in the output signal by up to 40 dB" [1]. A real and unusual circuit,
  and completely inaudible in a plug-in.
- **The input transformerless differential stage's common-mode behaviour.** The 160A publishes CMRR > 45 dB
  [2]; the 160 publishes nothing.
- **Unit-to-unit variation.** dbx wrote an entire page of the original manual explaining that they would not
  fit strapping jacks to the 160 because "component-to-component variation is typically 10 %; most
  potentiometers have 20 % tolerances" and two strapped units would drift apart audibly [1]. That is a
  wonderful piece of engineering candour and it tells us the spread is real, but it gives no distribution to
  sample from, so I will not fake one.

## 6. The knee, the ratio law, and the infinity setting

The brief for this dossier was to turn OverEasy from an adjective into a function. This section does that,
and on the way it turns the ratio control into a single coefficient and shows that "infinity" is a number.

### 6.1 The ratio law is a multiplication, and that is the whole thing

The 160 is feedforward, and both its detector and its gain cell are logarithmic with the same constant
(5.3). Write everything in decibels:

- **x** = the detector's reading minus the threshold, in dB. Positive means "above threshold".
- **r(x)** = what the rectifier passes. For a hard knee, r(x) = max(x, 0).
- **α** = the fraction of the rectifier's output that reaches the VCA's control port. This is what the
  COMPRESSION pot sets, and it is dimensionless.
- **GR** = the gain reduction the cell applies, in dB. GR = α · r(x), because both parts have the same
  6.1 mV/dB constant, so a volt is a volt is a decibel all the way through.

Then above threshold, with a hard knee:

> output excess = x − GR = x − αx = x(1 − α)
> **ratio R = x / (x − GR) = 1 / (1 − α)**, equivalently **α = 1 − 1/R**

That is the entire ratio law of the dbx 160, and it is a single multiply. Every ratio the panel prints is one
value of α:

| Panel | 1:1 | 1.5:1 | 2:1 | 3:1 | 4:1 | 6:1 | 10:1 | 20:1 | ∞:1 | −5:1 | −2:1 | −1:1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **α** | 0 | 0.333 | 0.500 | 0.667 | 0.750 | 0.833 | 0.900 | 0.950 | **1.000** | 1.200 | 1.500 | **2.000** |

Three things follow immediately, and each of them is a published behaviour of the hardware that this one line
explains.

**Infinity is not a mode.** It is the point where α reaches exactly 1, and the pot passes through it the way
it passes through 4:1. dbx describe the control as "continuously variable from 1:1 to ∞:1 to −1:1" [8][9][2]
precisely because on this axis it *is* continuous; there is nothing special at α = 1 except that 1/(1−α)
happens to blow up there. Every model that treats ∞:1 as a separate brick-wall branch has misunderstood the
circuit.

**Negative ratios are free.** α > 1 means the cell pulls down more decibels than the input rose, so the
output goes the other way. α = 2 gives exactly −1:1. dbx trademarked this as **Infinity+** and it needed no
new circuit, only a longer pot [8].

**Negative ratios are only possible because the box is feedforward.** In a feedback compressor, α > 1 is
positive feedback and the loop latches. dbx's own argument for feedforward was about stability at high ratios
(1.3), and Infinity+ is the reductio of that argument: a feedback design cannot get there at all. This is the
sharpest architectural contrast in the lab, because [[1176]] and [[Distressor]] are both feedback boxes whose
ratios emerge from loop gain, and neither could ever do this.

**And the ratio law is exact, not approximate.** There is no gain computer, no lookup, no interpolation. One
multiplication in the decibel domain, and the arithmetic of logarithms does the rest. THAT's own compressor
design note describes the same architecture in the same order — RMS detector, then "the threshold amplifier,
otherwise known as an **operational rectifier**", then a scaling stage, then the VCA control port, with
"K_control at the output of the level detector ... −6.1 mV/dB" [32]. And their reference soft-knee design
describes the ratio pot in exactly the terms α implies: "VR3 adjusts the compression ratio. **At one end of
the potentiometer, the compressor acts like an above-threshold limiter, while at the other end, there is no
compression**" [33] — which is α sweeping from 1 to 0, with nothing in between but the pot.

### 6.2 The knee is a diode, and dbx say so in a patent

The block between the threshold comparison and the ratio scaling is a rectifier: it must pass positive excess
and block negative excess, or the compressor would apply *gain* below threshold. How sharply it makes that
transition is the knee, and there are exactly two ways to build it.

**Hard knee: the diode goes inside a feedback loop.** An op amp with diodes in its own feedback path is a
precision rectifier. The diode's exponential softness is divided by the amplifier's open-loop gain, so the
corner is sharpened by five orders of magnitude. dbx hold a patent titled precisely "**Operational
rectifier**", US 4,097,767, and it is one of the five listed on the 160's own schematic page [1][26]. On the
160X's schematic I can see the shape of it: **D107 and D108 in series** at the summing junction of U104A, a
4558, with R150 (30.1 kΩ) and R151 (10 kΩ) around it [11].

**Derived:** for a 4558 at DC, open-loop gain is of order 10⁵, so a diode knee spanning roughly 40 mV
collapses to about 0.4 µV, which at 6.1 mV/dB is under 10⁻⁴ dB. That is why dbx could advertise the 160X as
offering "a **mathematically precise** 'hard' threshold — at any compression ratio selected" [9] and mean it
literally. The original 160's knee is not soft-by-accident. It is sharp by construction.

THAT's own reference design for this architecture is titled "**A simple, effective soft-knee
compressor/limiter**" and puts **two 1N4148s, D1 and D2**, around the threshold amplifier with R5 at 1.91 kΩ
and R4 at 383 kΩ [33]. Two diodes, a threshold amplifier, and a ratio pot after it: the same three blocks in
the same order, from the company that inherited the parts.

**OverEasy: the same diode, outside the loop.** dbx's OverEasy patent, US 4,182,993, filed 2 November 1978
by Leslie B. Tyler and assigned to dbx Inc., describes the gain-changer block as a **diode in series with a
resistor**, driven from the *output* of the difference amplifier and feeding a separate inverting stage
[28]:

> "The output of difference amplifier 32 is connected to the anode of **diode 192** of gain changer means 36,
> the cathode of diode 192 being connected to **resistor** 36 (numbered 194 elsewhere in the patent) of the
> gain changer means. The output of
> gain changer means 36 is connected to the negative input terminal of operational amplifier 196 having ...
> its output terminal connected through feedback resistor 198 to its negative input terminal."

and, in the closing paragraph, states the mechanism outright:

> "**diodes 192 and 234 are utilized for their forward bias voltage current characteristics in shaping the
> input output characteristic curve of FIG. 4**"

The diode is no longer inside a loop, so its own exponential turn-on is exposed, and the exponential *is* the
knee. Tyler states the design goal in exactly the terms the function has to satisfy:

> "an input-output characteristic curve which is a function having a **continuous slope or first derivative**
> and varying from a fixed gain, for input signal levels below the threshold level, to infinite compression
> for the maximum output level ... and a **gradually increasing compression ratio** between these two
> portions of the curve" [28]

"Gradually increasing compression ratio" is the key phrase. OverEasy is not a rounded corner joining two
straight lines. It is a curve whose **slope is a smooth sigmoid**, rising from 0 to α as the level rises
through the threshold region. dbx's own specification table says the same thing in the compression-ratio row:
in OverEasy mode "COMPRESSION RATIO control determines **maximum** compression ratio", while in hard-knee
mode "COMPRESSION RATIO setting defines **exact** compression ratio" [8][9].

### 6.3 OverEasy as a function

Solving the circuit Tyler describes. Let e be the voltage at the diode's anode, I the diode current, R the
series resistor and R_f the feedback resistor of the following stage. With V_θ = n·V_T the diode's
ideality-scaled thermal voltage,

> e = V_θ · ln(1 + I/I_S) + I·R,     GR = I·R_f / K

Substituting u = I·R and z = u/V_θ, and dropping the −1 (valid whenever the diode conducts at all), this
rearranges to z + ln z = e/V_θ − c for a constant c, whose solution is the **Wright omega function**,
ω(t) = W(eᵗ) where W is Lambert W. Writing the difference amplifier's gain as G and the excess as x decibels,
so that e = G·K·x, and defining a **knee width** w = V_θ/(G·K) in decibels:

> **GR(x) = α · w · ω( x/w − c )**   (**derived** from [28])

This is the same special function the [[Neve-33609]] dossier reached for its diode bridge (its 11.6), from
completely different physics, which is a small piece of evidence that the lab's habit of solving diode
networks in closed form rather than iterating is the right habit.

**But I do not recommend implementing it that way**, and here is why. ω(t) → t − ln t for large t, so the
OverEasy curve approaches a line that drifts logarithmically *below* the hard-knee line rather than merging
with it. That is real diode physics — the forward drop keeps creeping as the current rises — but it means the
OverEasy and hard-knee curves never converge, so switching the button at 30 dB of gain reduction would change
the level. dbx's own figures (Figure 2 in the 160A manual, Figure 3-1 in the 160X manual) draw the two curves
**merging** well above threshold [2][9], and dbx describe the threshold as the point "midway between the
onset of processing and that point at which the transfer curve **corresponds to the setting of the RATIO
control**" [9][2] — which asserts that the ratio really is attained.

So the function I recommend is the one with the same shape, the same derivative property and an exact
asymptote:

> **GR(x) = α · w · ln( 1 + exp( x / w ) )**

the softplus. Its properties, all of which are the properties Tyler specifies:

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| GR far below threshold | → α·w·exp(x/w) → 0, smoothly, never negative |
| GR far above threshold | → **α·x exactly**; the ratio really is attained |
| GR at the threshold | α·w·ln 2 = **0.693 α w**, i.e. "half-way into compression" |
| Slope d(GR)/dx | **α / (1 + exp(−x/w))**, a logistic rising smoothly from 0 to α |
| Instantaneous ratio | 1 / (1 − α·logistic), rising smoothly from 1:1 to R |
| Continuity | C^∞; the first derivative is continuous, as Tyler requires |
| Cost | one exp, one log, no iteration |

The choice between Wright omega and softplus is **mine**, it is a modelling decision rather than a reading of
the circuit, and it is recorded here as such. They differ only in the asymptote's drift; below about 15 dB of
gain reduction they are visually the same curve.

Setting **w = 0** recovers the hard knee exactly, since α·w·ln(1+e^(x/w)) → α·max(x,0) as w → 0. So one
function serves both modes and the OverEasy button is a knee-width switch. That is a pleasing correspondence
with the circuit, where the button also moves one component rather than switching a path.

### 6.4 How wide is the knee?

**dbx never published a knee width, and I could not derive one from the circuit.** This is the single largest
gap in the file and it is worth stating loudly rather than burying.

What I can bound it by. The knee width in decibels is w = V_θ/(G·K), where K = 6.1 mV/dB is known [30][31],
V_θ = n·V_T is the diode's scaled thermal voltage, and G is the difference amplifier's gain, which I could
not read. For a 1N4148 at sidechain currents the ideality factor n runs about 1.5 to 2, so V_θ is 39 to
52 mV at room temperature. Then:

| Difference-amp gain G | Knee width w |
|---|---|
| 1 | 6.4 to 8.5 dB |
| 2 (the value THAT use in their own design note [32]) | 3.2 to 4.3 dB |
| 3 | 2.1 to 2.8 dB |

So **w is somewhere between about 2 and 9 dB**, and I take **6 dB as the default**, labelled **estimate**.
That default puts about 4 dB of gain reduction at the threshold mark at ∞:1, and puts the top of the knee
roughly 12 dB above the threshold, which matches the proportions dbx draw in their own figures — but "matches
a drawing I looked at" is not evidence and I am not dressing it as any.

**What I tried and failed to do.** The OverEasy models have a third threshold LED that lights "when the
signal is in the Over Easy region" [9][2], so dbx defined that region's edges in hardware. On the 160X
schematic, switch section S103B swaps **R194 (43.2 kΩ)**, **R193 (3.3 MΩ)** and **R171 (604 kΩ)** into the
LM339 comparator chain that drives those LEDs [11]. Those three values encode the region's width. I could not
resolve enough of the surrounding network to turn them into decibels, so the knee width stays an estimate and
`dbx_knee_width` stays a parameter. If anyone ever traces that chain, this is the number to fill in.

**A consequence worth building in.** The rectifier comes *before* the ratio scaling (6.2), so the knee's
width in decibels does not depend on the ratio, but its depth does: at 2:1 the knee is the same number of dB
wide as at ∞:1 and half as deep. That is why dbx's OverEasy row reads "Program dependent, **affected by
THRESHOLD, COMPRESSION RATIO settings**" [8][9] while their hard-knee row does not.

### 6.5 What the infinity setting really is

The original 160's specification does not say infinity. It says:

> "COMPRESSION RATIO — Continuously variable from **1:1 to 120:1 (infinity)**." [1]

and the introduction repeats it: "can provide infinite compression (**approximately 120:1**)" [1]. So dbx
published the number, and the number is 120, not infinity. **The brief asked me to say what the ratio really
is if it is not what the panel says, and here it is: at the ∞ mark, the 160 is a 120:1 compressor.**

What limits it? Two readings, both consistent with the number, and I cannot distinguish them.

**Reading one: the control law is not perfectly linear.** THAT specify the descendant cell's gain-control
linearity as **0.5 % typical, 2 % maximum** over a −60 to +40 dB gain range [30]. A control-law error of ε
means that at α = 1 the achieved gain reduction is (1 ± ε) dB per dB of excess, leaving a residual slope of ε
and an effective ratio of 1/ε. At ε = 0.83 % that is **120:1** exactly (**derived**). dbx's figure sits
between THAT's typical (200:1) and worst case (50:1), which is where a 1976 potted module built from
hand-matched discrete transistors would be expected to sit.

**Reading two: the pot does not quite reach the end.** α = 1 − 1/120 = 0.9917, so a travel shortfall of 0.83 %
would do it too.

Either way the modelling instruction is the same: **do not clamp the ratio to a perfect brick wall at the ∞
mark**. Leave a residual slope of about 1/120 = 0.0083 dB per dB, which is one decibel of output rise for
every 120 dB of input rise, and expose it as a constant. It is inaudible on programme and it is the
difference between modelling the box and modelling the label.

The companion published figure is the maximum: "**over 60 dB maximum compression**" on the 160A [2], ">60 dB"
on the 160X [8][9], and on the original "the 160A is actually capable of delivering up to **60 dB** of gain
reduction" while its meter only shows 40 [2]. So the model's gain reduction should be allowed to reach 60 dB
and the meter should stop at 40.

### 6.6 The dial taper, measured and labelled as such

The electrical law is α, but the panel is marked in ratios, and the mapping between dial angle and α is
strongly non-linear because α = 1 − 1/R compresses everything above 4:1 into the last fifth of its range. dbx
shaped the pot to compensate: "The control curve of the RATIO potentiometer has been designed to provide
total operator control, with **scale expansion at the subtle lower ratios** for easy, repeatable settings"
[9][2].

I measured the nine label positions on dbx's own front-panel drawing for the original 160 (Image A, 3.1).
This is an **estimate** with real error bars — the labels sit outside the knob and their centroids are not
exactly radial, so each angle is good to perhaps ±10°, which is ±0.03 of full travel:

| Panel mark | 1 | 1.5 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 6 | 10 | 20 | ∞ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| α | 0 | 0.333 | 0.500 | 0.667 | 0.750 | 0.833 | 0.900 | 0.950 | 1.000 |
| Fraction of travel (**estimate**) | 0.00 | 0.09 | 0.17 | 0.31 | 0.46 | 0.67 | 0.84 | 0.92 | 1.00 |
| Angle from full CCW (**estimate**) | 0° | 27° | 54° | 95° | 142° | 208° | 261° | 286° | 311° |

A single-expression fit is **fraction ≈ α^2.5** (**estimate**; residuals up to 0.07 of travel, which is
within my measurement error at some points and not at others). For a plug-in, interpolating the nine points
is both more faithful and less trouble than fitting a curve to nine numbers I measured off a line drawing.

The 160A's dial is a different taper on a different set of marks — **1:1, 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 6:1, 10:1, ∞:1,
−5:1, −2:1, −1:1**, with the four negative marks squeezed into a fan at the clockwise end (3.2). Note what
that squeezing implies: α runs 0 → 1 over roughly five-sixths of the travel and 1 → 2 over the last sixth, so
the negative region covers as much *α* as the entire positive region in a fraction of the rotation. Infinity+
is a fine-adjustment nightmare on the hardware, and a plug-in that gives it a linear-in-α slider will feel
nothing like the box.

### 6.7 A summary of the static curve, in one place

For an input level L (dB), threshold T (dB), ratio coefficient α, knee width w and output gain g (dB):

```
x   = L − T                                   excess above threshold, in dB
GR  = α · w · ln(1 + exp(x / w))              gain reduction, dB   (w → 0 gives the hard knee)
GR  = min(GR, 60)                             published maximum compression [2][8][9]
out = L − GR + g                              output level, dB
```

with a residual slope of 1/120 left in at α = 1 (6.5). That is the whole static behaviour of a dbx 160 in
four lines, and every constant in it is either printed by dbx or labelled above as derived or estimated.

### 7.5 Two things the circuit publishes that the specification does not

**The audio path's low-frequency corner (derived).** C12 (0.15 µF) feeds R26 (100 kΩ) into the VCA's virtual
ground [4], so the input coupling corner is

> f₃ = 1 / (2π × 100 kΩ × 0.15 µF) = **10.6 Hz**   (**derived**)

which is −1.1 dB at 20 Hz. dbx publish no frequency response for the original 160, and the 160A's "+0, −3 dB
at 0.5 Hz" [2] is a different, later board with much bigger coupling capacitors. So the original had an
audible low-frequency tilt that its successor did not, and I would implement it, because it is a real
component pair and it is part of why people describe this box's bottom end the way they do (section 8).

**The audio path's high-frequency corner (derived).** C14 (22 pF) across R32 (100 kΩ) [4]:

> f₃ = 1 / (2π × 100 kΩ × 22 pF) = **72.3 kHz**   (**derived**)

The 160A publishes "−3 dB at 90 kHz" [2]. Different board, same order of magnitude, which is a small
independent check that I read R32 and C14 correctly.

## 8. How it is described as sounding, and what an emulation must get right

### 8.1 The published descriptions

dbx's own language is consistent across twenty years and two owners, and it is always about the detector:

> "True rms level detection **closely simulates the response of the human ear**. Even at high compression
> ratios, the gain changing action of the 160 Series is **highly listenable and natural sounding**." [1]

> "True RMS Level Detection — **senses the power in the program in a musical manner, much as the ear does**,
> giving results superior to peak or average detection." [2]

> "Due to the gentle OverEasy characteristic of your 160A you will find that **even fairly high ratios are
> handled transparently**." [2]

Universal Audio, who licensed it, describe it very differently, and the contrast is instructive:

> "Experience the **quirky** VCA compression of the vintage hardware ... capturing the **in-your-face sonics
> of its colorful VCA compression** ... the original hardware's **grabby, low-end glue and old-school
> character, full of the same unique nonlinearities** ... Give acoustic guitars and snare drums in-your-face
> **'thwack'**" [40]

dbx sold it in 1977 as transparent and accurate. Universal Audio sell it in 2026 as coloured and grabby. Both
are describing the same circuit, and both are right: a true-RMS detector *is* the accurate thing to do, and
the audible consequence of doing it — letting transients through untouched and clamping onto the body of the
note — is exactly what "grabby, low-end glue" describes.

### 8.2 The one testable sonic claim dbx made

Buried in the 160A's application notes is the only sentence anywhere in the shelf that predicts a specific
audible difference from a specific control:

> "In **OverEasy mode, the 160A takes slightly longer to react than in Hard Knee mode**, and will therefore
> **emphasize the slap at the beginning of the note and reduce the boominess of its body**." [2]

That is a claim about the *transient* consequence of the knee, and it is exactly what section 6.3's function
predicts: with a soft knee the slope starts at zero and rises through the logistic, so the first decibels of
a rising transient meet almost no gain reduction, and the reduction arrives as the level climbs into the
knee. A model with the softplus knee should reproduce that sentence, and section 12.4 makes it a test.

The rest of dbx's application advice is the usual starting points, and I list them because they are the
settings a preset should ship with [2]:

| Source | dbx's suggestion |
|---|---|
| Vocals | OverEasy, low-to-medium ratio around 4:1, threshold for 6–10 dB GR |
| Bass or electric guitar | 4:1, threshold for 10–12 dB GR |
| Hot guitar or synth parts | around 5:1, threshold at the average maximum level |
| Sustain on guitar or synth pads | 10:1 to ∞:1, threshold to taste |
| Kick drum | 6:1, threshold for **15 dB** GR, OverEasy |
| Drum submix, two channels | back off to **2:1** "to avoid an excess of cymbal 'splattering'" |
| Raising a track out of a mix | 2:1, threshold around −20 dB |
| Digital overload protection | Hard knee, ∞:1, threshold 2–3 dB below the converter's clip |
| Speaker protection | Hard knee, 10:1 or greater, 15 dB or more of compression |
| Line amplifier | 1:1, threshold fully clockwise, output gain to taste |
| Infinity+ effect | −1:1, −2:1 or −5:1, then "methodically strike a series of chords" |

### 8.3 What an emulation must get right, in priority order

**1. The detector must be true RMS, not a peak detector with a slow attack.** This is not a refinement, it is
the identity of the box. A true-RMS detector responds to *power*, so it under-reads high-crest material by a
specified amount (5.7: 1.0 dB at a crest factor of 8), and it therefore lets a snare's initial spike through
while grabbing the body behind it. A peak detector with a 15 ms attack will produce a similar-looking gain
reduction meter and a completely different sound, because it will chase the spike and then release from it.
Everything people mean by "grabby" and "glue" is downstream of this one choice.

**2. Attack and release must come from one time constant, and the release must be a constant dB/s.** Not two
knobs, not two constants, not an exponential release. τ ≈ 34.5 ms, attack faster for bigger steps, release a
straight line of about 120 dB/s in decibels (5.5). If a model has separate attack and release parameters it
is not a dbx 160, whatever its faceplate says.

**3. The hard knee must be genuinely hard.** dbx sold it as "mathematically precise" and built a precision
rectifier to make it so (6.2). A model that applies a 2 dB soft knee "because all real compressors have one"
has thrown away the thing that distinguishes the 160 from the 160A.

**4. The ratio must be one coefficient, and it must go past infinity.** α = 1 − 1/R, continuous through 1 and
on to 2 (6.1). Infinity+ is not a gimmick to bolt on; it is what the pot does when it keeps turning.

**5. The low-frequency third harmonic must come from detector ripple, not from a waveshaper.** It must fall
as 1/f, scale with the ratio, and be absent at 1:1 (5.6). This is the "glue" on bass and kick, and it is the
one distortion in the box that is a *consequence* of the compression rather than an addition to it.

**6. Leave the residual slope in at ∞:1.** 120:1, not a brick wall (6.5).

**7. The OverEasy knee must have a smoothly rising slope, not a rounded corner.** The instantaneous ratio
climbs from 1:1 to the set ratio through the knee, which is why dbx call the panel setting a *maximum* ratio
in that mode (6.3).

### 8.4 What I would not bother with

- The ground-loop compensation circuit (5.8).
- Modelling the discrete class-AB output stage's clipping behaviour. dbx publish the levels and no distortion
  figure at them [1], so anything here is invented.
- The optional output transformer on the 160A [2]. One sentence in a manual is not a model.
- The turn-on and turn-off muting FETs [4], unless somebody wants a bypass click.
- Unit-to-unit variation. dbx say it is 10 to 20 % [1] and give no distribution, so a randomiser here would
  be decoration.

## 10. Recommended DSP design

Real time, 44.1 to 96 kHz, no allocation on the audio thread, `f32` audio with `f64` where the detector needs
it. The model is the original 160 with the 160A's two features behind switches (2.5), and it says so on the
page.

### 10.1 The shape of the thing, in words

```
                     ┌──────────────────────────────── dbx_detector_source: external ──┐
                     │                                                                 │
in ──┬── dbx_lookahead delay ──► × 10^(−GR/20) ──► × makeup ──► dbx_mix ──► out         │
     │        (VCA: unity at GR = 0, because R26 = R32 = 100 kΩ)                        │
     └──► [ optional sidechain HP ] ──► power p = s²  (or s_L² + s_R² when linked) ─────┘
                     │
                     ├─► log-domain RMS filter, one pole, time constant τ  ──► L (dB)
                     ├─► x = L − T                                            excess, dB
                     ├─► knee:   G₀ = w · ln(1 + exp(x/w))                    softplus, dB
                     ├─► ratio:  GR = α · G₀ ,  α = 1 − 1/R                   one multiply
                     ├─► residual: at α = 1, leave slope 1/120 in
                     └─► clamp:  GR ≤ 60 dB
```

Four properties of that diagram are the whole model and each is defended above:

- The detector tap is on the **input**, not the output. Feedforward (1.3, 4.2).
- There is **one** time constant and **no** attack or release control (5.5).
- The knee and the ratio are **separate multiplications in the dB domain**, knee first (6.2, 6.4).
- The gain is applied as a **decibel** quantity, because both the cell and the detector are logarithmic with
  the same constant (5.3).

### 10.2 The detector, which is the interesting block

Work entirely in decibels. Define the **thermal decibel**

> **D = V_T / K = 25.9 mV / 6.1 mV/dB = 4.246 dB**   (**derived** from [30][31])

This is the natural unit of the log-domain filter and it is the reason everything about this box's ballistics
is scale-invariant. The continuous equation of the detector (5.5) is

> dL/dt = (D / τ) · ( exp( (L_inst − L) / D ) − 1 )

where **L_inst = 10·log₁₀(p)** is the instantaneous level of the detector's power signal, and **L** is the
stored level. This has an **exact discrete solution** for a sample-and-hold input over one sample period h,
which is what I recommend implementing — it costs one `exp` and one `ln` per sample, it is unconditionally
stable, and it needs no separate attack and release branch because there is not one:

```
a  = exp(-h / tau)                       // precomputed per sample rate
q0 = (L_inst - L) / D                    // excess in thermal decibels
m  = 1.0 - (1.0 - exp(-q0)) * a
L  = L_inst + D * ln(m)                  // note ln(m), since q = -ln(m) and L = L_inst - D*q
```

**Numerical guards, and why each is needed.**

- `L_inst` is `-inf` at a zero crossing. Floor the power at about −200 dBFS. **Do not smooth it** — the
  excursion at every zero crossing is precisely what generates the detector ripple, which is precisely what
  generates dbx's published low-frequency third harmonic (5.6). Smoothing it away removes the sound.
- `exp(-q0)` overflows when `q0` is very negative, which happens on silence after loud material. When
  `q0 < -40` (the signal more than 170 dB below the stored level), skip the general update and use the
  rate-limited form `L -= (D / tau) * h`, which is the exact asymptote.
- Run the detector in `f64`. The stored level can sit 100 dB above the instantaneous one and the difference
  matters.

**Why this is not a peak detector with a slow attack, in one line of behaviour:** feed it a sine and it
settles 3.01 dB below the peak, because RMS. Feed it a train of 1 ms pulses with a crest factor of 8 and it
reads about 1.0 dB low, because that is what a true-RMS detector does [31]. Test 12.2 asserts both.

### 10.3 The static curve

```
x  = L - threshold_db
G0 = if hard { max(x, 0.0) } else { w * ln_1p(exp(x / w)) }   // w -> 0 gives the hard knee
GR = alpha * G0
if alpha >= 1.0 { GR -= (alpha - 1.0 + 1.0/120.0) * ... }     // see below
GR = GR.min(60.0)
```

The residual slope needs care. At α = 1 the hardware achieves 120:1, not ∞:1 (6.5), and the cleanest way to
express that without special-casing is to cap the effective coefficient:

> **α_eff = min(α, 1 − 1/120) for α ≤ 1**, and α unchanged above 1

so that the ∞ mark gives a slope of exactly 1/120 and Infinity+ still reaches −1:1 at α = 2. Use
`ln_1p(exp(t))` rather than `ln(1.0 + exp(t))` for accuracy, and switch to the asymptote `t` for `t > 30` to
avoid overflowing `exp`.

### 10.4 The gain cell

Two effects, and only two.

**The gain itself.** `g = 10^(-GR/20)`, applied sample by sample. Exact, because the cell is exponential in
its control voltage by construction (5.2). No lookup table, no interpolation, no smoothing — the control
signal is already smooth because the detector smoothed it, and adding a second smoother would change the
published attack times.

**The symmetry residual.** The cell's own distortion is an even-order term from junction mismatch (5.2), and
it is what dbx trimmed R27 against [3]. Model it as a small asymmetric term applied to the cell's output:

> `y = g * (s + eps * s * |s| / s_ref)`

with `eps` set so that a 1 kHz sine at the reference level gives the published **0.075 % second harmonic at
infinite compression at +4 dBm output** [1]. This is the one place where a waveshaper is honest, because dbx
published the number it has to hit. It is level-dependent and ratio-independent, which is exactly what dbx's
footnote says: "2nd harmonic is relatively unaffected by compression ratio, time constants and frequency"
[8].

**The third harmonic is not modelled here at all.** It emerges from the detector's ripple modulating `g`
(5.6). If it does not appear on its own, the detector is wrong, and adding a third-harmonic waveshaper to
paper over that would hide the bug. Test 12.5 checks for it.

### 10.5 Filters

| Block | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Input coupling high-pass | −3 dB at **10.6 Hz**, one pole | **derived** from C12 = 0.15 µF and R26 = 100 kΩ [4] |
| Output low-pass | −3 dB at **72.3 kHz**, one pole | **derived** from C14 = 22 pF and R32 = 100 kΩ [4] |

The 72.3 kHz pole does nothing at 44.1 kHz and I would still implement it, because it costs one biquad and it
is the difference between the model being right at 96 kHz and being right by accident.

The **sidechain high-pass is not a dbx control**. I am adding it because the 160's detector input exists
precisely so that a filter can go in front of it, dbx spend three pages of the 160A manual explaining how
[2], and Universal Audio added the same thing to their licensed emulation [40]. It is labelled on the page as
an addition, in the same way the plug-in labels `dbx_mix`.

### 10.6 Stereo, and True RMS Power Summing

dbx's linking is not the usual maximum-of-two, and they explain why:

> "When two 160A units are slaved together, the **RMS energy** of the signal presented to the slave unit is
> **summed** with the RMS energy of the signal presented to the master unit **to prevent phase cancellation of
> the two signals** from causing unmusical compressor action." [2]

So the implementation is one detector fed with **p = s_L² + s_R²**, and one gain applied to both channels.
Not `max(L, R)`, not `(L+R)/2` summed as *signals* — dbx rejected that by name.

One consequence to document rather than hide: two identical channels give twice the power, so the linked
detector reads **3.01 dB higher** than either channel alone, and the effective threshold drops by 3 dB when
you engage the link. That is what the hardware does, it follows directly from the words above, and a model
that silently compensates for it is not modelling the box.

### 10.7 Metering

The original 160's meter is a VU movement with a three-position source switch, and the lab already has a VU
model in `dsp/vu.rs`. Three positions [1]:

- **INPUT LEVEL** and **OUTPUT LEVEL**: VU ballistics on the audio, 0 VU = +4 dBu at the factory setting,
  recalibratable from −10 dB (250 mV) to +10 dB (2.5 V).
- **GAIN CHANGE**: the control voltage, which in the hardware reaches the meter through the R49 / R50 divider
  (4.2). In the model this is simply `GR`, and it should be shown with the *meter's* ballistics, not the
  detector's, because that is what the movement does.

The two threshold LEDs get their own rule, and it is published: below lights under threshold, above lights
over it, and **both sit dimly lit when a steady sine is exactly at threshold** [1]. That is a two-line
special case and it is the single most charming detail on the panel.

If `dbx_model` is `160A`, swap the VU for the 19-LED level row and the 12-LED gain-reduction row, with the
scales and colours from 3.2, and add the third amber LED that lights inside the OverEasy region.

### 10.8 Oversampling and sample rate

Run the whole compressor at **2× oversampling**, reusing the lab's existing polyphase up- and down-samplers
from `dsp/fet/oversample.rs` ([[Distressor]] 6.3).

The reason is *not* the detector ripple, which is small and mostly below 1 kHz. It is the gain trajectory on
transients: at α = 1 the gain can move 30 dB in 3 ms, and a gain signal changing that fast multiplied against
full-bandwidth audio produces intermodulation products above Nyquist. 2× is enough; I see no argument for 4×
here, because there is no static waveshaper in the audio path except the small symmetry residual (10.4).

Everything derived from τ must be recomputed on a sample-rate change: `a = exp(-h/tau)` is the only
rate-dependent coefficient in the detector, which is a pleasant property of getting the exact solution rather
than discretising by hand.

## 11. Reuse, assessed honestly

The question put to me was whether a VCA component belongs in `noob-electrical-components`, shared between
the Distressor's engine, an SSL bus compressor being researched in parallel, and this one. The crate's own
rule is that "a component earns a place **once something real shares it, or is about to**", and its own
cautionary tale is the audit that found "three tube stages that were three different circuits sharing a word"
[47].

**My answer: yes to a Blackmer gain cell, no to a VCA.** Those are different proposals and the difference is
the whole point.

### 11.1 Why "a VCA" is the wrong shape

"VCA" is a functional category, not a part. It covers the Blackmer log-antilog cell, the OTA, the JFET in a
divider, the LDR, and the diode bridge — every one of which is a voltage-controlled amplifier and no two of
which share an equation. Building a component called `Vca` would be building the fourth tube stage.

More concretely, of the three candidate users:

- **The dbx 160 contains a named Blackmer cell.** The schematic prints "VCA (200)", reference designator M1,
  with a manufacturer's part number and a supplied matched transistor [4]. This is not an inference.
- **The SSL bus compressor is expected to contain one too**, since the SSL sidechain is a dbx-lineage RMS
  detector driving a dbx-lineage cell. I have not read the SSL dossier — it is being written in parallel —
  so I record that as my expectation and not as a fact, and the recommendation below is conditional on it.
- **The Distressor's gain element is an inference with no schematic behind it.** Empirical Labs never
  published one and had the reverse-engineered versions taken down; the lab's own dossier says so and labels
  the Blackmer attribution "a strong, standard inference, labelled as such, not a confirmed part choice"
  ([[Distressor]] 3.2). And the lab's *code* does not model a cell at all: `dsp/vca/compressor.rs` applies a
  dB-domain gain and carries a single constant, `VCA_THD = 0.0025`, standing in for everything the cell does.

So there is nothing in the Distressor to share. Extracting a component that two units genuinely contain and
a third merely resembles would repeat the tube-stage mistake exactly: a word doing the work that a circuit
should do.

### 11.2 What the Blackmer cell component would look like

If and only if the SSL turns out to contain one, `noob-electrical-components-blackmer-cell` earns a place on
the same footing as the diode bridge did — one real user today, a second next in the build order — and this
is its shape. Everything in it is a property of the **part**; everything excluded is a property of the
**machine**.

**What it knows** (all of it published in [30], [31] or [24]):

| Property | Value | Why it belongs to the part |
|---|---|---|
| Control law | gain_dB = −V_c / K | Blackmer's stated design objective, "constant decibels per volt" [24] |
| K | 6.1 mV/dB nominal, 6.0 to 6.2 | a manufacturing tolerance of the cell |
| Tempco | +0.33 %/°C referenced to 27 °C chip | a property of the junctions |
| Control-law linearity | 0.5 % typical, 2 % max over −60 to +40 dB | measured on the part, and it is what makes 120:1 (6.5) |
| Symmetry residual | an even-order term with a trim parameter | the mismatch of the two half-wave paths (5.2) |
| Gain range | >130 dB | |
| Dual control ports | positive and negative, +K and −K | pins 2 and 3 of the descendant [30] |

**What it must not know**: R26 and R32, the threshold, the ratio, the detector, the control-port divider, the
make-up gain, the meter. Those are the dbx 160, and an SSL will have different ones. This is the same line
the crate already draws around the photocell — "the photocell crate knows its own dark and lit resistances
... it knows nothing about the 70.7 kΩ series resistor an LA-2A puts in front of it" [47].

A sketch of the interface, in the crate's existing idiom:

```rust
pub struct BlackmerCell { k_mv_per_db: f32, symmetry: f32, linearity_pct: f32, temp_c: f32 }

impl BlackmerCell {
    /// Gain in dB for a control-port voltage, including the tempco and the
    /// control-law error. The caller owns the divider that produced `v_ctrl`.
    pub fn gain_db(&self, v_ctrl: f32) -> f32;
    /// The cell's own even-order residual, applied to one sample at the
    /// given gain. Zero when `symmetry` is trimmed to zero.
    pub fn process(&self, x: f32, gain_db: f32) -> f32;
}
```

Note that `gain_db` takes a **voltage**, not a decibel value. That is deliberate: the whole reason this is a
component rather than a multiply is the 6.1 mV/dB constant and its tolerance and tempco, and a caller that
passes decibels has already thrown those away.

### 11.3 The RMS detector: wait

The same test applied to the second module gives a different answer today. The dbx 208 is a real named part
on a real schematic [4], and the SSL almost certainly has a sibling of it — but "almost certainly" is exactly
the standard the crate's README warns about, and a detector extracted from one user would very likely be the
wrong shape for the second, because the SSL's sidechain has switched time constants and an auto-release that
this one does not.

So: **build the log-domain RMS detector inside the dbx model first**, in the lab, where it can be wrong
cheaply. When the SSL dossier lands, compare the two detectors block by block, and extract only what both
genuinely share — which I would guess is the log-domain filter and the crest-factor behaviour, and is
probably not the time constants or the release shaping. That is the discipline that produced the photocell,
and it is the one the CL 1B's refusal validated ([[CL-1B]], and [47]).

### 11.4 What the dbx 160 shares with the lab as it stands

- **Nothing with the photocell or the diode bridge.** Different families entirely.
- **The dB-domain gain application** already used by `dsp/vca`, `dsp/fet` and the rest. That is infrastructure,
  not a component, and the crate's README explicitly excludes infrastructure [47].
- **The oversamplers** in `dsp/fet/oversample.rs` (10.8).
- **The VU model** in `dsp/vu.rs` (10.7).
- **The Wright omega discussion** in [[Neve-33609]] 11.6, which is the same special function reached from
  different physics (6.3) — a shared *technique*, not shared code, and worth a cross-reference in both files.

- **Not** the Distressor's per-ratio curve table. That table exists because Empirical Labs never published a
  law and the shapes had to be fitted ([[Distressor]] 7.4). The dbx has a law: α = 1 − 1/R, exactly, from the
  circuit (6.1). Importing a table here would replace an exact result with a fitted one.

### 11.5 The blunt version

Two units, the dbx 160 and probably the SSL, contain the same named gain cell and would be better off sharing
one model of it. A third, the Distressor, contains a word. Unify the two; leave the third alone; and do not
call the result "a VCA".

## 12. Test plan

**The two standards this repository enforces, and both apply here.** Every test asserts a **published
figure**, names the figure, and cites it. Where no real number is reachable, the test says so and asserts a
*direction*, an *ordering* or a *circuit identity* instead of an invented bound. An audit found nine tests
across the lab's plug-ins that compared a model against its own output and therefore could never fail, so
this is a rule rather than a preference [44][47].

This model is unusually well served by that rule, because dbx published seven interlocking dynamic figures
and a distortion *law* rather than a distortion number. Section 12.3 is the strongest set of dynamics tests
any model in the lab has had, and section 12.5 asserts a frequency relationship that needs no absolute
calibration at all.

Unless a test says otherwise: `dbx_model` = `160`, `dbx_knee` = `hard`, `dbx_link` off, `dbx_mix` 100 %,
`dbx_lookahead` 0, `dbx_sidechain_hp` off, run at 44.1, 48 and 96 kHz.

### 12.1 Static behaviour and calibration

1. **Unity gain with no compression.** `dbx_ratio` 1:1, `dbx_threshold` +20 dBu, `dbx_output` 0: the output
   equals the input to within 1 × 10⁻⁶, above the 10.6 Hz corner.
   *Figure:* a circuit identity — R26 and R32 are **both 100 kΩ**, so the transimpedance stage exactly undoes
   the input resistor at zero control voltage. *Source:* the 160 schematic [4]. dbx also document the setting:
   "To use either model as a line amplifier, adjust COMPRESSION RATIO to its maximum counterclockwise
   position ('1:1'), THRESHOLD to its maximum clockwise position ('3V')" [1].

2. **Output gain is exact over its whole range.** With no gain reduction, stepping `dbx_output` from −20 to
   +20 dB in 1 dB steps changes the output by exactly the printed amount, **±0.1 dB** at every step.
   *Figure:* "Output Level Adjust (Continuous) — **±20 dB from unity gain point**", and the schematic marks
   R80's track ends "−20db" and "+20db". *Sources:* [1][4]. The ±0.1 dB is **my** tolerance; dbx publish
   none, and a DC potentiometer across a trimmed rail deserves a tight one.

3. **Output gain does not move the threshold.** With the threshold set for 10 dB of gain reduction, sweeping
   `dbx_output` across its full range changes the gain reduction by **less than 0.05 dB**.
   *Figure:* "The OUTPUT GAIN control **does not interact with the threshold of compression**." *Source:*
   160A manual [2], and the same sentence in the 160X manual [9]. This is a strong structural test: it fails
   immediately if anyone wires the make-up before the detector tap.

4. **The threshold dial is logarithmic at 10 dB per mark.** Set `dbx_threshold` to each of the six printed
   marks (10 mV, 30 mV, 100 mV, 300 mV, 1 V, 3 V) and find the input level at which gain reduction first
   reaches 0.1 dB. Successive marks must differ by **10 dB ±0.5 dB**, and the endpoints must be
   **−38 dB and +12 dB ±0.5 dB**.
   *Figure:* "Continuously variable from **10mV(−38dB) to 3V(+12dB)**", and the factory procedure "Step the
   oscillator up and down in **10 db steps** verifying that the threshold level matches the input signal at
   successive calibration marks on the threshold dial". *Sources:* [1][3]. **This is the primary calibration
   of the model**, because dbx's own factory used exactly this procedure.

5. **The threshold LEDs, including the both-dim case.** With a steady 1 kHz sine exactly at the threshold
   setting, the below-threshold and above-threshold indicators must **both** report a partial, equal
   illumination; 1 dB either side, one must be fully on and the other off.
   *Figure:* "A steady-state, sine-wave tone **exactly at the threshold voltage** causes both L.E.D.'s to
   remain **dimly illuminated**." *Source:* the 160's specification page [1].

6. **The hard-knee ratio is exact at every printed mark.** With the threshold at −20 dBu and the input 20 dB
   above it, raise the input a further 20 dB and measure the output change:

   | `dbx_ratio` | required output change |
   |---|---|
   | 1.5:1 | 13.33 dB |
   | 2:1 | 10.00 dB |
   | 3:1 | 6.67 dB |
   | **4:1** | **5.00 dB** |
   | 6:1 | 3.33 dB |
   | 10:1 | 2.00 dB |
   | 20:1 | 1.00 dB |

   *Figure:* "Hard-knee: **COMPRESSION RATIO setting defines exact compression ratio**, continuously variable
   from 1:1 to ∞:1 to −1:1." *Sources:* the 1981 brochure's specification table and the 160X manual's
   section 1.4 [8][9]. **dbx publish no tolerance**, so I set **±0.15 dB** over the 20 dB span, and record
   that as my number and not theirs. The 4:1 row is the one to weight hardest, because the 160's schematic
   carries a trimmer labelled "**4:1 CAL**" — R43, 50 kΩ — so 4:1 is the point the factory actually
   calibrated [4].

7. **The infinity mark is 120:1, not infinity.** `dbx_ratio` at ∞, threshold −40 dBu, input swept from
   −20 dBu to +20 dBu (a 40 dB rise, entirely above threshold): the output must rise by
   **0.33 dB, +0.15/−0.33 dB**.
   *Figure:* "Continuously variable from **1:1 to 120:1 (infinity)**", restated in the introduction as
   "infinite compression (**approximately 120:1**)". *Source:* the 160's specification page and introduction
   [1]. 40 dB ÷ 120 = 0.333 dB. The asymmetric tolerance is deliberate: a model that produces *less* than
   0.33 dB has built a brick wall dbx did not, which is the failure this test exists to catch. This is the
   single test that distinguishes modelling the circuit from modelling the silkscreen.

8. **Negative ratios invert.** `dbx_model` = `160A`, `dbx_ratio` = −1:1, threshold −40 dBu, input raised by
   10 dB well above threshold: the output must **fall** by **10 dB ±0.3 dB**.
   *Figure:* "At a setting of −1:1, the above threshold input signal must increase by 1dB in level to
   **decrease** the signal at the output of the 160A by 1dB." *Source:* the 160A manual [2]. Repeat at −2:1
   (output falls 5 dB) and −5:1 (output falls 2 dB), from α = 1 − 1/R.

9. **Maximum compression reaches 60 dB.** `dbx_ratio` ∞, threshold −40 dBu, input at +20 dBu: the model's
   gain reduction must reach at least **60 dB**.
   *Figure:* "**over 60dB maximum compression**". *Sources:* [2][8][9]. And the 160A manual's note that the
   meter shows 40 dB "although the 160A is actually capable of delivering up to 60dB of gain reduction" [2],
   which test 12.7.2 checks separately.

10. **Infinity+ is unreachable on the original.** With `dbx_model` = `160`, `dbx_ratio` must clamp at ∞:1 and
    the OverEasy switch must be unavailable.
    *Figure:* the 160's published ratio range stops at 120:1 (infinity) [1], and OverEasy is "the classic
    'Hard Knee' curve popularized by the **original dbx 160, 161 and 162**" [2][10]. This test exists because
    the composite model (2.5) is the easiest thing in the file to get wrong.

### 12.2 The detector really is true RMS

11. **A sine settles 3.01 dB below its peak.** Drive the detector with a 1 kHz sine of peak amplitude A and
    read the settled detector level: it must be **20·log₁₀(A) − 3.01 dB, ±0.15 dB**.
    *Figure:* not a dbx number but a mathematical identity — the RMS of a sine is 1/√2 of its peak, and dbx
    specify "**True rms level-detection**". *Source:* [1]. This is the cheapest possible test that somebody
    has not quietly substituted a peak detector, and it is the first one I would write.

12. **Crest-factor under-reading, at three published points.** Drive with rectangular pulse trains at a 1 ms
    repetition rate and crest factors of 3.5, 5 and 8, all of equal true RMS. The detector must read **low**
    by **0.2 dB, 0.5 dB and 1.0 dB** respectively, each within ±0.3 dB.
    *Figure:* the crest-factor table, "1 ms pulse repetition rate; 0.2 dB error — 3.5; 0.5 dB error — 5;
    1.0 dB error — 8". *Source:* the THAT 2252 datasheet [31], which is the descendant part and is cited as
    such (5.1). **dbx publish no crest-factor figure**, so this test asserts the successor's numbers and says
    so; if that substitution is judged unacceptable, the test should assert only the *ordering* (error grows
    monotonically with crest factor) rather than the magnitudes.

13. **A peak detector would read differently.** With the same pulse train, the ghost trace of 10.11 (a peak
    detector with the same time constant) must read **at least 6 dB higher** than the RMS detector at a crest
    factor of 8.
    *Figure:* no published number exists. This test asserts a **direction** and a magnitude that follows from
    the definitions, and is recorded as a sanity check on the ghost trace rather than as evidence about the
    hardware.

14. **The detector responds to power, not to sum.** Two uncorrelated noise sources of equal level, summed,
    must raise the detector by **3.01 dB ±0.2 dB** over either alone; the same two sources in antiphase must
    also raise it by 3.01 dB when power-summed through the link (12.6).
    *Figure:* a circuit identity from "**True RMS Power Summing**" [2] and from the squaring action of the
    detector [31].

### 12.3 Ballistics: the strongest test set in the file

15. **The three published attack times.** `dbx_ratio` ∞ (dbx measure "in the infinite compression region"),
    `dbx_knee` hard, threshold well below the signal. Step a 1 kHz sine up by 10, 20 and 30 dB and measure
    the time for the gain reduction to reach **63 % of its final value in decibels**:

    | Step | dbx's figure | Implied τ | Model at τ = 34.5 ms |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 10 dB | **15 ms** | 33.9 ms | 15.3 ms |
    | 20 dB | **5 ms** | 27.3 ms | 6.3 ms |
    | 30 dB | **3 ms** | 39.8 ms | 2.6 ms |

    *Figure:* "Program-Dependent; Typically **15ms for 10 dB, 5 ms for 20 dB, 3 ms for 30 dB**", with the
    definition "time required to reduce signal by **63 % of level increase** (above threshold)" measured "in
    the infinite compression region of the threshold curve". *Sources:* [1][2][8][9], identical in all four.

    **The tolerance and why it is what it is.** dbx's own three figures are **mutually inconsistent**: each
    implies a different detector time constant, spanning 27 to 40 ms (5.5), so no single-τ model — and the
    hardware is a single-τ model — can hit all three. I therefore assert **±30 %**, which is the spread of
    dbx's own data, and I record that the 20 dB point is the binding one and that the model is 27 % high
    there. If this test fails, **re-fit τ; do not widen the band.**

16. **Attack really does get faster with bigger steps.** From the same measurement, the three times must be
    **strictly decreasing**, and the 30 dB attack must be at least **four times faster** than the 10 dB one.
    *Figure:* 15 / 5 / 3 ms is a 5:1 spread [1]; the derived model gives 5.9:1. Asserting the *ordering* and a
    conservative bound tests the program dependence itself, independently of the absolute values, and it is
    the test that fails loudly if anyone substitutes a fixed-time-constant attack.

17. **The release is a straight line in decibels.** Drive to 40 dB of gain reduction, remove the input, and
    fit a line to gain reduction against time between 35 dB and 5 dB. The residual from the straight-line fit
    must be **under 0.5 dB**, and the slope must be **120 to 125 dB/s**.
    *Figures:* "**RELEASE RATE — 120dB/second**" (the 160) and "**125dB/Sec rate**" (the 160A). *Sources:*
    [1][2]. **The straight-line assertion is the important half**, because it is the structural claim: a
    log-domain filter releases at a constant dB/s and an ordinary RC does not. A model with an exponential
    release will pass the slope test near one point and fail the linearity test everywhere.

18. **The three published release times are one rate.** Measure the time to recover 1 dB, 10 dB and 50 dB of
    gain reduction: **8 ms, 80 ms and 400 ms**, each ±20 %.
    *Figure:* "Program Dependent; Typically **8ms for 1dB, 80ms for 10dB, 400ms for 50dB**". *Source:* the
    160A specification page [2]. These are 1, 10 and 50 divided by 125 dB/s to the digit (5.5), so this test
    is a consistency check on test 17 rather than new information — and if it passes while 17's linearity
    test fails, the implementation has hard-coded three numbers instead of one rate.

19. **Attack and release are locked.** Change `dbx_tau` from 34.5 ms to 69 ms. Every attack time must
    approximately **double** and the release rate must approximately **halve**, with the product of the 10 dB
    attack time and the release rate constant to within 5 %.
    *Figure:* "How fast the 2252 acquires a signal (the 'attack'), and how fast it returns to rest following a
    signal (the 'release'), are **locked in relationship to each other** ... **Separate attack and release
    adjustments are not possible** within the constraint of rms response." *Source:* the THAT 2252 datasheet
    [31], and dbx's own "no manual attack/release adjustments are required" [1]. This is the test that proves
    the model has one time constant rather than two dressed up as one.

20. **Release does not depend on the ratio.** Repeat test 17 at 2:1, 4:1 and ∞:1, each driven to the same
    40 dB of *detector* excess: the measured dB/s must agree within **5 %**.
    *Figure:* a circuit identity — the ratio coefficient α scales the detector's output *after* the filter
    (6.1), so it cannot change the filter's rate. Recorded as a structural test with no published number
    behind it.

### 12.4 The knee

**dbx never published a knee width** (6.4, 7.6). Every test here therefore asserts a published *statement*
about shape, not an invented magnitude, and the one number in the section is labelled.

21. **The hard knee is genuinely hard.** `dbx_knee` hard, `dbx_ratio` 4:1: measure the local slope 0.5 dB
    below and 0.5 dB above threshold. Below it must be **1.000 ±0.002**; above it must be **0.250 ±0.002**.
    *Figure:* "a **mathematically precise** 'hard' threshold — at any compression ratio selected", and "In
    Hard Knee mode, the threshold of compression is defined as that point above which the output level **no
    longer changes on a 1:1 basis** with changes in the input level". *Sources:* [9][2]. The tight tolerance
    is justified by the mechanism: the diode sits inside a feedback loop, so the corner is sharpened by the
    op amp's open-loop gain (6.2).

22. **OverEasy has a continuously rising slope.** `dbx_knee` overeasy, `dbx_ratio` 4:1: measure the local
    slope at 20 evenly spaced points from 20 dB below threshold to 20 dB above. The slope must be
    **monotonically decreasing** (from 1.0 toward 0.25), must have **no discontinuity** greater than the step
    size implies, and must be within 2 % of 1.0 at the bottom and within 2 % of 0.25 at the top.
    *Figure:* "an input-output characteristic curve which is a function having a **continuous slope or first
    derivative** ... and a **gradually increasing compression ratio** between these two portions of the
    curve". *Source:* dbx's OverEasy patent, US 4,182,993 [28]. This asserts the patent's stated design
    objective directly, which is the most authoritative statement about OverEasy that exists.

23. **The ratio control is a maximum in OverEasy and an exact value in hard knee.** At 4:1, OverEasy: the
    slope must **never exceed 0.25** anywhere on the curve, and must be strictly greater than 0.25 (that is,
    less compression) at every point below 10 dB above threshold.
    *Figure:* "OverEasy: ... **COMPRESSION RATIO control determines maximum compression ratio**" against
    "Hard-knee: COMPRESSION RATIO setting defines **exact** compression ratio". *Sources:* [8][9].

24. **The threshold sits in the middle of the knee.** In OverEasy at ∞:1, the gain reduction at the threshold
    setting must be **between 40 % and 60 %** of the value the hard-knee curve would give at the point where
    the OverEasy slope has reached 95 % of α.
    *Figure:* "the THRESHOLD setting corresponds to a point on the input/output transfer curve **midway
    between the onset of processing and that point at which the transfer curve corresponds to the setting of
    the RATIO control**". *Source:* [9][2]. The softplus gives exactly ln 2 = 0.693 of α·w at threshold
    (6.3), so the 40–60 % band is a loose reading of "midway" and is **my** interpretation of dbx's word.

25. **OverEasy is slower onto a transient than hard knee.** Feed a 20 dB step at ∞:1 with the threshold
    10 dB below the low level. The time to 63 % of final gain reduction in OverEasy must be **longer** than
    in hard knee, and the first 3 ms of the compressed transient must be **louder** in OverEasy.
    *Figure:* "In **OverEasy mode, the 160A takes slightly longer to react than in Hard Knee mode**, and will
    therefore **emphasize the slap at the beginning of the note** and reduce the boominess of its body."
    *Source:* the 160A manual's kick-drum application note [2]. This is the only published sentence anywhere
    predicting an audible consequence of the knee switch, and it is a direction rather than a magnitude, so
    the test asserts a direction.

26. **The knee width does not depend on the ratio; its depth does.** Measure the level span over which the
    slope moves from 5 % to 95 % of its final change, at 2:1 and at ∞:1. The two spans must agree within
    **10 %**, while the total gain reduction across the knee at 2:1 must be **half** that at ∞:1, ±5 %.
    *Figure:* a circuit identity — the rectifier precedes the ratio scaling in dbx's own patent figure
    (6.2, 6.4). *Source:* [28]. No number is published, so this asserts the topology.

### 12.5 Distortion, which is the detector showing through

27. **The third harmonic halves per octave of frequency.** `dbx_ratio` ∞, hard knee, threshold set for a
    steady 10 dB of gain reduction on a sine. Measure the third-harmonic level at 50, 100, 200 and 400 Hz.
    Each must be **0.50 × the previous, ±0.05**.
    *Figure:* "3rd-harmonic distortion in the 160 Series decreases linearly as the frequency rises: **at
    100 Hz 3rd-harmonic distortion is 1/2 the value at 50 Hz**, etc." *Source:* the 160's specification-page
    footnote [1]. **This is the best test in the file**, because it asserts a *ratio* between two of the
    model's own measurements against a published law, so it needs no absolute calibration and cannot be
    passed by fudging a gain constant. It also fails immediately if anybody adds a third-harmonic waveshaper,
    because a waveshaper's third harmonic does not depend on frequency.

28. **The third harmonic falls with lower ratios and rises with faster time constants.** At a fixed input
    level and threshold, the third harmonic at 2:1 must be **lower** than at ∞:1; and with `dbx_tau` doubled,
    it must be **lower** again at every ratio.
    *Figure:* "3rd harmonic decreases with **slower time constants, higher frequencies and lower compression
    ratios**". *Source:* the 1981 brochure's footnote 4 [8]. Three directions, all published, none of them a
    magnitude.

29. **The second harmonic is unaffected by ratio, time constant and frequency.** Measure the second harmonic
    at 2:1, 4:1 and ∞:1; at 100 Hz, 1 kHz and 10 kHz; and at two values of `dbx_tau`. All nine results must
    agree within **±20 %**.
    *Figure:* "**2nd harmonic is relatively unaffected by compression ratio, time constants and frequency**".
    *Source:* [8]. The ±20 % is **mine**; "relatively unaffected" carries no number. This test is the
    companion to 27 and 28, and together the three separate the two distortion mechanisms exactly as dbx's
    own footnote does.

30. **The second harmonic hits its published magnitude.** 1 kHz sine, `dbx_ratio` ∞, output at +4 dBm
    (+4 dBu at the model's reference level): second harmonic **0.075 %, ±30 %**.
    *Figure:* "**0.075 % 2nd harmonic at infinite compression at +4dBm output**". *Source:* the 160's
    specification page [1]. This is the one number the symmetry-residual constant of 10.4 is fitted to, so
    strictly it is a calibration rather than a test — and it is recorded as such. What makes it a real test
    is running it at 100 Hz and 10 kHz too and requiring the same answer (test 29).

31. **Total harmonic distortion stays under the published ceiling.** `dbx_model` = `160A`, 1 kHz, gain
    reduction stepped from 0 to 40 dB in 5 dB steps: THD must be **under 0.2 %** at every step.
    *Figure:* "THD **<0.2 %, Typical, Any Amount of Compression up to 40 dB @ 1 kHz**". *Source:* the 160A
    specification page [2]. A ceiling across the whole gain-reduction range, with its own stated conditions,
    and the only such figure in the family.

32. **Distortion below threshold.** `dbx_model` = `160A` (whose sibling the 160X publishes the figure),
    1 kHz at 0 dBm with no gain reduction: second harmonic **0.07 %** and third harmonic **0.07 %**, each
    ±50 %.
    *Figure:* "Distortion below threshold: 2nd harmonic 0.07 %; 3rd harmonic 0.07 %", "Measured at 1 kHz,
    0 dBm input and output". *Sources:* [8][9]. The ±50 % is mine and is deliberately loose, because this is
    the 160X's board and not the 160's.

33. **The model's own predicted third harmonic, recorded and checked.** At 100 Hz, ∞:1, steady compression,
    the third harmonic should come out near **0.8 %** (the value section 5.6 derives from the ripple
    equation), against dbx's published "**0.5 % 3rd harmonic typical at infinite compression ratio**" [1].
    The test asserts **0.3 % to 1.2 %** and its purpose is to catch a detector whose ripple is an order of
    magnitude wrong. The band brackets both the published figure and my derivation, and it is recorded as an
    order-of-magnitude check rather than a calibration, because dbx did not state the frequency of their
    0.5 % figure.

### 12.6 Stereo linking

34. **True RMS Power Summing, not maximum and not signal summing.** Feed the left channel a 1 kHz sine and
    the right channel silence, `dbx_link` on: both channels must show identical gain reduction, and it must
    equal what the left channel alone would produce. Now feed both channels the same sine: the detector must
    read **3.01 dB higher, ±0.2 dB**, than with one channel.
    *Figure:* "the **RMS energy** of the signal presented to the slave unit is **summed** with the RMS energy
    of the signal presented to the master unit **to prevent phase cancellation** of the two signals from
    causing unmusical compressor action". *Source:* the 160A manual [2]. The 3.01 dB follows from summing
    powers and is derived, not published.

35. **Antiphase channels do not cancel.** Feed the two channels the same sine in antiphase: the detector must
    read the **same** as with them in phase, ±0.2 dB.
    *Figure:* the "prevent phase cancellation" clause above [2]. This is the whole reason dbx sum energies
    rather than signals, and it is a one-line test that catches the wrong implementation instantly.

36. **Unlinked channels are independent.** `dbx_link` off, left driven, right silent: the right channel's
    gain reduction must be **0.00 dB**.
    *Figure:* "the 160As **DO NOT operate independently** because the 'ring' of the strapping cable still sums
    the audio of each unit. **The strapping cable must be removed** for proper single-channel operation."
    *Source:* [2] — dbx's warning that a plugged-in-but-unslaved pair is *not* independent, which is the
    hardware's quirk and not the model's, and which the model reproduces by having no cable at all.

### 12.7 Metering

37. **Meter range and calibration.** The level meter must span **60 dB, −40 dB to +20 dB**, and read **0** at
    **+4 dBu (1.23 V)** with `dbx_meter_cal` at its factory setting.
    *Figures:* "METERING Range — **60dB (−40dB to +20dB)**" and "The meter in the 160 and 161 is factory
    calibrated to read '0' at **+4dB (1.23V)** output level." *Source:* [1].

38. **The gain-reduction display stops at 40 dB while the compressor goes to 60.** Drive the model to 55 dB
    of gain reduction: the meter must read **40 dB** (its endpoint) while `GR` reports 55.
    *Figure:* "The lower row of 12 LEDs displays up to **40dB** of GAIN REDUCTION (although the 160A is
    actually capable of delivering up to **60dB** of gain reduction)". *Source:* [2].

39. **Make-up gain does not appear on the gain-reduction meter.** Sweep `dbx_output` across its range with a
    fixed 10 dB of gain reduction: the gain-reduction meter must not move, while the output-level meter must
    follow.
    *Figure:* "Note that fixed gain changes due to the OUTPUT GAIN control are **not displayed by the GAIN
    REDUCTION LEDs** but **are reflected in the OUTPUT LEVEL display**." *Source:* [2].

40. **Meter recalibration range.** `dbx_meter_cal` must move the 0 point over **−10 dB (250 mV) to +10 dB
    (2.5 V)** on the 160, and **−15 dBu (138 mV) to +10 dBu (2.45 V)** on the 160A.
    *Figures:* the two specification pages [1][2]. Note the disagreement recorded in 3.3 between the 160's
    figure caption and its own specification table; this test asserts the specification table.

### 12.8 Frequency response, robustness, and what I will not test

41. **The input coupling corner.** With no compression, the response must be **−3 dB at 10.6 Hz ±10 %** and
    **−1.1 dB at 20 Hz ±0.2 dB**.
    *Figure:* a circuit identity from **C12 = 0.15 µF** into **R26 = 100 kΩ**. *Source:* the 160 schematic
    [4]. **dbx publish no frequency response for the 160 at all** (7.1), so this test asserts two components
    rather than a specification, and it is labelled as derived. Note that this model will therefore *fail*
    the 160A's published "+0, −0.5 dB, 20 Hz – 20 kHz" [2] — correctly, because that is a different board.

42. **The output corner.** −3 dB at **72.3 kHz ±10 %**, tested at 96 kHz where it is in band.
    *Figure:* a circuit identity from **C14 = 22 pF** across **R32 = 100 kΩ** [4]. The 160A's published
    "−3 dB at 90 kHz" [2] is a loose corroboration from the later board.

43. **Sample-rate invariance.** Run tests 4, 6, 7, 15, 17 and 27 at 44.1, 48, 88.2 and 96 kHz. Thresholds
    within **0.2 dB**, ratios within **0.05 dB**, attack and release times within **3 %**, the third-harmonic
    frequency law within **±0.03** on the ratio. The only rate-dependent coefficient in the detector is
    `a = exp(-h/tau)` (10.2), so anything worse than this indicates a discretisation bug rather than a
    modelling choice.

44. **Numerical robustness.** Ten seconds at full scale followed by thirty seconds of digital silence: no
    NaN, no infinity, no denormals with flush-to-zero enabled, and the detector level must fall smoothly at
    the release rate rather than jumping. Then DC, full-scale square waves at 20 Hz and 20 kHz, single-sample
    impulses, and every parameter at both extremes: the output must stay finite and bounded. The
    `q0 < -40` guard of 10.2 must be exercised by the silence case.

45. **Performance.** One stereo instance at 2× oversampling and 96 kHz within a small fixed CPU budget
    (**estimate**: under 2 % of one 2020-class core), no allocation on the audio thread, and no branch on
    `dbx_model` inside the per-sample loop.

**What I will not test, and why.**

- **Noise.** dbx publish −78 dBm EIN for the 160, <−89 dBm for the 160X and <−90 dBu for the 160A
  [1][8][2], but the model has no noise source and adding one to pass a test would be inventing a
  measurement. If noise is ever modelled, these are the figures to assert.
- **Slew rate.** ">10 V/µs" [8][9] is meaningless for a sampled model.
- **Output clipping levels.** +26 dB bridging and +24 dBm into 600 Ω [1] with no distortion figure attached,
  so there is nothing to assert beyond the level itself, and the model does not clip.
- **CMRR, input impedance, output impedance, dimensions, weight and power.** Real published figures
  [1][2][8][9][13] with no meaning inside a plug-in.
- **Anything about the ground-loop compensation or the muting FETs** (5.8, 8.4).
- **A knee width in decibels.** dbx never published one and I will not assert a number I estimated (6.4).
  Section 12.4 tests the knee's *shape* against dbx's own statements instead, which is the honest substitute.

**Patents**

23. **David E. Blackmer, US 3,681,618, "RMS circuits with bipolar logarithmic converter"**, filed 29 March
    1971, granted 1 August 1972. The RMS detector: the bipolar log converter, the ±2 gain stages, the
    half-wave rectifiers, and the constant-current source that drives the long-term average of the rectifier
    currents into equality with itself. Sections 1.1 and 5.3.
    https://patents.google.com/patent/US3681618A/en
24. **David E. Blackmer, US 3,714,462, "Multiplier circuits"**, filed 14 June 1971, granted 30 January 1973.
    The Blackmer gain cell: opposite-polarity log feedback paths, antilog transistors, and the stated
    objective of "a constant decibels per volt control characteristic" over "at least a ±50 decibel range".
    Sections 1.1 and 5.2. https://patents.google.com/patent/US3714462A/en
25. **US 3,789,143, "Compander with control signal logarithmically related to the instantaneous rms value of
    the input signal"**. One of the five listed on the 160's schematic page.
    https://patents.google.com/patent/US3789143A/en
26. **US 4,097,767, "Operational rectifier"**. Listed on the 160's schematic page, and the block section 6.2
    is about. https://patents.google.com/patent/US4097767A/en
27. **US 4,101,849, "Adaptive filter"**. The fifth of the five, covering dbx's companding noise reduction
    rather than the compressor. https://patents.google.com/patent/US4101849A/en
28. **Leslie B. Tyler, assigned to dbx Inc., US 4,182,993, "Signal amplitude compression system"**, filed
    2 November 1978, granted 8 January 1980. **The OverEasy patent.** Names the dbx 160 as prior art with a
    fixed ratio and a discontinuity, states the objective of "a continuous slope or first derivative" and "a
    gradually increasing compression ratio", describes the gain-changer block as a diode in series with a
    resistor into an inverting stage, and says in terms that "diodes 192 and 234 are utilized for their
    forward bias voltage current characteristics in shaping the input output characteristic curve". Sections
    2.2, 6.2 and 6.3. https://patents.google.com/patent/US4182993A/en
29. **David E. Blackmer, US 4,403,199, "Gain control systems"**, granted 6 September 1983. Later than the
    160 and not used here. https://patents.google.com/patent/US4403199A/en
