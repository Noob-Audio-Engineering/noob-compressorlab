# The Universal Audio variable-mu limiting amplifier, 175, 175B and 176: research notes for the variable-mu side of `noob-compressorlab`

Research dossier for the variable-mu model of the `noob-compressorlab` example plug-in of
noob-vst-webgui-framework. The example is a humorous, affectionate spoof of the Universal Audio 175B and 176
limiting amplifiers. It is not a product, it is not endorsed by anybody, and it does not use the Universal
Audio, UA, UREI or 176 names as its own name. Trademarks below belong to their owners and are used only to
identify the devices and the products discussed. This model sits behind the same per-instance `model` switch
that already selects the 1176, LA-2A, LA-3A, CL 1B, Distressor, 6176 and Neve behaviours; see [[1176]],
[[610]], [[LA-2A]], [[LA-3A]], [[CL-1B]], [[Distressor]] and [[Neve-33609]] [41] [42] [43].

Conventions (kept the same as the other dossiers so they all read alike):

- Citations are `[n]`; the numbered list in section 12 gives the URL for every source, and reference-style
  link definitions at the very end make the `[n]` markers clickable.
- Numbers that come from a manufacturer specification, a manual, a schematic, a datasheet or a published
  graph are attributed. Numbers that are my own derivation or assumption are labelled **estimate** or
  **derived**. Nothing labelled a measurement was invented.
- "GR" is gain reduction. "CV" is the DC control voltage applied to the grids. "THD" is total harmonic
  distortion. The 1965 documents say **dbm** and **cps** and **kc**; I write dBm, Hz and kHz, and where the
  distinction matters I say which document used which. Into 600 Ω, dBm and dBu are the same number. dBFS is
  digital full scale.
- Component designators come from the drawing I name, every time. C-10475 and C-10476 use overlapping
  designators for *different* parts: R39 is a fixed 47 kΩ resistor in the 175-B's sidechain divider on
  C-10475, and on C-10476 the same designator is the 47 kΩ leg of the compression-ratio ladder. I say which
  drawing each one is from.
- This is a spoof target, not a parity goal. What I want is the *feel*: a limiter that is quiet and linear
  until it isn't, a ratio knob that changes two things at once, and the family resemblance to the 1176 I
  already model. Universal Audio ship the real emulation, from their own golden units, and I am not trying to
  beat it [14].

**The two things that make this unit worth the effort, and they are the spine of this document.**

First, it is **variable-mu**, a family `noob-compressorlab` does not have. Every gain element in the lab
reduces gain by shunting signal away through something whose resistance falls: a FET channel, a photocell, a
Blackmer multiplier, a forward-biased diode bridge. The 176 reduces gain by moving a triode's operating point
along its own transconductance-versus-grid-bias curve. Nothing is shunted. The amplifying device *is* the
attenuator, and its gain curve and its distortion curve are the same curve read at two points. That is a
genuinely new shape for the codebase, not a variation on one it already knows.

Second, it is **the direct ancestor of the 1176**, and I can now say precisely what Putnam kept and what he
changed, because I have both schematics. The short version, which section 5.6 proves line by line: he kept
the feedback loop, the fixed-threshold input-driven workflow, the backwards attack and release knobs with
their OFF detent, the "ratio switch changes the sidechain drive *and* the threshold" trick, and — remarkably —
**the actual resistor values in the timing network**. The 176's release pot is 5 MΩ with a 270 kΩ end-stop and
the 1176's release pot is 5 MΩ with a 270 kΩ end-stop; both attack pots are 25 kΩ. What he changed was the
gain element, the timing capacitor and the fixed attack resistor, and those three changes are exactly what
moves the published attack range from 100–1000 µs to 20–800 µs and the release range from 27–527 ms to
50–1100 ms. No other dossier in this repository is positioned to make that comparison, and it is the most
useful thing this one contains.

## 1. What these units are, and the family

### 1.1 The one-paragraph description

A 2U, 19-inch, hinged-front, self-contained tube limiting amplifier with its own power supply, built by
Universal Audio Inc. of Hollywood, California to a Bill Putnam design. Audio enters through a stepped
attenuator and an input transformer, passes through a **6BC8 twin triode running as a balanced variable-gain
stage**, through an interstage transformer into a 12AX7 and 12BH7 push-pull amplifier, and out through a
tapped output transformer and a second stepped attenuator. A **6AL5 twin diode** rectifies a sample of the
output, taken from taps on the output transformer's primary, and the resulting DC is fed back to the 6BC8's
grids. Because the sample is taken *after* the gain element, it is a **feedback** limiter, and its ratio is
not designed in, it emerges from the loop gain (5.4). The 175B does this at a fixed 12:1; the 176 adds a
four-position COMPRESSION RATIO switch offering 2:1, 4:1, 8:1 and 12:1 [1] [3] [12].

against the item's machine-readable OCR transcript [11]:

Two corrections to the survey that sent me here. The survey records C-10476 as titled "SCHEMATIC LIMITING
AMPLIFIER MODEL 175/176" [44]. It is not: **C-10475 is the 175-B and C-10476 is the 176**, two separate
drawings with consecutive numbers by the same draughtsman. It is the *board* drawing that is titled "MODEL 175
AND 176", and that is the more interesting fact anyway (1.2.1).

**What actually differs, from the two drawings side by side.** I diffed them component by component. The
answer is: astonishingly little.

**The 176 at 12:1 is the 175-B.** That is not a rhetorical flourish, it falls out of the two drawings: the
175-B's fixed shunt leg is R39 47 kΩ, and R39 47 kΩ is also the largest leg of the 176's ratio ladder and
carries the same designator. The 176's schematic annotates its DC voltages "at 12 to 1 ratio" precisely
because that is the setting at which it matches the earlier drawing. Universal Audio say the same thing in
their own plug-in manual, from the other direction: *"The 175-B's sharper knee is the same as the 12:1 curve
on the 176"* [12]. **This is the single most useful structural fact for the model: one engine, one extra
switch, and the 175B is the 176 with the switch welded to its top position.**

#### 1.2.1 The 175 and the 175A

The board drawing is titled "MODEL 175 AND 176" [4], not "175-B and 176", which says the plain **175** shared
the board. There is a separate archive.org item, "Universal Audio 175A Schematic" [6], found by enumerating Universal
Audio items through the archive's search API [37], establishing that a **175A** existed between them. I have not read the 175A drawing closely enough to diff it and I am not going
to pretend otherwise; the model will offer 175B and 176 only, and the 175 and 175A are named here so the
family is honest rather than because I can say anything about them.

#### 1.2.2 The 177, and the family's edge

Drawing A-1050 is titled "OUTPUT TRANSFORMER / LIMITING AMPLIFIER / MODELS 176/177" and refers the reader to
"DWG. NO. C-10477 FOR CIRCUIT" [7]. So there was a **Model 177**, it used the same output transformer as the
176, and its schematic is the next drawing number after the 176's. A June 1997 *EQ* studio listing names
"UREI 175B [2], 176 [4], 177, and 1178" in one inventory [34], confirming the 177 was a real product that
survived into the UREI era. I could not reach C-10477 and I do not know what a 177 is. Flagged for whoever
picks this family up next.

### 1.3 Dating: what I can prove, and what I cannot

**I could not find a dated trade-press advertisement or news item naming the 175, 175B or 176.** The
worldradiohistory.com full-text index works and covers the right magazines, but 1960s scans OCR too badly for
phrase search: `"176 limiting amplifier"` returns one hit, a 2010 *EQ* article, and `"175B"` returns nineteen,
all of them either OCR noise or 1980s and 1990s studio equipment lists [38]. The Wayback Machine returned
HTTP 429 to every request from this machine, so one clearly-identified source I know exists — Universal
Audio's own 2004 webzine article on the original, linked by Retro Instruments at
`uaudio.com/webzine/2004/february/text/content4.html` — went unread [17]. Universal Audio's own blog no longer
carries a 175B or 176 history article at all: I grepped their current blog sitemap and there is none [39], and
the 1176 history article that other sources cite has itself gone to a 404 [16]. I am saying all of that rather
than filling the gap.

What I *can* prove, all of it from documents I opened:

1. **The drawings are dated.** C-10475 (175-B) is signed "GEIER 2/1/65" and C-10476 (176) "GEIER 4/12/65" in
   the DRAWN box [2] [3]. On American drafting convention that is 1 February 1965 and 12 April 1965. The
   175-B's date is crisply legible; the 176's final digit is partly clipped by the scan edge and I read it as
   a 5 by shape and by consistency with its neighbour. **This dates the drawings, not the products.**
   Consecutive drawing numbers ten weeks apart, in one hand, on a set that also includes a shared board
   drawing, reads as a documentation exercise for a manual rather than as original design work.
2. **The address on the drawings is pre-ZIP.** All three drawings carry "6000 SUNSET BOULEVARD / HOLLYWOOD
   28, CALIF." [2] [3] [7], a postal zone number. ZIP codes replaced zone numbers on 1 July 1963. That is
   weaker evidence than it looks — pre-printed title-block vellum outlives an address change — but it does
   mean the *stationery* predates mid-1963.
3. **The manual's own typewritten pages carry a ZIP.** "11922 Valerio Street / North Hollywood, California
   91605" [1], and by then Universal Audio is "a division of Studio Electronics Corporation". So the manual
   text is later than the brochure and the drawings, and archive.org dates the item 1965 [1].
4. **The company name changes between the 1968 and 1969 Broadcasting Yearbooks.** 1968 lists "Universal Audio.
   Division of Studio Electronics Corp. 11922 Valerio St. North Hollywood, Calif. 91605" [31]; 1969 lists
   "United Recording Electronics Industries. 11922 Valerio St. North Hollywood, Calif. 91605 … M. T.
   Putnam" [32]. Same street, same phone number, new name. That brackets the UA-to-UREI transition.
5. **The unit stayed in service for decades.** *Mix*, May 1989, lists "Universal Audio 1176, Universal Audio
   175B tube limit[er]" in Right Track Recording's New York inventory [33]; the 1997 *EQ* listing above has
   two 175Bs and four 176s in one room [34].

**Secondary claims, both approximate, both flagged as such.** Universal Audio say "Introduced in the early
1960s" [12] [14]. Sound On Sound say "When they were introduced in around 1960" and "It remained in production
for most of the 1960s, until it was replaced by the 1176" [25]. Neither cites anything. **Estimate for the
dossier's purposes: designed around 1960–1962, documented in the form I have in 1965, superseded by the 1176
from 1967.** The 1967 date for the 1176 is from Wikipedia [35] and is itself uncorroborated here.

**On the discontinuation date I have nothing at all**, and I would rather say that than repeat a number.

### 1.4 Why it is worth modelling

Three reasons, in the order that convinced me.

**It fills the empty family.** The survey's own framing: every gain element the lab owns shunts signal to
ground [44]. The variable-mu tube does not. Building this forces a new gain element, a new detector shape
(a back-biased vacuum-diode peak rectifier with a genuinely large threshold voltage), and a new way of
thinking about where the distortion comes from.

**It completes a Putnam lineage no other single plug-in tells.** The lab already has the 1176 in nine
revisions and the 610 preamp inside the 6176. Adding the 176 gives the tube limiter, the tube preamp and the
FET limiter that replaced them, and — because I have both schematics — lets the plug-in's documentation say
what changed rather than gesture at a resemblance. Universal Audio's own account of the transition is a single
sentence: *"Subsequently, Putnam redesigned the UA 175B/176 tube compressors using FETs, and thus was born the
1176"* [15]. Section 5.6 is that sentence with the resistor values filled in.

**It is small, and it is not the Fairchild.** The Fairchild 670 is being written up in parallel and is also
variable-mu, but it is a two-channel, twenty-tube, mid-side monster with six time-constant presets. The 176 is
one twin triode, one twin diode, two knobs and a four-way switch, and Sound On Sound describe it as sounding
*more* aggressive than an 1176 set to stun [25]. They will not be confused with each other, and section 10
argues about how much code they can honestly share.

Three facts about the knobs are worth stating loudly, because two of them surprise people and all three are
inherited by the 1176.

1. **Attack and release get faster as you turn them clockwise.** The manual says so in one line: "The fastest
   attack or release time is attained by turning the control full clockwise" [1]. The 1176's manual says the
   same thing about the same two knobs [40].
2. **There is no threshold control.** The threshold is fixed by the rectifier's back-bias, and you drive the
   unit into it with the INPUT attenuator. On the 176 the ratio switch moves the threshold as a side effect
   (5.3). Again, exactly the 1176's workflow.
3. **Turning ATTACK fully anticlockwise defeats limiting entirely** and leaves you with a tube line amplifier:
   "By turning the attack control full counter-clockwise, the limiting action may be defeated and the unit
   performs as a normal line amplifier" [1]. The 1176's OFF detent does the same job, and the 1176's manual
   makes the same "colour without compression" point [40].

The manufacturer's setup procedure, verbatim in outline: both attenuators fully anticlockwise, meter to GR,
raise the INPUT until the wanted gain reduction shows, meter to OUTPUT, raise the OUTPUT until the wanted
level shows, meter back to GR. And a limit that says a lot about the intended use: **"limiting should not
exceed 3 to 4 db except where the program material contains extremely loud peaks"** [1]. This is a peak
limiter for programme protection that people later found out was a wonderful compressor, which is the same
story as the 1176 and the LA-2A.

Also from the manual, and worth having in the plug-in's tooltips because it is the only programme-dependent
advice UA gave: for vocal or narration with little low frequency, use fast attack and release; **lengthen the
release as the low-frequency content rises**; **lengthen the attack when the material has tremolo or vibrato
that the limiter might otherwise follow** [1].

### 2.3 The 175B faceplate, and what changes

From the brochure photograph [8]. The layout is **the same panel with one control swapped**: where the 176 has
COMP. RATIO, the 175B has a three-position rotary silkscreened `INPUT` / `G.R.` / `OUTPUT` — the meter
selector. Everything else lines up: the same four jacks bottom-left, the same two large 0–40 attenuator knobs
with the same numerals, the same two VERNIER knobs under the two small top knobs, the same ATTACK and RELEASE
in the same places, the same ON toggle at the far right, the same corner screws and piano hinge.

The silkscreen below the meter reads `UNIVERSAL AUDIO INC.` — diamond logo — `LIMITING AMPLIFIER NO. 175-B`.
Note the **`NO.`** and the **hyphen**: the 175B says "LIMITING AMPLIFIER NO. 175-B" and the 176 says "LIMITING
AMPLIFIER 176". A small thing, and exactly the sort of small thing a faceplate gets wrong.

I cannot state the 175B's panel colour from this photograph. It is a duotone print in cyan and black, so
everything reads as blue-grey and the actual hue is unrecoverable. Universal Audio's own marketing photograph
of the pair racked together shows a **dusty rose or mauve 175B above a black 176** [14], and their plug-in
renders the 175-B in maroon [12]; a colour photograph of a real 175B would be needed to settle it and I did
not find one. **Flagged as unresolved**: if the plug-in offers a 175B faceplate, its colour is a guess.

### 2.4 The meter

From the 176 photograph [29], enlarged 7×:

- **Rectangular VU meter** in a black bezel with a shallow recess, window roughly 0.185 W by 0.36 H.
- **Ivory or cream face**, not white and not black. This is a real difference from the 1176, whose meter face
  is black, and it changes the whole feel of the panel.

The loop closes **after** the gain element and after the whole amplifier, at the output transformer's primary.
Every other feedback compressor in the lab taps its sidechain somewhere in the middle of the amplifier chain;
this one takes the last node before the output attenuator. Sound On Sound make the same reading and say why it
matters: *"Like virtually all compressors of this era, the 176 is a feedback design, whose detector signal is
tapped after the gain-reduction element, and the custom-made output transformer plays a very important part in
this scheme. Its primary coil has four taps for the various ratio settings. This way, a low driving impedance
to the detector helps to maintain fast attack times"* [25].

### 3.2 Input attenuator and T1

The INPUT attenuator is a **ladder attenuator**, 0 to 40 dB in 2 dB detents [1], drawn on C-10476 as a
variable element with a shunt leg, followed by **R1 300 Ω** in series into **T1**'s primary (pins 1 and 5).
It sits **ahead of the input transformer**, which Sound On Sound flag as another point of continuity: *"On the
original 176, as on the later 1176LN, the input attenuator is placed before the input transformer"* [25]. The
1176 does exactly this with its bridged-T attenuator ahead of T1 [40].

The manual is explicit about what this buys you: with 0 to 40 dB available "the 175 B may be utilized at any
point in the system where the input signal level is between −25 dbm and +15 dbm" [1]. It also notes that
replacing the output attenuator with a Langevin Model AT 601 gains 6 dB more output [1], which is the kind of
detail that tells you what a 1965 machine room was like.

**T1's secondary is centre-tapped and the centre tap is grounded.** Pin 7 is one end and pin 6 the other, and
the ground symbol sits on the middle of the winding [3]. This is the whole reason the machine works, and it is
easy to miss: the centre tap makes the drive to the two triode grids **differential**, which lets the control
voltage be applied **common-mode** and cancel (3.4).

### 3.3 The GAIN jumpers

Two jumpers, both labelled GAIN with HI and LO contacts, one per triode section. The chain is
**R2 20 kΩ — R3 6.8 kΩ — R4 6.8 kΩ — R5 20 kΩ** in series between T1's two secondary ends, and the R3/R4
junction is the common centre node. Each jumper selects, for its grid, either the end of the secondary (HI) or
the R2/R3 (respectively R4/R5) junction (LO) [3].

**Derived:** the LO position attenuates by `6800 / (20000 + 6800) = 0.2537`, which is **−11.9 dB**.

That number is worth dwelling on, because the manual contradicts itself about it. The brochure page gives
"GAIN: Jumper in low gain position: 24 db / Jumper in high gain position: 34 db" and "MINIMUM INPUT LEVEL TO
ACCOMPLISH LIMITING: Jumper in low gain position: 12 dbm [sic, −12] / Jumper in high gain position: −24 dbm"
[8]. The typewritten specification page inside the same manual gives the same 24/34 gain but "MINIMUM SINE
WAVE INPUT LEVEL TO ACCOMPLISH LIMITING: Jumper in low gain position: −12 dbm / Jumper in high gain position:
−22 dbm" [9]. And the operating text says the jumpers give "low gain (25 db)" and "high gain (35 db)" [1].

So the same document says the HI/LO step is **10 dB** (from both gain figures and from the 175B page's
−12/−22), **12 dB** (from the brochure's −12/−24), and calls the absolute gain both 24/34 and 25/35 dB. The
resistors say **11.9 dB**. **Derived conclusion, labelled as such: the brochure's 12 dB step is the one the
circuit supports; the 10 dB figures are rounded or mistyped.** I model the step as 11.9 dB and expose the
jumper as a switch, because a plug-in can afford to be right where the manual is inconsistent.

### 3.4 The 6BC8 balanced variable-gain stage

This is the part the rest of the document exists to explain, so it gets read slowly.

**V1 is a 6BC8 twin triode wired as a balanced pair.** Section 1: grid pin 7, cathode pin 8, plate pin 6.
Section 2: grid pin 2, cathode pin 3, plate pin 1. The boxed no-signal DC voltages are **90 V on both plates**
and **1.85 V on both cathodes** [3]. That is a low-plate-voltage, low-current operating point for a tube whose
datasheet class-A condition is 150 V and 10 mA [18] [19].

**Audio path.** T1's centre-tapped secondary drives the two grids in antiphase, through the R2/R3 and R4/R5
dividers. The two plates go through **R12 4.7 kΩ** and **R14 4.7 kΩ** into the ends of **T2**'s push-pull
primary, with **R13 1 kΩ PLATE BALANCE** as a potentiometer between them and its wiper returning to the supply.
The two cathodes are joined through **R7 47 Ω — R6 100 Ω (CATHODE BALANCE) — R8 47 Ω**, and a second string
**R9 330 Ω — R10 1 kΩ (GR ZERO) — R11 330 Ω** sits across the same region [3].

**Control path.** The control voltage arrives at the **R3/R4 junction**, the electrical centre of the grid
network, so it appears **equally on both grids** while the audio appears **oppositely** on them. The CV bus is
bypassed to ground by **C5 0.1 µF** [3].

**Why that arrangement, and why it is the whole trick.** A variable-mu stage reduces gain by driving the
control grid more negative, which lowers transconductance. But moving the grid also moves the plate current
and therefore the plate voltage, and a plate-voltage step is a thump. In a balanced pair with the control
applied common-mode, both plates move the *same* way, so the step is a common-mode signal at T2's primary and
the transformer rejects it, while the audio, being differential, passes. The residual thump is whatever the
two triode halves fail to match, and that is exactly what PLATE BALANCE and CATHODE BALANCE are for.

The manual's balancing procedure confirms this reading better than any description could. It tells you to
remove the input signal, set the meter to OUTPUT, inject the internal test signal, and *"Adjust first the
PLATE BALANCE and then the CATHODE BALANCE controls for minimum meter deflection"*, iterating; and it warns
that *"If the two sections of the 6BC8 are not reasonably symmetrical, optimum balancing cannot be
obtained"*, with a target of *"in the order of −20 db"* [1]. Balancing is a null of the control feedthrough,
and its quality is limited by tube matching. **That is a modelling parameter: the plug-in should have a
mismatch control, because the residual thump is part of the sound and a perfectly balanced model will be too
clean.**

The same procedure contains the single most characterful line in the whole manual, and it is a real circuit
fact about a real failure mode:

> Before proceeding with balancing, check for GASSY condition of the 6BC8. Excessive gas in the envelope of
> this tube can cause serious distortion. … Set the meter switch to GR position. Rotate the RELEASE
> potentiometer to the full clockwise position, and then (while watching the meter) rotate it full
> counter-clockwise. An increase in the reading of more than 1/4 db indicates excessive gas and the 6BC8
> should be discarded. [1]

That works because a gassy tube leaks grid current, and grid current through the **5.27 MΩ** release path
develops a DC offset that changes with the pot setting. A quarter of a decibel of GR change as you sweep the
release knob means about a nanoamp of grid current. It is a beautiful piece of field diagnosis, and it is also
a hint about what an aged unit sounds like: **a slightly gassy 6BC8 sits with a standing gain reduction that
drifts with the release setting**, which is a lovely thing for a "worn" mode to reproduce.

### 3.5 The three balance trims, and what each one is for

| Trim | Value | Where | What it nulls |
|---|---|---|---|
| PLATE BALANCE | R13 1 kΩ | Between the two plate loads R12 and R14 | Mismatch in the two sections' plate resistance and load, i.e. the first-order control feedthrough into T2 |
| CATHODE BALANCE | R6 100 Ω | Between the two cathodes, flanked by R7 and R8 47 Ω | Mismatch in the two sections' bias, i.e. the residual after PLATE BALANCE |
| GR ZERO | R10 1 kΩ | In the R9/R11 330 Ω string across the cathode circuit | The meter's zero in GR position, and with it the tube's resting operating point |

GR ZERO is the 176's equivalent of the 1176's **Q-bias** trimmer. The 1176's factory procedure is to apply a
sine and turn Q-bias until the output falls by exactly 1.0 dB, which "places your gain reduction FET Q1
slightly into conduction" [40] ([[1176]] section 3.6). Here the same idea is executed in the cathode circuit
instead of the gate circuit: setting the resting cathode-to-grid voltage sets where on the 6BC8's
transconductance curve the tube idles, and therefore how many decibels the first volt of control voltage
buys. Both units park the gain element just inside its steep region so a small overshoot produces a large
gain change. **Same design intent, different electrode.**

### 3.6 T2, the 12AX7 and the 12BH7

**T2** is the interstage transformer, push-pull, primary from V1's plates (leads P and B on the drawing), and
its secondary drives V2's grids with terminals marked G, F, F, G [3]. Universal Audio's plug-in offers an
"Interstage Transformer Bypass" on Retro Instruments' hardware clone [26], which tells you this transformer
is audible enough that a clone thought bypassing it was a feature.

**V2 is a 12AX7 push-pull voltage amplifier** [49]. Plates at **80 V** boxed, cathodes at **2.1 V**, plate loads
R18 and R21 **180 kΩ**, cathode resistors R19 and R20 **2.2 kΩ**, and R15/R17 **20 kΩ** with R16 **2.2 kΩ**
forming the grid return network with a **1.4 V** node. C1 and C2 **0.47 µF** shunt the plate loads; C3 and C4
**0.047 µF** couple to the next stage [3].

**V3 is a 12BH7 push-pull output stage** [50], plates at **295 V**, driven through R22 and R25 **180 kΩ** with grid
returns R23 and R24 **220 kΩ**, and R27 and R29 **1.2 MΩ** with R28 **330 Ω** setting a **275 V** node and an

**Estimate:** the whole chain has 38 dB of gain with both attenuators at 0, which I derive in 6.2 from the
slope and intercept of the published compression-ratio curves, not from the specification. The specification

## 4. The variable-mu gain element itself

### 4.1 What variable-mu means, and what it is not

A **variable-mu** stage is an amplifier whose *own* gain is the control. There is no separate attenuator: the
tube that amplifies the signal is biased along its transconductance-versus-grid-voltage curve, and pushing the
grid more negative makes the same tube amplify less. Three consequences follow immediately, and all three
matter more than the name does.

1. **Nothing is shunted, so the source impedance the signal sees does not change with gain reduction.** In the
   1176, gain reduction happens because a FET's channel resistance falls and drags the divider down; in the
   LA-2A, because a photocell's resistance falls. Both change the impedance at a node. Here the divider does
   not exist. The stage's output impedance does move a little, because plate resistance rises as the tube is
   biased back, but that is a second-order effect on a transformer-coupled stage, not the mechanism.
2. **The gain curve and the distortion curve are the same curve.** The signal on the grid moves the operating
   point *within each cycle*, so the instantaneous gain varies over the waveform. That is not a side effect of
   gain reduction, it is what gain reduction is made of. Any model that computes a smooth gain and multiplies
   by it has thrown away the mechanism.
3. **The control is a voltage on a grid that draws essentially no current**, so the sidechain sees a very high
   impedance and the timing network is free to use megohm-scale resistors. The 176 uses a 5 MΩ release pot.
   The 1176's FET gate also draws no current and also uses a 5 MΩ release pot, which is not a coincidence
   (5.6).

"Variable-mu" is the trade name the audio industry settled on; the tube-design literature calls the family
**remote cutoff**, and the *Radiotron Designer's Handbook* treats it as a grid-structure technique for
extending the range over which a valve stays usefully amplifying rather than as a distinct kind of device
[47]. Sound On Sound use the audio industry's word and list the 175 and 176 in the family alongside the Altec
436, the RCA BA-6A and the Gates Sta-Level [48].

The name is misleading in one respect, but not the one I first thought, and the correction matters enough to
show its working (4.3.1). **Both µ and gm fall**, and they fall at very different rates: across the working
range gm falls by about a factor of a hundred while µ falls by about a factor of three [20]. Since
`r_p = µ/gm`, the plate resistance therefore **rises** by roughly a factor of thirty, and the stage gain of a
triode into a load `R_L`,

```
A = µ · R_L / (r_p + R_L)
```

is not proportional to gm across the whole range: it approaches `gm · R_L` only once `r_p` has grown large
compared with `R_L`. Modelling this stage as a gain proportional to transconductance overstates the available
gain reduction by several decibels (4.4). "Variable-mu" is the trade name the audio industry settled on;
"remote-cutoff" is the tube industry's description, and it is the more accurate one for both this tube and the
Fairchild's.

### 4.3 The transconductance law, read off the curve

Tung-Sol plate #6282 [19] plots gm against grid #1 volts for E_b = 100, 150 and 200 V. Reading the E_b = 150 V
curve, with the two tabulated points as anchors:

| Grid volts | gm (µmho) | Relative to 6200 µmho | Provenance |
|---|---|---|---|
| −2.2 | **6200** | 0.0 dB | **tabulated**, at E_p 150 V and R_k 220 Ω [18] [19] [20] |
| −3 | ~4800 | −2.2 dB | **estimate**, read off plate #6282 |
| −5 | ~1400 | −12.9 dB | **estimate**, read off plate #6282 |
| −6.5 | ~500 | −21.9 dB | **estimate**, read off plate #6282 |
| −7.5 | ~200 | −29.8 dB | **estimate**, read off plate #6282 |
| −13 | **50** | **−41.9 dB** | **tabulated** [18] [19] [20] |

Reading precision off a 1961 plate reproduced as a scanned PDF is not better than about ±20 % in gm, which is
±1.6 dB, and I have labelled every graph-derived row an estimate accordingly.

**The shape is the important part, and it is not what a textbook remote-cutoff device does.** A true
remote-cutoff pentode is designed so that gm falls roughly exponentially with grid voltage, giving a nearly
constant number of decibels per volt, which is why such tubes make clean logarithmic gain controls. The 6BC8
does not do that. Its decibels-per-volt **increases** as it is biased back:

| Interval | Δ gain | Δ grid | dB per volt |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to −2.2 V | ~−2 dB | 2.2 V | ~1.0 |
| −2.2 to −5 V | −12.9 dB | 2.8 V | **4.6** |
| −5 to −7.5 V | −16.9 dB | 2.5 V | **6.8** |
| −7.5 to −13 V | −12.1 dB | 5.5 V | 2.2 |

Shallow near zero bias, steepest around −5 to −7.5 V, then a long shallow tail as the tube goes properly off.
**Derived, and it is the single most consequential fact for the sound of this model:** the gain law is
*expansive in the control domain*, so the deeper the gain reduction, the more decibels each extra volt of
control buys, and therefore the higher the loop gain and the higher the effective ratio.

A convenient closed form that fits the useful part of the curve. Taking `v` as the total negative grid voltage
in volts and fitting the two anchors at −2.2 V and −5 V:

```
G_dB(v) = −8.686 · (v / V0)^n ,   V0 = 4.16 V,  n = 2.16          (derived, valid 0 ≤ v ≤ 8 V)
```

**Quote this exponent as a range, not as three significant figures.** It is fitted to two interior points on
*one* manufacturer's plate. Sylvania's own curve for the same tube gives **n between 1.6 and 2.0** over the
readable range, and my law runs 20 % to 60 % high against it (4.6.1). **The defensible statement is
`n ≈ 1.6 to 2.2`**, and 4.6.1 explains why nobody can do better from published curves: both makers plot gm on
linear axes that cannot resolve the region below about 200 µmho.

Checked against a point not used in the fit: at v = 7.5 V this gives −31.0 dB against the −29.8 dB read off
the plate, a 1.2 dB error, inside the reading tolerance. **It fails badly beyond about −8 V** (it predicts
−101 dB at −13 V against the tabulated −41.9 dB), because the real curve turns into a shallow tail there. For
the model, clamp the law at a maximum gain reduction and do not extrapolate; the tail is a region where the
tube is off and nothing musical is happening anyway.

#### 4.3.1 The amplification factor, and a correction I had to make

**My first reading of this was wrong and I am leaving the correction visible**, because the way it went wrong
is a trap worth naming. I originally wrote that the 6BC8's µ was comparatively flat, on the strength of three
datasheets tabulating it as 33 to 35. That is a single tabulated value at a single operating point, and a
tabulated value can say nothing whatever about how a quantity behaves as you bias the tube away from that
point. Only a curve can. The Fairchild researcher, comparing the two tubes for section 10, pointed this out
and directed me at the right plate; I then measured it myself rather than take the reading second-hand.

**The curve is Sylvania page 4**, "AVERAGE TRANSFER CHARACTERISTICS", which plots gm *and* µ against grid
voltage on a shared axis for E_b = 100, 150 and 200 V [20]. Saved as
`ref/ua176-6bc8-sylvania-p4-mu-gm.png`. The µ scale is the trap within the trap: its ticks are printed in a
legend column beside the gm labels, and **µ = 0 aligns with gm = 6000 µmho, µ = 25 with 7000 and µ = 50 with
8000**, so `µ = (gm_height − 6000) / 40`. Read with the wrong origin the curve looks much flatter than it is.

Measured against that calibration, at **E_b = 150 V**, the condition the tabulated data uses:

| Grid volts | µ | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| −0.5 | ~39 | **estimate**, read off Sylvania page 4 [20] |
| −2.2 | **~33** | **estimate**; the datasheets tabulate **35** at this point [18] [19] [20] |
| −2.6 | ~32 | **estimate** |
| −5.4 | ~19 | **estimate** |
| −10.1 | ~12 | **estimate** |

**Two independent checks that the calibration is right.** The reading at −2.2 V lands on 33 against a
tabulated 35, inside my ±2 reading error. And, more convincingly, `r_p = µ/gm` must hold identically: at
−2.2 V that is 33 / 6200 µmho = **5320 Ω**, against the **5300 Ω** the same datasheets tabulate [18] [19].
A calibration error of any size would break that closure, and it does not.

So **µ falls by a factor of about 2.8 across the working range**, roughly 8.8 dB over 7.8 V, or about
**1.1 dB per volt**. Not flat.

**What that does to plate resistance.** With gm falling about a hundredfold and µ about threefold,
`r_p = µ/gm` rises from **5.3 kΩ at idle to of order 100 kΩ** at deep gain reduction — **an estimate**, since
it depends on reading gm out in the tail where the curve is shallow. That is a thirty-fold change in the
impedance this stage presents to the interstage transformer, and it is why 4.4's gain table has to be computed
from `µ·R_L/(r_p + R_L)` rather than from gm alone.

### 4.4 How gain reduction actually happens in this circuit

Put the law together with the circuit. The cathodes idle at **1.85 V** and the grids sit at 0 V DC through
their return network, so the quiescent grid-to-cathode voltage is **−1.85 V** [3]. That is just below the
tabulated −2.2 V operating point, i.e. Putnam idles the tube at slightly *more* transconductance than the
datasheet's nominal, in the shallow part of the curve. GR ZERO (R10 1 kΩ, in the cathode string) trims exactly
this point [3], which is why it is the direct analogue of the 1176's Q-bias trimmer (3.5).

Writing `u` for the extra negative grid volts the sidechain adds beyond idle, and using the law above:

| `u` (V) | Total grid volts | Gain reduction (dB) | Local slope (dB/V) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | −1.85 | 0.0 | — |
| 1 | −2.85 | **2.3** | 2.3 |
| 2 | −3.85 | **5.8** | 3.5 |
| 3 | −4.85 | **10.6** | 4.8 |
| 4 | −5.85 | **16.6** | 6.0 |
| 5 | −6.85 | **24.0** | 7.4 |
| 6 | −7.85 | **32.7** | 8.7 |

All **derived** from 4.3, and **this column is the transconductance ratio, not yet the stage gain**. Each
successive volt buys more than the last, which is the property that matters most for how the loop behaves
(5.4). That is unlike the lab's other elements: the photocell's law is roughly logarithmic in light, the FET's
is roughly linear in decibels per volt over its working range, and this one accelerates.

**The stage gain is smaller than the transconductance ratio, because µ falls too** (4.3.1). Using
`A = µ·R_L/(r_p + R_L)` with `r_p = µ/gm`, and taking `R_L = 5 kΩ` as a stand-in for the plate load, the same
six volts of control gives:

| | idle (−1.85 V) | deep (−7.85 V) | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| gm | 6200 µmho | ~110 µmho | **−35.0 dB** |
| µ | ~34 | ~12 | −9.0 dB |
| r_p = µ/gm | 5.3 kΩ | ~109 kΩ | ×20 |
| **stage gain** `µ·R_L/(r_p+R_L)` | 16.0 | 0.53 | **−29.7 dB** |

So the falling amplification factor **costs about 5 dB of available gain reduction** relative to a model that
treats stage gain as proportional to transconductance. **All of this is an estimate**, and the weakest link is
`R_L`: the plate load is R12 4.7 kΩ in series with T2's reflected primary impedance, and nobody publishes a
measurement of T2 (6.3), so the 5 kΩ is a placeholder chosen to be the right order. **The 5 dB is the size of
the error, not a calibrated figure**, and it is recorded here so that a later measurement of T2 can sharpen it
rather than discover it.

**The balanced arrangement means this happens to both halves at once** (3.4). The audio is differential and
the control common-mode, so the gain of the *stage* follows the law while the DC shift at the plates is
rejected by T2 to the extent that the two halves match.

### 4.5 Distortion, which is the same curve read differently

The signal on each grid moves the operating point within the cycle. On the positive half-cycle of one grid the
tube is momentarily more conductive, on the negative half less, and gm is a curved function of grid voltage, so
each half produces a distorted current. Two things then happen.

**Even harmonics cancel; odd harmonics do not.** The two halves are driven in antiphase into a push-pull
primary, so the even-order products, which are in phase in the two halves, subtract at T2 and the odd-order
products, which are in antiphase, add. **The residue of a well-balanced 6BC8 stage is odd-order, dominantly
third.** The quality of that cancellation is limited by how well the two triode halves match, which is exactly
what the manual's balancing procedure and its warning about asymmetric sections are about [1]. **A perfectly
matched model will be too clean, and a mismatch parameter is not a garnish, it is the second-harmonic
generator.**

**Distortion rises with gain reduction, because the curvature does.** From 4.3, the local slope goes from
2.3 dB/V at 1 V of control to 8.7 dB/V at 6 V. Curvature scales similarly. So the deeper the limiting, the
more the tube's law bends inside each cycle, and the more third harmonic appears. This is the same qualitative
statement the [[Neve-33609]] dossier makes about the diode bridge [42] ("the bridge is the attenuator *and* the
nonlinearity, so harmonic content rises with gain reduction") and it is *not* true of the lab's optical
models, whose photocell distortion is largely a function of cell resistance rather than of instantaneous
signal. The variable-mu family and the diode-bridge family share this property and the optical family does
not.

**Universal Audio's specification claims this is small**: "negligible increase in distortion at levels above"
the limiting threshold, and "+24 dbm with less than 1% THD from 50 cps to 15 kc" measured with limiting
switched off [8] [9]. Those two statements together are the manufacturer saying the amplifier is clean and the
limiter does not spoil it, at the levels and in the era they cared about. **No source I found publishes a THD
figure with a stated amount of gain reduction**, which is a real gap, and section 11 says so rather than
inventing a bound.

**Compared with the 1176's FET.** The 1176's junction FET is a single-ended shunt element and its natural
residue is second harmonic; UA cancel it with a network that feeds exactly half the drain signal to the gate,
leaving a much smaller third-order term [40] ([[1176]] section 3.3). So both units end up third-order dominant
when they are working properly, but they get there differently, and they fail differently:

- The 1176's cancellation is a **resistor network referred to a DC bias point**. Disturb that bias point — for
  example by pressing all four ratio buttons — and the cancellation stops working and second harmonic returns
  in quantity [40].
- The 176's cancellation is **structural push-pull symmetry**. Disturb the symmetry — a drifting or gassy tube,
  a mistrimmed PLATE BALANCE — and second harmonic returns, along with a control-voltage thump.

That is a genuinely different failure mode, it is audible, and it is one of the things that should make the two
models distinguishable in a blind comparison (section 11.4).

### 4.6 Why this is not the Fairchild's tube, and why that matters here

The Fairchild 670 uses **6386** remote-cutoff twin triodes; the 176 uses a **6BC8** semi-remote-cutoff twin
triode from a television tuner family. Both are "variable-mu twin triodes", and that shared phrase is exactly
the kind of thing that produces one abstraction with several different circuits underneath it, which an audit
of this codebase already found and rejected once. So the question for section 10 is not whether both tubes
carry the same adjective. It is whether their published curves are **the same functional shape with different
constants**. I fetched the 6386 datasheet and did the comparison [54].

**General Electric ET-T1113, page 1, dated 8-54** [54], is unambiguous about design intent in a way the 6BC8's
sheets are not. The heading reads "FOR REMOTE-CUTOFF CASCODE-AMPLIFIER APPLICATIONS / REMOTE-CUTOFF
CHARACTERISTIC / 9-PIN MINIATURE / MEDIUM-MU", and the description opens:

> The 6386 is a miniature medium-mu twin triode in which each section exhibits a remote-cutoff characteristic.
> It is designed primarily for use as a cascode radio-frequency amplifier, intermediate-frequency amplifier,
> or mixer **in circuits to which it is desired to apply automatic-gain-control**.

**That last clause is the real difference between the two tubes, and it is a difference of intent, not of
physics.** The 6386 was built to have its gain controlled. The 6BC8 is a television tuner tube that happens to
have a stretched taper, which Putnam noticed and used. Same family, opposite provenance.

**The tabulated figures, side by side.** The 6386's are from GE page 2 [54]; the 6BC8's from section 4.2.

| | **6BC8** [18] [19] [20] | **6386** [54] |
|---|---|---|
| Datasheet wording | "semi-remote cutoff" | "**remote-cutoff**" |
| Operating point | E_p 150 V, R_k 220 Ω, I_p 10 mA, so V_g ≈ −2.2 V | E_p 100 V, R_k 200 Ω, I_p 9.6 mA, so V_g ≈ −1.92 V |
| µ | 33–35 | **17** |
| gm | 6200 µmho | **4000 µmho** |
| r_p | 5300 Ω | **4250 Ω** |
| Small-gm endpoint | **50 µmho at −13 V** | **100 µmho at −16 V** |
| `r_p = µ/gm` closes? | 33/6200 µmho = 5320 Ω against 5300 tabulated | 17/4000 µmho = **4250 Ω exactly** |

Both datasheets are internally consistent to the point where `r_p = µ/gm` reproduces the tabulated plate
resistance, the 6386's exactly. That matters for section 10: it means µ, gm and r_p are a coupled triple that
any shared component can carry as derivatives of one law rather than as three independent tables.

**The tapers, and a correction to the correction.** The Fairchild researcher rightly pointed out that the two
small-gm endpoints are not comparable as printed, since GE quote 100 µmho and the 6BC8's makers quote 50, six
decibels further down a still-falling curve. That is a real objection. Their arithmetic for the size of it is
not right, though, and I am recording my own:

| Basis | 6386 | 6BC8 | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| As printed | 32.0 dB over 14.1 V = **2.28 dB/V** | 41.9 dB over 10.8 V = **3.88 dB/V** | 1.70 |
| Both normalised to a 100 µmho endpoint | 2.28 dB/V | 35.9 dB over ≈8.1 V = **4.43 dB/V** | 1.95 |
| Both normalised to a 50 µmho endpoint | 38.1 dB over ≈17.1 V = **2.23 dB/V** | 3.88 dB/V | 1.74 |

The 6BC8 reaches 100 µmho at a grid voltage **less** negative than the −13 V at which it reaches 50, because
the curve falls monotonically; extrapolating in the other direction is what produced the reported ratio of
1.28. Taking the local tail slope of about 2.2 dB/V from 4.3, its 100 µmho point sits near −10.3 V. **So the
6BC8's taper is roughly 1.7 to 1.95 times steeper than the 6386's, on any consistent basis, not 1.28.**

**And the amplification factors fall at similar rates.** From 4.3.1 the 6BC8's µ falls about 1.1 dB per volt.
The Fairchild researcher measures the 6386's at about 1.45 dB per volt, composed from GE's µ-versus-plate-
current and plate-current-versus-grid plots. **I have not verified that figure myself** — it needs two eyeball
readings composed, and I only checked their gm figures — so it is reported as theirs, not confirmed as mine.

**The conclusion, and I reversed it once before arriving at it.** On the average-taper figures above I
concluded "same functional shape, different constants", because a taper ratio of 1.8 is a constant and
absorbing constants is what a per-type parameter set is for. **That conclusion was wrong, and the error was
one of method rather than arithmetic: average taper is a first-moment statistic and is blind to curvature,
which is exactly what distinguishes these two laws.** 4.6.1 measures the curvature directly, on a logarithmic
plot for the 6386 and against a second manufacturer's curve for the 6BC8, and the answer is:

- **6386: an ordinary exponential, `n ≈ 1.0`**, constant decibels per volt over three readable decades.
- **6BC8: `n ≈ 1.6 to 2.2`**, an accelerating slope, though poorly constrained.

**Different shape, not just different constants.** What the two tubes *do* share is the coupled behaviour of
µ, gm and r_p — both fall, both close on `r_p = µ/gm`, and neither has a flat µ. Section 10 takes the shape
difference as the finding and treats it as a reason for per-type fits rather than as a reason against the part.

#### 4.6.1 The exponent, and why it cannot carry the comparison

**This subsection has been rewritten three times. I am leaving the trail visible, because every wrong turn was
instructive and two of the three were mine.** The destination — that published data cannot settle whether
these two tubes share a shape — is where I started, but every reason I first gave for it was wrong, and the
reason that survives was found by measurement rather than by argument.

I originally argued that the two tubes might differ in *functional form* rather than in constants, on the
grounds that my 6BC8 fit needed `n = 2.16` in `gm(w) = gm₀·exp(−(w/V0)^n)` while a true remote-cutoff tube
ought to sit near `n = 1`. **That was an assumption about what such a tube ought to do, not a reading.** Then
I wrote that the question was unanswerable because GE publish only two transconductance points for the 6386
and their plate-current curves are on a linear 0 to 40 mA scale that cannot be differentiated at the deep end.
**That was simply false, and the error was mine: I looked at page 4 and never opened page 3.** Then, having
found page 3, I briefly held that the shapes differ and were measured. That was built on one curve from each
tube, and it did not survive either.

**GE page 3 plots the 6386's transconductance on a logarithmic axis**, from 10 to 10 000 µmho against grid
voltage from 0 to −60, for the cascode connection at E_b = 250 V with E_cc2 as parameter [54]. On a
logarithmic axis a pure exponential is a straight line, so the shape question is answerable by inspection.
Saved as `ref/ua176-6386-gm-log-plot.png`.

**The curves are straight.** Reading decade crossings off the E_cc2 = 300 V curve on a calibrated crop:

| Decade | Volts spanned |
|---|---|
| 10 → 100 µmho | **20.3 V** |
| 100 → 1000 µmho | **18.6 V** |

Constant volts per decade *is* an exponential. Treating the decay rate as proportional to `w^(n−1)` gives
**n = 1.10** from my own reading of that one curve. The Fairchild researcher, fitting the same trace over more
crossings, gets **n = 1.00 at 0.37 dB rms**, and forcing 2.16 onto it costs 2.88 dB, ten times worse [55]. Two
independent readings of the same curve agree.

**But one curve is not the tube, and this is where the finding turns.** That plot carries **four** curves, one
per divider-supply voltage. Fitted separately they give [55]:

| E_cc2 | `n` | rms |
|---|---|---|
| 300 V | **1.00** | 0.37 dB |
| 250 V | **0.84** | 0.33 dB |
| 200 V | **0.71** | 0.31 dB |
| 150 V | **0.59** | 0.44 dB |

**The exponent varies by a factor of 1.7 across four operating conditions of one tube on one plot**, moving
monotonically with the supply, with every fit good to under half a decibel. I did not reproduce this myself:
my own calibration of that plot was solid enough for the decade crossings of a single trace and not for
telling four converging curves apart in the bunched upper region, which is where the Fairchild researcher says
the error lives, and I am reporting their measurement as theirs rather than claiming a check I did not run.

**Three facts about that plot that I had wrong or had not known, all from [55] and worth recording** because
they resolve apparent contradictions rather than papering over them:

- **`E_cc2` is not a screen voltage.** The 6386 is a triode and has no screen. GE's page 2 names it the
  "Voltage-Divider Supply Voltage", the rail feeding the 470 kΩ pair that biases the upper grounded-grid
  section [54].
- **The tabulated −16 V and the curve do not contradict each other.** The tabulated 100 µmho at −16 V is
  Class A1, *single section*, plate at 100 V; the curves are the *cascode* at E_b = 250 V. The lowest divider
  supply puts its 100 µmho crossing at −14.6 V, which closes to within 1.4 V of the tabulated figure and in
  the right direction, since it is the condition with the lowest plate voltage. A reading with a wrong axis
  origin could not land there.
- **The curves are a cascode connection**, so the lower section's plate floats at the upper section's cathode
  rather than sitting at a working voltage. **They are therefore not the law of a grounded-cathode stage**,
  which is what both the 176 and the 670 actually run. The 6BC8 curves I read are single-section transfer
  characteristics at a fixed plate voltage, so **the two tubes' curves are not measured on the same footing
  either**, and that is a further reason the comparison cannot be made cleanly.

**Now the check that was asked of my own number, and it is the part I would rather not report.** My `n = 2.16`
was fitted to two interior points on **Tung-Sol's** plate #6282 [19]. Sylvania publish their own gm curve for
the same tube on page 4 [20]. Reading it at E_b = 150 V over the range where a linear axis can be read:

| Grid volts | Sylvania gm | my fitted law | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| −3 | ~4030 µmho | 4870 | **1.21** |
| −4 | ~2370 µmho | 3185 | **1.34** |
| −5 | ~1200 µmho | 1800 | **1.50** |
| −6 | ~557 µmho | 879 | **1.58** |

**My law runs 20 % to 60 % high against a second manufacturer's curve for the same tube, and the error grows
with bias.** Fitting the exponent to Sylvania's own readings instead gives **n = 1.98 between −3 and −5 V and
n = 1.58 between −4 and −6 V**. So the honest figure for the 6BC8 is a range, **n ≈ 1.6 to 2.2**, not 2.16 to
three significant figures. Two makers' "average characteristics" for one tube type differ by up to 60 % in the
middle of the control range, and I quoted one of them as though it were the tube.

**The plate-resistance closure, applied as asked, and what it is actually good for.** Sylvania page 5 plots
**plate resistance against grid voltage** [20], and this turns out to be the most useful curve in either
datasheet for a reason worth recording: `r_p = µ/gm` grows without limit as the tube cuts off, so **it is
readable exactly where gm is not**. A linear gm axis puts 50 µmho eleven pixels off the baseline; the same
operating point sits at 180 kΩ, high on the r_p scale.

| Grid volts | Sylvania r_p, E_b = 150 | µ from 4.3.1 | implied gm = µ/r_p |
|---|---|---|---|
| −7 | ~69 kΩ | ~14.5 | 212 µmho |
| −8 | ~105 kΩ | ~13 | 124 µmho |
| −9 | ~145 kΩ | ~12.5 | 86 µmho |
| −10 | ~180 kΩ | ~12 | 67 µmho |

All **estimates**. My fitted law gives 368, 131, 40 and 10 µmho at those four points: right at −8 V, then
falling far too fast, six-fold low by −10 V. **The deep end of my law is not supported.** But the implied gm
values are themselves the quotient of two eyeballed curves, so their decay rate is too noisy to fit an
exponent to — I tried, and it returns nonsense. What they establish is a **direction**, not a number: the real
curve flattens into its tail sooner than a single power law predicts, which is exactly what the tabulated
50 µmho at −13 V also says and what my law misses by extrapolating.

**Where this leaves the comparison, and it is not where I expected.**

| | exponent | why it moves |
|---|---|---|
| **6386** | **0.59 to 1.00** | Varies with **operating condition**: four curves on one GE plot, monotonic in divider supply [55] |
| **6BC8** | **1.6 to 2.2** | Varies with **anchor choice and with maker**: Tung-Sol's curve gives 2.16, Sylvania's 1.6 to 2.0, and the two disagree by up to 60 % where both can be read |

**The exponent is not a property of a tube. It is a property of a tube, a plot, an operating condition and a
fitting procedure**, and it moves by a factor of 1.7 within one tube on one page before any comparison between
two tubes begins. **So published data cannot settle whether these two tubes share a shape**, and the
comparison I originally proposed was never one the documents could support — not because the curves are
missing, but because the quantity is not stable enough to carry it. Both researchers now hold that position;
the Fairchild researcher has withdrawn the single figure they first sent me, and I have withdrawn the
"different shape, measured" claim I briefly held on the strength of one curve each.

**What the documents do support, stated as weakly as it deserves.** The 6386 sits **at or below a pure
exponential in every condition its maker plots**, and forcing 2.16 onto it costs seven to twelve times the
free fit's residual across the four curves [55]. So the 6386 is not a 2.16 tube in any condition GE publish.
That is much weaker than "the exponents differ", and it does not restore my original argument, which reached
a defensible destination by an indefensible route.

**Anyone quoting 2.16 from this document should quote the range instead**, and 9.4's constants table has been
changed to say so.

#### 4.6.2 A stated validity range for the fitted law

The Fairchild researcher asked for this so that the two dossiers could carry documented validity ranges beside
each other rather than two laws with none. It is the right thing to ask for and it should be part of the
fitting procedure of 10.3. Errors are `20·log₁₀(my law / the source)`:

| Grid volts | vs Tung-Sol gm [19] | vs Sylvania gm [20] | vs Sylvania r_p [20] |
|---|---|---|---|
| −3 | **+0.1 dB** | +1.6 dB | — |
| −4 | — | +2.6 dB | — |
| −5 | **+2.2 dB** | +3.5 dB | — |
| −6 | — | +4.0 dB | — |
| −6.5 | **+1.3 dB** | — | — |
| −7 | — | — | **+4.9 dB** |
| −7.5 | **+1.0 dB** | — | — |
| −8 | — | — | **+0.5 dB** |
| −9 | — | — | **−6.7 dB** |
| −10 | — | — | **−16.2 dB** |

**Validity range: 0 to −8 V of grid, which is about 30 dB of gain reduction.** Inside it the law tracks the
curve it was fitted to within ±2.5 dB and the second maker's within +4 dB. It **cannot** be better than that,
because the two makers' own curves differ by 1.3 to 1.5 dB where both are readable: **a fitted law cannot be
more accurate than its sources are consistent**, and quoting a tighter figure would be quoting my own
arithmetic rather than the tube.

**That principle needs a second clause, and it came from applying it to the other tube.** The 6386 has
**exactly one datasheet**: General Electric ET-T1113, in volume 142 of Frank Philipse's archive, with no
second manufacturer's sheet under 6386, 6386A or 6386LGP [55]. So no floor can be computed for it at all. The
full statement is therefore: **a validity range is bounded below by the spread between independent sources
where two exist, and must be declared unbounded where only one does.** The second half is the uncomfortable
half, and it applies to the Fairchild rather than to this unit.

**Which produces an asymmetry between the two dossiers that should not be read the wrong way round.** This
law has the *narrower* usable range, 0 to −8 V, and the *better* foundation, because two manufacturers'
curves constrain it and disagree by a known amount. The Fairchild's has the wider range and a single source
behind it. **The wider range is not the stronger result**, and the Fairchild researcher put that on the record
themselves rather than letting the comparison flatter their own file.

**Outside it the law is wrong in a specific and predictable way: it cuts the tube off too early.** By −10 V it
is 16 dB low against the only published curve that constrains that region. **This is the same failure, in the
same direction, that the Fairchild researcher found in Raffensperger's 6386 current law**, which is 8.6 dB low
at −50 V and 35.7 dB low at −70 V [55]. Two independent fits, two different tubes, two different functional
forms, and both cut off early — because both were fitted to the mid-range where linear-axis plots are readable
and then extrapolated into a region those plots could not show. **A remote-cutoff tube's whole purpose is the
long shallow tail, and it is exactly the part that fitting to readable data throws away.** Any tube law in
this family should be validated against a plot that resolves deep cutoff on its own terms — a logarithmic
axis, a plate-resistance curve, or constant-parameter plate characteristics — before it is trusted anywhere
near the region a limiter actually works in. All three give the deep end its own resolution instead of
crushing it against a baseline.

**Which of the three to reach for, since I first got the ordering wrong.** I told the Fairchild researcher
their constant-grid-voltage plate characteristics were simply the better technique. They corrected me, and the
correction is the useful form: **directness where available, generality where not.** Constant-parameter
curves are the most direct evidence, because each condition gets its own line rather than a quotient of two
estimates — but a maker only supplies them if they chose to plot down into cutoff, and Sylvania did not for
the 6BC8 while GE did to −70 V for the 6386. The plate-resistance route is noisier, since it divides one
eyeballed curve by another, but it can be run on **any** sheet that plots two of µ, gm and r_p, which is most
of them. A procedure should name both and say to prefer the first where it exists.

**A general lesson, since this is the third time the same trap has caught one of us in this comparison.** A
tabulated value at one operating point says nothing about a law's shape (4.3.1). Two figures quoted to
different endpoints are not comparable (4.6). And **a curve on a linear axis cannot constrain a law over a
range where the quantity spans two orders of magnitude** — which is why the one datasheet that used a
logarithmic axis settled in five minutes a question that two linear plots could not settle at all.

## 5. The sidechain, the ratio switch, and attack and release, against the 1176

### 5.1 The detector

**V4 is a 6AL5 twin vacuum diode** [51]. Its two sections are wired **in series, both conducting the same way**,
with the junction between them forming the timing node: pin 1 (plate 1) → pin 2 (cathode 1) tied to pin 7
(plate 2) → pin 5 (cathode 2) [3]. The outer ends, pins 1 and 5, are the two rectifier inputs; each is coupled
to one of the transformer taps through a **0.5 µF** capacitor, **C6** and **C7**, and returned through a
**220 kΩ** resistor, **R36** and **R37**, to a common DC bias node [3].

So the detector is a **balanced, back-biased peak rectifier**: the two ends are driven in antiphase from the
symmetric transformer taps, both ends sit at a positive standing bias, and current flows into the timing node
only when the differential drive exceeds that bias. The bias *is* the threshold, and the drawing boxes it as
**45 V at the 12:1 setting** [3].

Forty-five volts is a startling threshold if you are used to solid-state limiters, and it is the right order
for this circuit: the taps come off the primary of an output transformer whose plates sit at 295 V, so tens of
volts of programme swing at the tap is ordinary. It also explains why the detector is quiet — nothing at all
happens in the sidechain until the signal is large, so there is no low-level rectifier noise modulating the
control voltage.

**What I could not resolve, said plainly.** The DC chain that sets that bias is **R38 82 kΩ** in series with
the ratio-selected shunt (R39 47 kΩ, R45 20 kΩ, R46 6.8 kΩ or R47 1 kΩ), bypassed by **C8A 20 µF** [3]. I
traced those parts and their values with confidence. I could **not** determine from the scan which supply node
feeds R38, nor the exact polarity by which the timing node's voltage reaches the 6BC8 grids, nor the function
of the net the drawing labels "X" (which appears once at R31 47 kΩ near the test switch and once in the power
supply). Working backwards from the boxed 45 V through an 82 kΩ / 47 kΩ divider implies a source of about
**123.5 V**, and the nearest labelled node on the drawing is the OB2-regulated **108 V**, which would give
39 V instead. Something in that chain is not what I think it is. **For the model it does not matter**, because
what the model needs is the ratio *between* the four bias values, and that is fixed by the four resistors
regardless of the source (5.3). I would rather record the gap than paper over it.

### 5.2 The timing network, and an exact match to the published figures

The whole timing network is four parts:

| Part | Value | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **C5** | **0.1 µF** | The timing capacitor, on the control bus, bypassing it to ground |
| **R32** | **10 kΩ** | Fixed resistor in the charge path |
| **R33** | **25 kΩ**, linear | ATTACK, ganged to S4 (limit defeat) |
| **R34** | **5 MΩ**, linear | RELEASE |
| **R35** | **270 kΩ** | Fixed end-stop in the discharge path |

**The release figures fall straight out, and they are exact.**

```
τ_rel(fastest) = R35 · C5          = 270 kΩ  × 0.1 µF = 27.0 ms
τ_rel(slowest) = (R35 + R34) · C5  = 5.27 MΩ × 0.1 µF = 527 ms
```

Published: **"RELEASE TIME: Adjustable from 27 to 527 milliseconds"** [8] [9]. Not "about 27", not "roughly
half a second" — 27 and 527, which are the two RC products to three and three significant figures. **This is
the strongest single confirmation in the document that I have read the circuit correctly**, and it establishes
two things at once: C5 is the timing capacitor, and Universal Audio quoted **plain RC products** rather than a
measured time to some percentage of final value.

**The attack figures half fall out.** Applying the same convention:

```
τ_att(slowest) = R32 · C5 = 10 kΩ × 0.1 µF = 1000 µs
```

Published: **"ATTACK TIME: Adjustable from 100 to 1000 microseconds"** [8] [9]. The slow end is exact. The fast
end, 100 µs, needs the charging resistance to fall to **1 kΩ**, one tenth of R32.

**What I could not settle**: whether R33's 25 kΩ track shunts R32 or sits in series with it. The drawing shows
R32 spanning between two points on the charge path with R33's track and wiper between them, and at the scan's
resolution the wiper's terminal is ambiguous. Each reading I could make gives a range of the right order and
none reproduces both published endpoints cleanly:

| Reading | Range of charging resistance | Implied τ range |
|---|---|---|
| R33 as a rheostat in series with R32 | 10 kΩ to 35 kΩ | 1000 µs to 3500 µs |
| R33's used section shunting R32 | 0 to 7.14 kΩ | 0 to 714 µs |
| Track in parallel with (R32 + wiper section) | 7.14 kΩ to 14.6 kΩ | 714 µs to 1460 µs |
| **What the published numbers require** | **1 kΩ to 10 kΩ** | **100 µs to 1000 µs** |

**This is worth flagging as a family trait rather than a defeat**, because the 1176 dossier hits exactly the
same wall on exactly the same control: a GroupDIY analysis of a Rev D 1176 "could not reproduce the 800 µs
attack figure from the 25 kΩ attack pot and 220 nF with a plain RC formula" ([[1176]] section 3.5) [40]. Two
units, five years apart, both with a 25 kΩ attack pot, both with a release range that matches RC exactly and
an attack range that does not. Whatever convention Universal Audio used for the attack figure, they used the
same one twice. **For the model I take the published endpoints as the specification and fit the law to them**
(9.3), and I say in the test plan that the attack figure is a manufacturer's number of unstated criterion
(11.2).

**Note 4 settles the taper, which the 1176 dossier had to guess at.** "ALL POTENTIOMETERS ARE LINEAR TAPER"
[3]. So the release resistance sweeps linearly with knob angle and the time constant is **linear in time, not
geometric**:

```
τ_rel(p) = 27 ms + 500 ms · (7 − p) / 6           p = panel mark 1 … 7, 7 = fastest      (derived)
```

giving **527, 444, 360, 277, 194, 110, 27 ms** at the seven marks. Compare [[1176]] section 7.2, which had to
choose between a linear and a geometric mapping with no documentation and recommended geometric on feel. Here
the drawing says linear, and the 176 model should use linear even though it puts most of the audible change at
the fast end of the dial. **That is a real difference in how the two knobs feel, and it is documented, so the
model should keep it rather than smoothing it away.**

### 5.3 The ratio switch does two things at once

**S5 is a three-deck rotary with four positions** [3]:

| Deck | What it selects | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | One tap on the **upper** half of T3's primary, via C6 to the rectifier | Sets how much of the output the detector sees |
| 2 | The **mirror-image tap** on the lower half, via C7 | Same, for the other side of the balanced feed |
| 3 | One of **R39 47 kΩ, R45 20 kΩ, R46 6.8 kΩ, R47 1 kΩ** to ground, below R38 82 kΩ | Sets the rectifier's DC back-bias, i.e. the **threshold** |

The tap positions, from drawing A-1050 (3.7), are **13 %, 32 %, 60 % and 100 %** of the plate-to-centre
voltage on each side [7]. Tracing deck 2 on C-10476, the ratio positions map to the plain-colour leads as
**2:1 → BRN (13 %), 4:1 → VIO (32 %), 8:1 → SL (60 %), 12:1 → BLK (100 %)**, with deck 1 taking the matching
white-tracer lead on the other half [3]. So **the higher the ratio, the more of the output the detector sees.**

Sound On Sound reached the same conclusion independently and said why it is clever:

> Its primary coil has four taps for the various ratio settings. This way, a low driving impedance to the
> detector helps to maintain fast attack times, and, just as importantly, **the four ratio taps counteract the
> threshold, so that every ratio setting is in the sweet spot of the compressor.** [25]

That is exactly what deck 3 is for. The tap alone would drop the threshold by 17.7 dB going from 12:1 to 2:1
(the ratio of 100 % to 13 %); the bias ladder pushes back the other way, and the two together give the modest
8 dB threshold spread that the published curves actually show (6.2). **This is the same two-ladder trick the
1176 uses**, with one ladder setting the sidechain drive and the other the rectifier bias, and the 1176's
manual describes its own version in almost these words: "A bias level is applied to the diodes by the
Compression Ratio pushbutton switches. This controls the threshold of limiting" [40] ([[1176]] section 3.5).

**Derived arithmetic on the bias ladder.** Treating R38 82 kΩ as the series element and the selected resistor
as the shunt, the bias is proportional to `R_sel / (R_sel + 82 kΩ)`:

| Ratio | R_sel | Bias fraction | Relative to 12:1 | Bias if the 12:1 value is the boxed 45 V |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12:1 | 47 kΩ | 0.3643 | 0.0 dB | **45.0 V** (given [3]) |
| 8:1 | 20 kΩ | 0.1961 | −5.4 dB | 24.2 V (derived) |
| 4:1 | 6.8 kΩ | 0.0766 | −13.6 dB | 9.5 V (derived) |
| 2:1 | 1 kΩ | 0.01205 | −29.6 dB | 1.5 V (derived) |

**The assignment of resistors to ratios is derived, not read.** I could not resolve deck 3's contact order from
the scan. It is fixed by two independent pieces of evidence pointing the same way: the drawing's own note that
the 45 V figure applies "AT 12 TO 1 RATIO", which requires 12:1 to take the largest bias, i.e. the largest
shunt, i.e. R39 47 kΩ; and the fact that R39 47 kΩ is *also* the fixed shunt on the 175-B, which is a fixed
12:1 machine [2]. Two documents agree, so I am confident, but it is a derivation and it is labelled one.

**Now put the two decks together to predict the thresholds.** Threshold occurs when the tap voltage reaches the
bias, so the plate-to-plate voltage needed is `V_bias / k`:

| Ratio | k (tap) | V_bias | V_thr = V_bias / k | Predicted, relative to 12:1 | **Measured off the published curve (6.2)** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12:1 | 1.00 | 45.0 V | 45.0 V | 0.0 dB | **0 dB** (knee at ≈ −20 dBm) |
| 8:1 | 0.60 | 24.2 V | 40.4 V | −0.9 dB | **−2 dB** (≈ −22 dBm) |
| 4:1 | 0.32 | 9.5 V | 29.6 V | −3.6 dB | **−5 dB** (≈ −25 dBm) |
| 2:1 | 0.13 | 1.5 V | 11.4 V | −11.9 dB | **−8 dB** (≈ −28 dBm) |

Right ordering, right sign, the two middle positions within about 1.5 dB, and the 2:1 position over-predicted
by 4 dB. Given that the threshold readings come off a scanned graph at ±1 dB and that the bias source voltage
is itself unresolved (5.1), I call that a **good corroboration of the mechanism and a poor calibration of the
constants**, and section 9.4 says which of the two the model should trust.

### 5.4 Why ratio = 1 + loop gain, and why the tap percentages predict the nameplate

In a feedback compressor the ratio is not computed anywhere. It emerges. The [[1176]] dossier derives this in
its section 3.7 [41] and the derivation carries over unchanged, because the loop has the same shape. The
general treatment of where a detector sits relative to a gain computer, and why feedback and feedforward
topologies behave differently, is Giannoulis, Massberg and Reiss [46]; what follows is the feedback case with
a real gain element in the loop instead of a computed one:

Let `Y` be the output level in dB, `X` the input level in dB, `G` the gain reduction in dB, `k` the fraction of
the output the detector sees, `V_T` the detector's back-bias, and `F` the gain element's control law in dB per
volt. Then `G = −F(k·y − V_T)` where `y` is the output amplitude in volts, and linearising about an operating
point gives a loop gain

```
L = dG/dY = F'(e) · (ln 10 / 20) · (V_T + e),     e = k·y − V_T
```

and, since `Y = X + G`, a static slope `dY/dX = 1 / (1 + L)`, i.e. an effective ratio

```
R = 1 + L
```

**`L` is proportional to `k` at a fixed operating point.** So if the four transformer taps are the only thing
that changes, the four ratios should be `1 + L₀·k` for a single constant `L₀`. The taps are 13 %, 32 %, 60 %
and 100 % [7]. Setting `L₀ = 11` so that the 100 % tap gives 12:1:

| Tap `k` | `R = 1 + 11k` | **Nameplate** | Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | **12.0** | 12:1 | 0 % |
| 0.60 | **7.6** | 8:1 | −5 % |
| 0.32 | **4.5** | 4:1 | +13 % |
| 0.13 | **2.4** | 2:1 | +22 % |

**Four nameplate ratios predicted from four transformer tap percentages and one fitted constant, all within
22 % and three of the four within 13 %.** That is the strongest quantitative result in this dossier, and it is
built entirely on numbers Universal Audio printed: the tap percentages are on drawing A-1050 [7] and the
ratios are on the front panel [29].

A one-parameter refinement fits better still. Allowing the loop gain to scale as a power of the tap,

```
R(k) = 1 + 11 · k^1.15                                                    (derived)
```

gives **12.0, 7.2, 4.0 and 2.1** against the nameplate 12, 8, 4 and 2. The exponent slightly above 1 is what
you would expect if the ratio switch also changes `V_T` in the same direction, which it does (5.3), since `L`
depends on `V_T` as well as on `k`.

**Three consequences follow, and all three are audible.**

1. **The ratio rises as the unit works harder.** `L` depends on `F'`, the gain element's local slope, which
   from 4.4 goes from 2.3 dB/V at one volt of control to 8.7 dB/V at six. So the curve keeps bending toward
   limiting as gain reduction deepens, which is exactly what the published curves show above their knees
   (6.2), and it is the same mechanism, though not the same device, that makes the 1176 do it.
2. **The knee is soft, and it hardens with ratio.** Near threshold `e` is small and `L` is small, so the
   initial slope is gentle; the higher the tap, the faster `L` grows and the harder the corner. UA say the same
   thing about their own emulation: *"the response knee is gentler at lower compression ratios"* [12].
3. **Attack and release are programme-dependent for free.** The capacitor charges from the overdrive `e`,
   which shrinks as gain reduction builds, so a big transient is caught faster than a small one and the last
   few decibels of gain reduction arrive slowly. Universal Audio's own plug-in manual notes a
   ratio-dependence that falls out of the same structure: *"Actual attack time varies slightly based on the
   selected ratio. Higher ratios will maintain the fastest attack times"* [12] — which is what you would
   expect, because the higher tap delivers more overdrive to charge the same capacitor through the same
   resistor.

### 5.6 What Putnam kept, and what he changed

**Kept, and it is more than anybody says.**

- The **feedback loop tapped after the gain element**, and therefore the emergent-ratio behaviour, the soft
  knee that hardens with ratio, and the programme-dependent timing (5.4).
- The **fixed threshold with no threshold control**, driven into by an input attenuator that sits ahead of the
  input transformer.
- The **two-ladder ratio trick**: one ladder sets the detector's drive, the other sets its bias, and they are
  arranged so that raising the ratio also raises the threshold.
- The **backwards attack and release knobs**, clockwise for faster, with a **limit-defeat detent past the
  anticlockwise end of ATTACK** that leaves you a line amplifier. The 176 implements it as a switch physically
  ganged to the attack pot [3]; the 1176 does the same thing at the same place on the same knob [40].
- The **1–7 panel marks** on both small knobs.
- The **resting-point trim** on the gain element, set by a factory procedure, that parks the device just inside
  its steep region so a small overshoot buys a large gain change.
- **The actual timing resistors.** A **25 kΩ attack pot**, a **5 MΩ release pot**, and a **270 kΩ fixed
  resistor** in the release path. Those are the same three values in both machines.

**Changed, and there are only four things.**

1. **The gain element**, tube for FET, which brings the single-ended shunt topology, the different anti-
   distortion scheme and the loss of the balancing trims.
2. **The timing capacitor**, 0.1 µF to 0.022/0.22 µF. This is the change that moves the numbers. Release:
   270 kΩ and 5.27 MΩ into 0.1 µF give 27–527 ms; into 0.22 µF they give 59–1159 ms, and the 1176 is published
   at 50–1100 ms [40].
3. **The fixed attack resistor**, 10 kΩ to 470 Ω, which is what drops the slow end of the attack range from
   1000 µs to 800 µs while the pot lets the fast end reach 20 µs.
4. **The ratio set**, 2/4/8/12 to 4/8/12/20, and the switch type, rotary to interlocking push-buttons — which
   is what makes all-buttons mode possible at all. There is no equivalent on the 176: a four-position rotary
   cannot select two ratios at once, so **the 176 has no British mode and never could have**.

That last point is worth stating for the plug-in's documentation, because it is the kind of thing users ask
for. The 1176's most famous trick is a consequence of a mechanical choice Putnam made *after* the 176.

**A one-sentence summary for the plug-in's about box, which I believe is defensible from the two drawings:**
*the 1176 is the 176's sidechain and control philosophy, kept almost part-for-part, wrapped around a different
gain element and re-scaled by one capacitor.*

**Where I come down on each disagreement.**

- **Frequency response.** No way to choose. The tighter figure is on the more technical page; the looser one
  is in the sales copy, where you would expect the *looser* figure to be a mistake rather than a hedge. I model
  ±0.5 dB and note the conflict.
- **Gain step.** The resistors settle it: R2 20 kΩ over R3 6.8 kΩ is **11.9 dB** (3.3). That supports the
  brochure's −12/−24 minimum-input pair, which is a 12 dB step, over the typewritten page's −12/−22. **The
  circuit outranks both pages.**
- **Absolute gain.** 24/34 dB, 25/35 dB, and my reading of the published curve's slope and intercept says
  **38 dB** (6.2). Three answers. The curve is the only one of the three whose measurement conditions are
  printed on it, so it wins for calibration purposes, but the 4 dB gap between it and the nearest specification
  is unexplained and I am flagging it rather than resolving it.

**One number worth pulling out for the model**, because it fixes the operating window: **maximum output
+24 dBm, output at the threshold of limiting +13 dBm**, both from both pages. That is **11 dB of headroom above
threshold**, which is a compact and useful statement of what this machine is for: it is a peak limiter with
about ten decibels of room to work in, and the manual's advice that limiting "should not exceed 3 to 4 db" [1]
sits comfortably inside it.

### 6.2 The compression ratio curves

**This is the only published transfer measurement of either unit that I found.** Universal Audio reproduce a
graph headed **COMPRESSION RATIO CURVES** in the support manual for their own emulation, and say where it came
from: *"The graph below from the original UA 176 hardware manual illustrates how the response knee is gentler
at lower compression ratios"* [12]. The image is at [13] and is saved as
`ref/ua176-uadman-3-15706680558740.png`.

**What the axes are.** The x axis is `INPUT DBM`, ruled at −32, −28, −24, −20, −16, −12, −8, −4 and 0. The y
axis is `OUTPUT DBM`, ruled at +6, +10, +14, +18 and +22. Each axis carries a **second scale labelled
LADDER**, and the x-axis ladder numbers stand at exactly the same gridlines as the input numbers with the
relation `ladder = −input − 6`. **Derived:** the sweep was made by holding a source at **−6 dBm** and turning
the input ladder attenuator from 26 dB down to about 6 dB, which is precisely the procedure the manual tells
the user to follow [1]. That is a nice piece of evidence that the graph is a real bench measurement of a real
unit rather than a draughtsman's illustration.

**What I read off it.** All rows are **estimates** at about ±1 dB on levels and ±20 % on slopes, because this
is a scanned reproduction of a 1960s plot.

| Feature | Reading |
|---|---|
| Common low-level segment | All four curves lie on **one straight 45° line** below about −28 dBm, so **the low-level gain is identical at every ratio** |
| Implied gain on that segment | `output = input + 38 dB` |
| Knee of the **12:1** curve | ≈ **−20 dBm in**, ≈ **+18.5 dBm out**, and the corner is **sharp** |
| Departure of the **8:1** curve | ≈ **−22 dBm** |

| Slope of 8:1 from −16 dBm to 0 dBm | ≈ 0.126, i.e. **about 8.0:1** |
| Slope of 4:1 from −20 dBm to 0 dBm | ≈ 0.195, i.e. **about 5.1:1** |
| Slope of 2:1 from −20 dBm to 0 dBm | ≈ 0.37, i.e. **about 2.7:1** |
| **Right-hand end** | **All four curves converge to about +20 dBm at 0 dBm input** |

**Three structural facts matter more than any of the individual numbers.**

1. **The four curves converge at the top.** This is the visible signature of the two-ladder ratio switch: the
   taps raise the drive and the bias ladder raises the threshold, and the two are arranged so that at full
   drive every setting delivers the same output. Sound On Sound describe the design intent in exactly these
   terms — "the four ratio taps counteract the threshold, so that every ratio setting is in the sweet spot"
   [25]. **A model that gets the ratios right and the convergence wrong has missed the point of the switch.**
2. **The thresholds spread by only about 8 dB** across a range of taps that spans 17.7 dB, which is the
   arithmetic of 5.3 done backwards.
3. **The measured slopes are all steeper than the nameplate** except at 12:1, and the excess grows as the
   nameplate ratio falls: 2.7 against 2, 5.1 against 4, 8.0 against 8, 11.5 against 12. That is consistent
   with the loop-gain law of 5.4 including its `k^1.15` correction and with the curves continuing to bend as
   gain reduction deepens (4.3).

**One thing does not reconcile.** The specification says the output at the threshold of limiting is +13 dBm
[8] [9]; the graph puts the 12:1 knee at about +18.5 dBm. The gap is 5.5 dB. The specification is for the
175B, does not state the gain-jumper position or the output attenuator setting, and the +13 dBm figure happens
to sit exactly on the graph's 4:1 departure point, which may be coincidence. I have not resolved it. **For
calibration purposes I take the graph, because its conditions are printed on it and the specification's are
not**, and the test plan asserts the graph's features rather than the specification's single number (11.1).

### 6.3 What nobody publishes

Said explicitly, so that the test plan does not invent bounds where there is nothing to assert against.

- **No THD figure with a stated amount of gain reduction.** Both specification pages give THD only for the
  maximum-output condition **with limiting switched off** [8] [9]. The only claim about distortion under
  limiting is the brochure's qualitative "negligible increase in distortion at levels above" threshold [8].
- **No harmonic spectrum**, at any level, from anybody.
- **No attack or release measurement to a stated criterion.** The published 100–1000 µs and 27–527 ms are, as
  5.2 shows, plain RC products of the timing network rather than measured settling times, and no source states
  a percentage of final value.
- **No independent laboratory measurement of the hardware.** The survey found the same thing for every unit in
  the lab [44] and it is true here: Audio Science Review does not measure studio compressors, and no plug-in
  vendor publishes a null test against a 176.
- **No noise spectrum.** The 80 dB signal-to-noise figure has no stated bandwidth or reference level [8] [9].
- **No transformer measurement.** Drawing A-1050 gives tap percentages but no turns ratio, no primary
  impedance, no inductance and no bandwidth [7], so the low-frequency roll-off and the core behaviour are
  entirely uncalibrated.
- **No frequency response measured under gain reduction beyond the one line** saying it is within ±0.5 dB at
  5 dB of limiting [9].

## 7. How it is described as sounding, and what an emulation must get right

### 7.1 The descriptions

The best single source is Hannes Bieger's Sound On Sound review of Retro Instruments' hardware recreation,
March 2013, which describes the design rather than only the clone [25]:

> Bill Putnam's 176 Valve Limiter, the predecessor of the ubiquituous UREI 1176LN, is among the most coveted
> and sought-after vintage compressors.

> Like the 1176, the 176 was originally named 'Limiting Amplifier', and it's a description that's right on the
> money: it illustrates the muscular, forceful action of the compression much better than the word
> 'compressor'.

> The compressor/limiter seems to lend a sense of urgency to any audio that's fed through it. In a similar way
> to the sonic behaviour of a well-maintained Blackface UREI 1176LN, **the audio gets brighter and more present
> in the mix**, which makes the 176 an ideal tool for bringing important tracks, such as lead vocals, guitar
> solos or bass lines, forward, even in a dense production.

> With very short attack, and especially release, values … **the 176 can sound even more aggressive than an
> 1176 set to stun**, which is quite amazing.

> In certain cases, **the 176 breaks up on loud bass transients pretty fast**.

Universal Audio, about their own emulation and therefore about the hardware they measured:

> Based on a pair of UA's 1960s golden units, the UA 175-B and 176 Tube Compressor Collection gives you all
> the ultra-musical harmonics and colorful saturation of these original units — down to their magical feedback
> compression circuit — **giving you softer compression at lower input levels and harder, nearly brickwall
> limiting at higher levels.** [12]

And the two endorsement quotes on Retro Instruments' page, which are marketing but are at least attributed
[26]: Marek Stycos calls it "an incredibly fat-sounding dynamics controller" for drums and bass and says you
can "use it to just clamp down on peaks, or push it hard to add energy, excitement and brightness"; Chris
Lord-Alge says it has become his "first choice for lead vocals".

Sound On Sound's survey of classic compressors puts the pair in company: it lists the "Universal Audio 175 or
176" among the vintage vari-mu designs worth hunting emulations of, alongside the Altec 436, the Collins 26U1,
the Federal AM864U, the Gates Sta-Level and the RCA BA-6A, and records Sylvia Massey's vocal chain of
"Universal Audio 175 plus RCA BA6A" [48]. That is the clearest statement I found of what company this machine
keeps.

**On records: I found nothing usable.** Universal Audio say the units were "used on thousands of seminal
records" [12] [14] and name none. I found two dated studio inventories proving working use — a 175B at Right
Track Recording in *Mix*, May 1989 [33], and two 175Bs plus four 176s in a room listed in *EQ*, June 1997 [34]
— and no credible source attributing a specific record to one. **I am not going to repeat the usual
attributions from memory.**

### 7.2 What an emulation has to get right

Assembled from the above and from the circuit, in the order I think matters:

1. **Two curves, not one.** The gain law and the distortion law are the same tube characteristic (4.5), so
   distortion must rise with gain reduction rather than being a fixed saturator bolted on afterwards. If gain
   reduction and distortion can be varied independently in the model, the model is wrong.
2. **Odd-order residue, and a mismatch control that reintroduces even orders.** The push-pull pair cancels even
   harmonics structurally, and the cancellation is only as good as the tube matching, which the manual itself
   warns about [1]. A perfect model sounds sterile.
3. **The soft knee that hardens with ratio**, and **the four curves converging at high input** (6.2). Both
   fall out of the loop, so a model that implements the loop gets them free and a model that uses a gain
   computer has to fake them.
4. **Ratios that are not the nameplate.** Measured off UA's own graph, the four settings run at roughly 2.7,
   5.1, 8.0 and 11.5 to one (6.2). A model whose 2:1 setting measures 2.0:1 has taken the silkscreen literally.
5. **A ratio that increases as gain reduction deepens**, because the tube's decibels-per-volt increases (4.3).
   This is what "harder, nearly brickwall limiting at higher levels" [12] means mechanically.
6. **Programme-dependent timing for the right reason.** The capacitor charges from the overdrive, which
   shrinks as gain reduction builds, so big transients are caught faster than small ones and the last
   decibels arrive slowly. Also the ratio-dependence UA note: higher ratios attack faster [12].
7. **Fast enough to break up.** "Breaks up on loud bass transients pretty fast" [25] and "more aggressive than
   an 1176 set to stun" [25] both mean the loop can track individual cycles of low-frequency material at the
   fast settings. At 100 µs and 27 ms it certainly can. That produces intermodulation on bass, and the model
   must produce it rather than smoothing it away — which means the sidechain runs at the oversampled rate.
8. **A bright tilt, not a flat response.** Both Sound On Sound and the 1176 literature describe the same
   perceived brightening [25]. Some of it is the loop, some is transformer and tube behaviour, and a model
   that is spectrally flat under gain reduction will not sound like the descriptions.
9. **A VU meter with VU ballistics** switched between input, gain reduction and output, on a **cream face**
   (2.4).
10. **Limit defeat that still colours.** Attack fully anticlockwise must still pass audio through the tube,
    transformers and amplifier [1].

**And what makes an emulation wrong**: a feed-forward peak compressor wearing the panel; ratios that measure
their nameplate; a knee that is the same shape at every ratio; distortion added by a separate stage whose
amount does not track gain reduction; and — the specific failure this repository has to avoid — **something
that measures within a decibel or two of the lab's own 1176 on every test**, which would make it a faceplate
rather than a model. Section 11.4 exists to catch exactly that.

## 9. Recommended DSP design

Target: 44.1 to 192 kHz, real time, one `Processor` hosting both units behind a `ua176_model` switch, because
the two machines are one circuit with one extra switch (1.2). Everything in this section is a recommendation
for the example plug-in; none of it belongs in the framework, which contributes the shared behaviour (knob
widgets, detented rotaries, VU ballistics, oversampling infrastructure) and none of the colour.

### 9.1 Block diagram

```
in ─► input ladder + vernier ─► T1 (transformer flavour) ─► grid divider (gain jumper)
                                                                     │
                                       control u ────────────────────┤
                                                                     ▼
                                                       balanced 6BC8 pair  (9.3.2)
                                                                     │
                                       ┌── common-mode residue ──────┤  (thump, mismatch-dependent)
                                       ▼                             ▼
                                   summed in                  differential out
                                                                     │
                                                        T2 ─► 12AX7 ─► 12BH7 ─► T3   (9.3.4)
                                                                     │
                                    ┌────────────────────────────────┤
                                    ▼                                ▼
                        tap k_r · y[n−1]                     output ladder + vernier ─► out
                                    │
                        half-wave back-biased detector,  e = max(0, s − V_T,r)      (9.3.5)
                                    │
                        C5 timing:  v[n] = v[n−1] + a_att·max(0, e−v) − a_rel·v
                                    │
                                    └──────────────► u = v  (volts of extra grid bias)
```

**The loop runs at the oversampled rate and carries a one-sample delay.** Both are load-bearing: the delay
makes the feedback loop computable, and the oversampled rate is what lets the detector track individual cycles
of low-frequency material and produce the intermodulation that Sound On Sound hear as "breaks up on loud bass
transients pretty fast" [25].

must crossfade `k_r` and `V_T,r` over about 10 ms, because both change simultaneously and a step in `V_T`
inside a feedback loop is a click. `ua176_attack` crossing the OFF detent should ramp the control voltage to
zero over about 20 ms rather than dropping it.

### 9.3 Per-block equations

#### 9.3.1 Attenuators

```
A_in_dB  = −(2 · ua176_input  + 2.5 · ua176_input_vernier)          0 … −42.5 dB
A_out_dB = −(2 · ua176_output + 2.5 · ua176_output_vernier)
G_jumper_dB = 0 if hi, −11.9 if lo                                  (derived, 3.3)
```

The input attenuator sits **before** the transformer model, and the output attenuator **after** the output
transformer, so the output control has no effect whatever on the loop [3]. Getting that wrong is the single
most common way to make a fixed-threshold limiter behave incorrectly.

#### 9.3.2 The balanced 6BC8 pair, which is the whole model

Write `w` for the magnitude of the negative grid-to-cathode voltage, so `w = 1.85 V` at idle. From 4.3:

```
gm(w) = gm₀ · exp( −(w / V0)^n )         V0 = 4.16 V,  n = 2.16      (derived, 4.3)
```

**Treat `n` as a tunable in the range 1.6 to 2.2, not as a constant.** 4.6.1 shows Sylvania's curve for the
same tube implies 1.6 to 2.0 where Tung-Sol's implies 2.16, and that the law's deep end is unsupported past
about −8 V. Fit `n` inside that range to the published compression curves of 6.2 rather than freezing it at
one maker's value, and clamp the gain reduction at 30 dB (9.3.2 below).

That form is exact rather than approximate as a restatement of 4.3, because `10^(−8.686·x/20) = e^(−x)`. Now
let `I(w)` be the plate current, which is the integral of transconductance:

```
I(w) = ∫_w^∞ gm(s) ds                    precompute once as a 1-D table
```

Then, with `x` the instantaneous audio at the grids (signed, differential) and `u ≥ 0` the control voltage:

```
w_A = 1.85 + u − x
w_B = 1.85 + u + x

y_diff   = [ I_A(w_A) − I_B(w_B) ] / (2 · gm(1.85))          the audio output
y_common = [ I_A(w_A) + I_B(w_B) ] / 2                        the thump, before T2 rejects it
```

`I_A` and `I_B` are the same table with each half's own `gm₀` and `V0`, offset by the mismatch:

```
gm₀_A = gm₀ · (1 + δ),   gm₀_B = gm₀ · (1 − δ),   δ = 0.1 · (1 − ua176_tube_match)
```

**This one block delivers five behaviours that would otherwise each need their own hack:**

1. **The static gain law.** For small `x`, `y_diff → x · gm(1.85 + u) / gm(1.85)`, which is the table in 4.4.
2. **Odd-order distortion only, when matched.** `y_diff` is an odd function of `x` when the halves are
   identical, so even harmonics cancel exactly, as the push-pull hardware does (4.5).
3. **Even-order distortion when mismatched**, in the right proportion, without a separate saturator.
4. **Distortion that rises with gain reduction**, because the curvature of `I` grows with `w` (4.5). No
   separate mechanism, and no way to decouple the two, which is exactly the constraint 7.2 asks for.
5. **The control thump.** `y_common` moves with `u` and is rejected in proportion to the match; feed
   `(1 − ua176_tube_match) · d(y_common)/dt` into the output through the transformer's common-mode leakage.

**One thing the table alone does not give: the load division.** Section 4.3.1 shows µ falls by a factor of
about three while gm falls by a factor of a hundred, so `r_p = µ/gm` rises about thirtyfold and the stage gain
is `µ·R_L/(r_p + R_L)`, not `gm·R_L`. Carry µ as a second datasheet-anchored curve and divide:

```
µ(w)     = interpolate( [(0.5, 39), (2.2, 33), (2.6, 32), (5.4, 19), (10.1, 12)] )
r_p(w)   = µ(w) / gm(w)
A(w)     = µ(w) · R_L / (r_p(w) + R_L)
y_diff  *= A(w_dc) / A(1.85)             normalise so idle gain is unity
```

**µ is tabulated and interpolated rather than fitted to a closed form, and that is deliberate.** I tried two.
An exponential `34·exp(−(w/12.5)^1.5)` tracks the shallow end but reads 16.4 where the curve says 12, and a
power law `55·w^−0.66` fits the three deep points to within 5 % but diverges to 87 near zero bias where the
curve says 39. The measured points are the five in 4.3.1, and a monotone interpolation through them is both
more accurate and more honest than a two-parameter form that is wrong at one end. gm keeps its closed form
because that one genuinely fits (4.3).

`R_L` is the weakest constant in the model, because T2 is uncharacterised (6.3); **estimate 5 kΩ**. The effect
of getting it wrong is bounded and known: it changes the maximum available gain reduction by a few decibels
(4.4), and section 11.1's calibration tests against the published curve will absorb the difference into the
loop-gain constant if `R_L` is off. **Skipping this correction entirely is what is not acceptable**, because
it overstates the gain reduction the tube can deliver by about 5 dB.

**And the same table is what antiderivative anti-aliasing wants.** `I` is already the antiderivative of the
nonlinearity, so first-order ADAA on this block costs one extra table lookup and one divide. That matters: a
gain element being modulated at audio rate is an aliasing generator, and this model gets the cure nearly free.
(Anti-aliasing is infrastructure, not a component, so it lives in the DSP crate — see the components crate's
own boundary rule [45].)

**Clamp** the gain reduction at `G_max`. **Estimate: 30 dB**, lowered from my first figure of 36 dB because
4.6.1 shows the fitted law's deep end is not supported: past about −8 V of grid it falls roughly six times
faster than Sylvania's plotted plate resistance implies. Clamping at 30 dB keeps the model inside the region
two manufacturers' curves agree on. **The published 41.9 dB that the −13 V datasheet point implies is
reachable by the tube and not by this law**, so a model that needs the last ten decibels should blend the
fitted law into the tabulated endpoint rather than extrapolate the power form out there.

#### 9.3.3 The `ua176_gas` control

A gassy 6BC8 leaks grid current, and grid current through the release path develops an offset that changes
with the release setting, which is exactly the manual's field test [1] (3.4):

```
u_gas = ua176_gas · 0.25 · (R_rel(ua176_release) / 5.27 MΩ)          volts    (estimate)
u_total = u + u_gas
```

Scaled so that sweeping the release knob from fastest to slowest changes the standing gain reduction by about
0.25 dB at `ua176_gas = 1`, matching the manual's "an increase in the reading of more than 1/4 db indicates
excessive gas". **The 0.25 factor is taken from the manual; the linear dependence on release resistance is
derived from Ohm's law; the overall scaling is an estimate.**

#### 9.3.4 T1, T2, the amplifier and T3

No source publishes a measurement of any of the three transformers, so this block is **entirely estimated**
and should be labelled as such wherever it surfaces. Recommended shape, matching what the lab already does in
`dsp::pre` for the 610 [43], and following the tube and transformer modelling practice collected in the DAFx
archive [53]:

- **T1**: gentle high-pass at about 15 Hz, gentle low-pass at about 40 kHz, a small odd-order saturation that
  only engages well above nominal level.
- **T2**: the interstage transformer, the same shape with a slightly lower high-frequency corner. Retro
  Instruments thought a bypass switch for this transformer was worth building [26], so it should be audible.
- **12AX7 and 12BH7 push-pull**: a mild symmetric soft clipper. Push-pull, so odd-order.
- **T3**: high-pass at about 20 Hz. This is the transformer whose primary the sidechain taps, so its
  low-frequency behaviour is inside the loop and affects how the detector sees bass.

The published response of ±0.5 dB from 20 Hz to 20 kHz [9] constrains all of this: whatever corners are
chosen, the assembled chain must meet that, and the test plan checks it (11.5).

#### 9.3.5 The detector

Per ratio index `r`, with the tap fractions from drawing A-1050 [7] and the bias ratios from the ladder (5.3):

| `ua176_ratio` | Ratio | `k_r` (tap) | `V_T,r` relative | `V_T,r` volts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2:1 | **0.13** | 0.0331 | 1.5 |
| 1 | 4:1 | **0.32** | 0.210 | 9.5 |
| 2 | 8:1 | **0.60** | 0.538 | 24.2 |
| 3 | 12:1 | **1.00** | 1.000 | **45.0** |

The `k_r` column and the 45.0 V are from Universal Audio drawings [7] [3]; the rest of the `V_T,r` column is
derived from the four ladder resistors (5.3).

```
s[n] = k_r · y_tap[n−1]                     one-sample loop delay, oversampled rate
e[n] = max(0, s[n] − V_T,r)                 half-wave, back-biased               (see note)
v[n] = v[n−1] + a_att · max(0, e[n] − v[n−1]) − a_rel · v[n−1]
u    = v
```

**Note on half-wave.** Reading C-10476, the 6AL5's two sections are in series conducting the same way, so the
detector responds to **one polarity** of the differential drive (5.1). That is derived from the drawing, not
stated by anybody, and it makes the model asymmetric on asymmetric material — which is a plausible origin for
the behaviour Retro Instruments expose on their clone as a "Vocal Asymmetry Tool" [26]. **I am not certain
enough of that trace to make it unconditional**: implement half-wave as the default and keep a full-wave
option behind a development flag, and let 11.6 be the test that tells them apart.

Timing coefficients, with `fs_p` the oversampled rate:

```
a_att = 1 − exp( −1 / (τ_att · fs_p) )
a_rel = 1 − exp( −1 / (τ_rel · fs_p) )

τ_rel(p) = 27 ms + 500 ms · (7 − p) / 6                             (schematic + note 4, 5.2)
τ_att(p) = 100 µs · (1000/100)^((7 − p)/6)   OR   linear in R       (see below)
```

**On the attack law**, 5.2 shows the published endpoints require a charging resistance from 1 kΩ to 10 kΩ,
which the schematic's R32 10 kΩ brackets at the slow end and which I could not reproduce exactly at the fast
end. Note 4 says the pot is linear, so **the honest choice is linear in resistance, which for a shunt across
R32 is not linear in time**. Recommended: model `R_att(p) = 10 kΩ ∥ R_sh(p)` with `R_sh` swept linearly so
that `R_att` hits 10 kΩ at p = 1 and 1 kΩ at p = 7, giving τ at the seven marks of **1000, 632, 424, 302, 224,
171, 100 µs** (derived). That lands both published endpoints, uses the stated taper, and is labelled an
estimate in between.

#### 9.3.6 Meter

VU ballistics: a second-order response reaching 99 % of a step in **300 ms** with 1 % to 2 % overshoot, the
same model already used for the 1176 and the LA-2A. In `gr` the needle deflects left by the gain reduction in
decibels; in `input` and `output` it reads level against the `ua176_meter_cal` reference (+4 or +8 dBm at
0 VU) [1].

#### 9.3.7 Stereo

The hardware has no link. When `ua176_link` is on, compute `e[n]` from the larger of the two channels'
rectified sidechains and run one capacitor, then apply the control to each channel's **own** tube model so the
distortion stays per-channel. When off, two independent instances.

## 10. Reuse: should a variable-mu component exist in `noob-electrical-components`?

### 10.1 The question, and the rule it has to satisfy

The components crate has a stated admission rule and I am going to hold this to it rather than argue around
it. From its README [45]:

> A component earns a place **once something real shares it, or is about to**. I am not trying to atomise a
> codebase into parts that each have one caller. An abstraction pulled out of a single user is usually the
> wrong shape for the second one, and I would rather discover the right shape from two real users than guess
> it from one.

and, on where the boundary falls:

> **Circuitry does not belong here.** A component is the part. The resistive divider it shunts, the sidechain
> that drives it and the make-up gain after it are the machine, and those differ from unit to unit while the
> part does not.

The README already anticipates this exact question: "**Variable-mu element.** The gain element of a whole
family the plug-ins do not cover yet, so it will arrive with the first unit that needs one" [45]. The 176 is
that unit, and a Fairchild 670 dossier was written in parallel, so the second user looked imminent.

**The rule has since been tightened, and the tightened version is the one this section is judged against.**
A part is now admitted when **two units are documented to contain it**, not when one does and another is
expected to. That closes the "or is about to" half of the original wording, which is the half the diode bridge
was admitted under and which the README itself recorded as the weaker justification. The change matters here
because it removes the argument I would otherwise have had to make about how imminent the Fairchild is: under
the tightened rule that question does not arise, and only the state of the documents counts.

### 10.2 What could honestly be shared, and what could not

Applying the crate's own boundary strictly, there is exactly one thing in this document that is *the part*:

**Shareable — the tube's own law.** A remote-cutoff triode is characterised by its **anode current as a
function of grid and anode voltage**, from which transconductance, amplification factor and plate resistance
all fall out as derivatives, and whose antiderivative in the grid variable is what any
instantaneous-nonlinearity model and any antiderivative anti-aliasing scheme actually needs (9.3.2). Section
4.3.1 shows why the current law rather than gm alone has to be the primitive: µ falls by a factor of three
while gm falls by a factor of a hundred, `r_p = µ/gm` therefore rises about thirtyfold, and a component that
exposed only gm would silently commit its callers to the wrong stage gain by several decibels.

The Fairchild researcher has a candidate form already fitted to the 6386, from Raffensperger, checked against
three points on GE's curves [54] [55]:

```
Ia = p1·Vak^p2 / [ (p3 − p4·Vgk)^p5 · (p6 + exp(p7·Vak − p8·Vgk)) ]
```

with the `(p3 − p4·Vgk)` factor producing the stretched taper, so that a smaller `p4` or `p5` gives a sharper
cutoff.

**A warning about that form, from the person who fitted it.** Differentiating it gives a poor
transconductance even though it tracks plate current well: at the class-A1 operating point it returns
2309 µmho where GE tabulate 4000, and its decay rate is not monotone, falling to 0.15 dB per volt around
−40 V and rising again below that, an inflection GE's own curve does not have [55]. **A fit to current can be
40 % wrong in slope and still look right**, so any current-law fit intended to drive a gain element must have
its derivative checked against the published gm curve before it is trusted. This matters directly for the 176,
where the audio *is* the derivative.

My own two-parameter transconductance form for the 6BC8,
`gm(w) = gm₀·exp(−(w/V0)^n)` with `V0 = 4.16 V` and `n = 2.16` (4.3), is a reduction of the same idea in one
variable. **A component holding a current law of this shape, per tube type, with gm, µ and r_p as derived
methods, plus the datasheet anchor points each parameter set was fitted to, would be a real part**: it is a
property of the tube and of nothing else in the circuit.

**Not shareable — everything else in section 3 and 5.** The balanced pair, the common-mode control injection,
the plate and cathode balance trims, the thump and its cancellation, the interstage transformer, the
back-biased vacuum-diode detector, the transformer-tap ratio switch, and the timing network are all *the
machine*. The Fairchild has its own versions of most of them and they are not the same versions. Pushing any
of that into a shared component is precisely the failure the audit found when it turned up three tube stages
that were three different circuits sharing one word.

### 10.3 The question I asked, the answer, and my recommendation

**The question was never whether both tubes are called variable-mu. It was whether their published curves are
the same functional shape with different constants.** I sent the 6BC8 figures to the Fairchild researcher and
asked for the 6386's equivalents: its gm, µ and r_p, the grid voltage for some small transconductance, whether
its datasheet says "remote cutoff", whether its plotted curve is a smooth monotonic decay of the same shape,
how much gain reduction the 670 actually uses, and whether the 670 also injects its control voltage
common-mode into a balanced pair.

**The answer took three passes to settle, and the version that survived is not the one I first accepted.**
4.6.1 has the working. In outline: I was told the two tubes were the same law with different constants, on
average-taper figures; I accepted that and rewrote this section to match; then, checking the exponent
directly on a logarithmic plot rather than through an average slope, the shape difference reappeared and is
now measured. **Average taper is blind to curvature, and curvature is the whole question.**

**Where it stands.** The exponent moves by a factor of 1.7 across four operating conditions of the 6386 on a
single GE plot, and by a similar factor for the 6BC8 depending on which maker's curve and which anchor points
are used. **It is not a property of a tube but of a tube, a plot, a condition and a fitting procedure**, so
**published data cannot settle whether these two tubes share a shape.** The most that survives is that the
6386 is at or below a pure exponential in every condition GE plot, and that an exponent near 2 is excluded for
it. 4.6.1 has the numbers and the retractions.

**What happened to my three original reasons for caution**, stated plainly since two of them changed status:

- **The shape reason: withdrawn, reinstated, and withdrawn again — this time for a better reason than either
  of the first two.** I could not have claimed it originally, because I was reasoning from what a
  remote-cutoff tube "ought" to do rather than from a curve. I briefly reinstated it on one curve each. It is
  now withdrawn because **the exponent moves by a factor of 1.7 within one tube on one plot** (4.6.1), so no
  amount of care in fitting makes it a quantity that can compare two datasheets. The shape question is not
  answered either way; it is unanswerable from published data.
- **The flat-µ reason: gone, and it was simply an error.** I inferred it from a single tabulated value.
  4.3.1 records the correction and the µ curve that refutes it. Both tubes' µ collapse at similar rates.
- **The admission-rule reason: stands.** Under the tightened wording (10.1) a part is admitted when two units
  are *documented* to contain it, and only one parameter set has been fitted.

**And one reason I did not have at the start, which is now the sharpest of them.** The plate-resistance check
(4.6.1) says **my own law's deep end is not supported**: below about −8 V it falls six-fold faster than
Sylvania's plotted r_p implies. So the 6BC8 parameter set that a shared component would ship with today is
one I have myself shown to be wrong in the region where a limiter spends its loudest moments. **A component
built now would enshrine that.**

**The constraint that replaces the abandoned shape comparison**, and it is the durable output of this whole
exchange. If the component is ever built, the two parameter sets must come from **one documented fitting
procedure, using the same class of anchor points, on curves measured in the same topology**. All three clauses
are load-bearing and each was learned by getting it wrong:

1. *One procedure*, because my own exponent moved from 2.16 to 1.71 on a change of anchor points inside a
   single curve.
2. *Same class of anchor points*, because a fit to interior points and a fit to endpoints are not the same
   measurement of the same thing.
3. *Same topology*, because GE's 6386 curves are a **cascode** connection whose lower section's plate floats
   at the upper section's cathode, while the 6BC8 curves are single-section characteristics at a fixed plate
   voltage — and **neither is the grounded-cathode stage that either compressor actually runs** (4.6.1).

Without all three, two implementations can agree on a number while disagreeing about the curve, which is
worse than not sharing at all because it looks like corroboration.

**So my recommendation changes shape.** It is not "do not build a variable-mu component". It is:

> **The component is right, and it should not be created until two real fits exist.**

Those are different conclusions, and the second is the one the evidence supports. The distinction matters
because a "do not build" verdict would get quoted later as a finding about the tubes, and the finding about
the tubes is the opposite.

**Why not create it today anyway.** There is one populated parameter set. Raffensperger's eight-parameter
current law is fitted to the **6386** [55]; **nobody has fitted a 6BC8 to it**, and I have not done it here —
my `gm₀`, `V0`, `n` form is a two-parameter reduction in one variable, not the same fit. A component created
now would ship with one filled slot and one empty one, which is the one-user abstraction the crate's README
warns against in its own words, and the crate already records the diode bridge's "about to be shared"
admission as **explicitly weaker** justification. Taking the weaker route twice running turns the exception
into the rule.

**What to do instead, and it costs almost nothing.** Build the 176's gain element inside the compressor lab as
a self-contained block, express it in the current-law form of 10.2, and **write the fitted constants and the
datasheet points they came from into the code as a documented table**. The Fairchild does the same with the
6386 inside its own engine. When the second fit exists, promote the interface — anode current as a function of
grid and anode voltage, with gm, µ and r_p as derived methods, parameterised per type — **with two real fits
on the day it is created**, which is exactly how the photocell earned its place.

**The shape it should have when it is created**, recorded so the decision is not re-litigated:

- **Holds:** a per-type parameter set for a current law `Ia(Vgk, Vak)`; `gm`, `µ` and `r_p` as derived methods
  rather than independent tables, since the datasheets show they are a coupled triple (4.6); the
  antiderivative in the grid variable, tabulated, which serves both the instantaneous nonlinearity and its
  anti-aliasing; the datasheet anchor points each parameter set was fitted to, with provenance; per-section
  parameter offsets so a caller can build a deliberately mismatched pair.
- **Refuses to hold:** anything about balanced pairs, plate or cathode circuits, control injection, bias
  supplies, detectors, transformers, ratio switches or time constants. Those are the machine. Both the 176 and
  the 670 build their own, and they are different — the 176 trims its balance with two pots and a separate
  GR ZERO, the 670 with a single 100 Ω BALANCE pot and a ZERO screw [55].

**One thing the two machines genuinely do share, and it is a design idea rather than a part.** The Fairchild
researcher confirms that the 670's input transformer secondary is centre-tapped, that its two ends drive the
two halves' grids so the audio is differential, and that the control voltage is injected at the centre through
two 100 kΩ resistors so it appears at both grids in common mode, with the output transformer taking the
difference so the control-induced DC shift cancels [55]. That is, part for part, what section 3.4 reads off
C-10476 for the 176, down to the resistor values being the same order. Fairchild's first published boast is
"the complete absence of audible thumps" [55], and the 176's manual devotes a page and a half to the balancing
procedure that achieves the same thing [1]. **Two designers, working independently, arrived at the same
answer to the same problem.** That is worth a paragraph in the plug-in's documentation, and it is emphatically
not worth a shared code path.

## 11. Test plan

**Two standards this repository enforces, and both apply here.** Every test asserts a **published figure**,
names it, and cites it. Where no real number is reachable, the test says so and asserts a *direction*, an
*ordering* or a *circuit identity* rather than an invented bound. Section 6.3 is the list of things nobody
publishes, and no test below pretends otherwise.

Unless a test says otherwise: 1 kHz sine, `ua176_model` = `176`, `ua176_ratio` = 12:1, `ua176_gain_jumper` =
`lo`, `ua176_tube_match` = 1.0, `ua176_gas` = 0, run at 44.1, 48 and 96 kHz.

### 11.1 Static behaviour, against the published curve

1. **The low-level segment is common to all four ratios.** Sweep the input from −40 to −30 dBm at each ratio.
   The four output curves must agree with each other **within 0.2 dB**, and the slope must be **1.00 ± 0.02**.
   *Figure:* all four curves lie on one straight 45° line below about −28 dBm on the COMPRESSION RATIO CURVES
   graph. *Source:* the graph from the original 176 hardware manual, reproduced by UA [12] [13].

2. **The four curves converge at high input.** At 0 dBm input, the four outputs must agree **within 1.0 dB**.
   *Figure:* the visible convergence of all four curves to about +20 dBm at 0 dBm input on the same graph
   [13]. This is the test that proves the ratio switch changes drive **and** threshold together; a model that
   only changes the ratio will fail it by many decibels. *Source:* [12] [13], corroborated by Sound On Sound's
   "the four ratio taps counteract the threshold, so that every ratio setting is in the sweet spot" [25].

3. **Threshold ordering and spread.** Define threshold as the input producing 1 dB of gain reduction. The four
   thresholds must be **monotonically increasing** from 2:1 to 12:1, and the spread from 2:1 to 12:1 must be
   **8 dB ± 3 dB**.
   *Figure:* departure points of about −28, −25, −22 and −20 dBm read off the published graph [13]. *Source:*
   [12] [13]. **The ±3 dB is mine**, because reading a departure point off a scanned 1960s plot is worth about
   ±1 dB per point and the tolerance has to carry that; it is a sanity bound, not evidence.

4. **Slopes above the knee.** Measure the local slope between 6 and 16 dB above each threshold:

   | `ua176_ratio` | required slope | as a ratio |
   |---|---|---|
   | 2:1 | 0.37 ± 0.08 | ≈ 2.7 : 1 |
   | 4:1 | 0.195 ± 0.04 | ≈ 5.1 : 1 |
   | 8:1 | 0.126 ± 0.03 | ≈ 8.0 : 1 |
   | 12:1 | 0.087 ± 0.02 | ≈ 11.5 : 1 |

   *Figure:* slopes measured off the published curves [13]. *Source:* [12] [13]. **Note that these are not the
   nameplate ratios**, and that is the point of the test: 2:1 measures nearer 2.7:1 on Universal Audio's own
   graph. A model that measures 2.00:1 at the 2:1 setting has taken the silkscreen literally and must fail
   here. The ±20 % tolerances are mine and are the reading error, stated as such.

5. **The knee hardens with ratio.** At each ratio, compare the slope 1 dB above threshold with the slope 10 dB
   above. The **ratio between those two slopes must decrease monotonically** from 2:1 to 12:1, i.e. the corner
   gets sharper as the nameplate ratio rises.
   *Figure:* "the response knee is gentler at lower compression ratios", and the visibly sharp 12:1 corner
   against the smooth 2:1 curve on the same graph. *Source:* UA's own manual text [12] and the graph [13].

6. **The 175B is the 176 at 12:1.** With `ua176_model` = `175B`, the full static curve must match the 176 at
   `ua176_ratio` = 12:1 **within 0.05 dB at every input level**.
   *Figure:* "The 175-B's sharper knee is the same as the 12:1 curve on the 176". *Source:* UA's manual [12],
   independently confirmed by the two schematics sharing R39 47 kΩ as the sidechain shunt (1.2) [2] [3]. **If
   this test fails, the two models have diverged and one of them is wrong**, because the hardware difference
   is one switch.

7. **The gain jumper step is 11.9 dB.** Switching `ua176_gain_jumper` from `lo` to `hi` must raise the gain by
   **11.9 dB ± 0.2 dB**, and the threshold measured at the input must fall by the same amount.
   *Figure:* R2 20 kΩ over R3 6.8 kΩ gives 6800/26800 = −11.9 dB. *Source:* schematic C-10476 [3]. **This is a
   derived figure that contradicts a published one** — the typewritten specification page implies a 10 dB step
   [9] while the brochure implies 12 dB [8] — and the test asserts the circuit, with 3.3 as the argument.

8. **Output at the threshold of limiting.** With `ua176_output` at 0 and the gain jumper at `hi`, the output
   level at the 12:1 threshold must be recorded and reported. **No pass/fail bound is asserted.**
   *Figure:* "MAXIMUM OUTPUT AT THRESHOLD OF LIMITING: +13 dbm" [8] [9], which **does not reconcile** with the
   +18.5 dBm knee on the published graph [13] (6.2). Two Universal Audio numbers disagree by 5.5 dB, neither
   states the attenuator settings, and I will not invent a tolerance to hide that. The test records the value
   so a future reader can compare, and this note is the reason.

### 11.2 Attack and release

9. **Release, both published endpoints.** Tone burst from −40 to −6 dBm, held 2 s, then removed. Measure the
   time for the gain reduction to fall to 1/e of its value. At `ua176_release` = 7 it must be
   **27 ms ± 15 %**; at `ua176_release` = 1 it must be **527 ms ± 15 %**.
   *Figures:* "RELEASE TIME: Adjustable from 27 to 527 milliseconds". *Source:* both specification pages
   [8] [9]. **These are the strongest timing figures in the document**, because 5.2 shows they are exactly the
   RC products of R35 × C5 and (R35 + R34) × C5, so the manufacturer's criterion is unambiguous: it is the
   time constant, and 1/e is the right measurement.

10. **Release is linear in time across the dial.** The seven marks must give **527, 444, 360, 277, 194, 110,
    27 ms**, each within 15 %.
    *Figure:* the endpoints above, plus "ALL POTENTIOMETERS ARE LINEAR TAPER" (note 4). *Source:* [8] [9] and
    schematic C-10476 [3]. **The intermediate values are derived** from the linear sweep of a 5 MΩ pot, and
    that derivation is the whole content of the test; the endpoints are the published anchors.

11. **Attack, both published endpoints.** Tone burst stepping from −40 to −6 dBm. Time to 1 − 1/e of the final
    gain reduction must be **1000 µs ± 25 %** at `ua176_attack` = 1 and **100 µs ± 25 %** at
    `ua176_attack` = 7.
    *Figures:* "ATTACK TIME: Adjustable from 100 to 1000 microseconds". *Source:* [8] [9]. **The criterion is
    weaker than the release criterion and the test says so**: 5.2 shows the slow endpoint is exactly R32 × C5
    but the fast endpoint does not fall out of any reading of the pot wiring I could make, so the manufacturer's
    measurement convention for attack is not established. The looser ±25 % carries that uncertainty. **This is
    a case where a real number exists but its conditions do not**, and the honest response is a wide bound with
    a stated reason, not a tight bound with an invented one.

12. **Attack is faster at higher ratios.** With the same burst producing the same *final* gain reduction at
    each ratio, the time to first reach 3 dB of gain reduction must be **monotonically shorter** as the ratio
    rises.
    *Figure:* "Actual attack time varies slightly based on the selected ratio. Higher ratios will maintain the
    fastest attack times." *Source:* UA's own plug-in manual [12]. A direction, not a magnitude, because UA
    give no magnitude.

13. **Programme dependence.** A step to −6 dBm must reach 90 % of its final gain reduction **sooner** than a
    step to −18 dBm does, at every ratio.
    *Figure:* none published. *Source:* this is a **circuit identity**, not a figure: the capacitor charges
    from the overdrive `e = k·y − V_T`, which is larger for a larger step (5.4). The test asserts the sign of
    an inequality that the topology guarantees, and asserts nothing about its size.

14. **Limit defeat.** With `ua176_attack` in the OFF detent, the gain reduction must be **exactly 0 dB at every
    input level up to +24 dBm**, while the transformer and tube colouration stages remain active.
    *Figure:* "By turning the attack control full counter-clockwise, the limiting action may be defeated and
    the unit performs as a normal line amplifier." *Source:* the operating manual [1].

### 11.3 The gain element

15. **The transconductance law hits its datasheet anchors.** The implemented `gm(w)` must return
    **6200 µmho ± 2 % at w = 2.2 V** and the model's total available gain reduction from idle must not exceed
    **41.9 dB**, which is 20 log₁₀(6200/50).
    *Figures:* "Transconductance … 6200 µmhos" at E_p 150 V, R_k 220 Ω, and "Grid volts for gm = 50 µmhos …
    −13". *Source:* RCA, Tung-Sol and Sylvania 6BC8 datasheets, which agree on both numbers [18] [19] [20].

15a. **The amplification factor and plate resistance close on the datasheet.** At w = 2.2 V the implemented
    `µ(w)` must return **33 to 35**, and `r_p = µ(w)/gm(w)` must return **5300 Ω ± 5 %**.
    *Figures:* "Amplification Factor … 35" and "Plate Resistance … 5300 ohms", tabulated at the same operating
    point. *Source:* RCA and Tung-Sol 6BC8 datasheets [18] [19]. **This is the strongest structural test of the
    tube model**, because it asserts three published numbers that are not independent: any error in the µ curve
    or the gm law breaks the quotient. It is also the test that would have caught my original mistake, since a
    flat µ cannot produce a plate resistance that rises (4.3.1).

15b. **The stage gain is not the transconductance ratio.** With the µ curve active, the gain reduction at
    u = 6 V must be **smaller in magnitude than the transconductance change over the same interval**, by
    between 3 and 8 dB.
    *Figures:* **none published.** *Source:* structural — `A = µ·R_L/(r_p + R_L)` with a falling µ and a rising
    r_p (4.4). The 3 to 8 dB window brackets my estimate of 5 dB with the uncertainty in `R_L`, and is a sanity
    bound, not evidence. **A model that fails this by returning equality has dropped the load division.**

15c. **Plate resistance against Sylvania's own curve, which is the deep-end check.** At grid voltages of −7,
    −8, −9 and −10 V the model's `r_p = µ(w)/gm(w)` must fall within **±40 %** of **69, 105, 145 and 180 kΩ**.
    *Figures:* the E_b = 150 V plate-resistance curve on Sylvania page 5 [20], read at ±10 kΩ (4.6.1).
    *Source:* [20]. **The fitted law of 4.3 fails this test at −9 and −10 V, and that is deliberate**: the
    test is written against the datasheet rather than against the model, so it records a known miss instead of
    hiding it. Either the law gains a corrected tail or the test carries an explicit, dated exemption naming
    4.6.1. **It must not be widened to pass.** Plate resistance is the right quantity for this check because
    it grows as the tube cuts off, so it is readable exactly where a linear transconductance axis is not.

16. **The law accelerates.** Measure decibels of gain reduction per volt of control at u = 1, 3 and 5 V. The
    three must be **strictly increasing**.
    *Figure:* the gm curve of Tung-Sol plate #6282 falls with increasing steepness on a logarithmic gm axis
    [19]. *Source:* [19]. A direction read off a published plot, asserted as a direction, because the plot is a
    scan and the magnitudes are estimates (4.3).

17. **Even harmonics cancel when the halves match.** `ua176_tube_match` = 1.0, 1 kHz at a level giving 10 dB of
    gain reduction: the second harmonic must be **at least 40 dB below the third**.
    *Figure:* **none published.** *Source:* this asserts the *structure* — a push-pull pair driven
    differentially cancels even orders exactly when the halves are identical (4.5) — and 40 dB is a numerical
    stand-in for "cancels", chosen to be well inside floating-point reach and well outside audibility.
    **Stated as a structural assertion, not as a measurement of hardware.**

18. **Mismatch reintroduces even harmonics, and a thump.** Sweeping `ua176_tube_match` from 1.0 down to 0.0
    must raise the second harmonic **monotonically**, and must raise the level of the control-voltage
    feedthrough transient at the onset of a burst **monotonically**.
    *Figure:* "If the two sections of the 6BC8 are not reasonably symmetrical, optimum balancing cannot be
    obtained", with a null target "in the order of −20 db". *Source:* the balancing procedure in the operating
    manual [1]. The −20 dB target is a real published number and the test may additionally assert that at
    `ua176_tube_match` = 0.9 the thump sits near it, but **only as a recorded value**, since the manual's
    −20 dB refers to a meter deflection under a test-signal condition I cannot reproduce exactly.

19. **The gassy-tube control reproduces the manual's field test.** With `ua176_gas` = 1 and no input, sweeping
    `ua176_release` from 7 to 1 must change the standing gain reduction by **about 0.25 dB**; with
    `ua176_gas` = 0 it must change by **less than 0.01 dB**.
    *Figure:* "An increase in the reading of more than 1/4 db indicates excessive gas and the 6BC8 should be
    discarded." *Source:* the operating manual [1]. The quarter-decibel is Universal Audio's number; the
    linear dependence on release resistance is derived (9.3.3).

### 11.4 Telling it apart from the 1176

The lab already models the 1176 and the two are relatives. A listener should be able to tell them apart, and
so should a test.

20. **The release ranges differ by roughly two to one, at both ends.** Same burst, same gain reduction, both
    models at `release` = 1: the 176 must recover to 1/e in **527 ms ± 15 %** and the 1176 in **1100 ms ± 25 %**.
    At `release` = 7: **27 ms** and **50 ms** respectively.
    *Figures:* 176, "27 to 527 milliseconds" [8] [9]; 1176, "50 ms to 1.1 s" [40]. *Source:* both
    manufacturers' own specifications. **This is the cleanest cross-model test available**, because both
    numbers are published, they differ by about the ratio of the two timing capacitors (5.6), and the test
    catches a model that borrowed the 1176's engine and relabelled it.

21. **The attack ranges differ at the fast end by five to one.** Both models at `attack` = 7, same step: the
    176 must reach 1 − 1/e in **100 µs ± 25 %** and the 1176 in **20 µs ± 25 %**. On a 1 kHz square-wave
    transient the 1176 must clamp measurably sooner.
    *Figures:* 176, "100 to 1000 microseconds" [8] [9]; 1176, "20 to 800 µs" [40]. *Source:* both
    specifications.

22. **The ratio sets do not overlap at the ends.** The 176 must offer a **2:1** setting and must offer **no
    20:1** setting; the 1176 the reverse.
    *Figure:* the 176's front-panel legend reads "2:1 4:1 8:1 12:1" [29] and its ratio control is a
    **four-position rotary**; the 1176's is four **push-buttons** reading 4, 8, 12, 20 [40]. *Source:* the
    Vintage King photograph [29] and the 1176 manual [40].

23. **There is no all-buttons mode, and there cannot be.** The model must expose no state in which two ratios
    are engaged at once.
    *Figure:* a single-pole four-position rotary switch cannot select two positions. *Source:* C-10476's S5 as
    drawn [3] and the front panel [29]. **A circuit identity asserted as a negative test**, which is worth
    having because users will ask for the feature and the answer is that Putnam invented it after this unit.

24. **The distortion signatures differ in kind.** At 10 dB of gain reduction on a 1 kHz tone at each model's
    default settings, record the second and third harmonic levels for both. The 176 at
    `ua176_tube_match` = 0.9 must show a **lower second-to-third ratio** than the 1176 in its Blue mode.
    *Figure:* **none published for either unit under gain reduction** (6.3). *Source:* structural — the 176
    cancels even orders by push-pull symmetry while the 1176's blue-stripe behaviour is second-harmonic
    dominant because its gate anti-distortion network is absent or defeated ([[1176]] sections 3.3 and 4.5)
    [40]. **The test asserts an ordering and records both spectra**; it deliberately asserts no magnitude,
    because there is no magnitude to assert.

25. **A blind A/B must be decidable.** Feed both models the same 30-second programme (a vocal, a bass and a
    drum bus) configured for the same measured gain reduction, and require the null between them to be **no
    quieter than −12 dB** relative to either output.
    *Figure:* none. *Source:* none. **This is an engineering guard-rail, not evidence**, and it exists because
    the survey's third criterion is "does it actually sound different from what we have" and a unit that lands
    within a decibel of our 1176 on every test is "a faceplate, not a model" [44]. If this test fails, the
    problem is the model, not the threshold.

### 11.5 The amplifier chain

26. **Frequency response, no limiting.** With `ua176_attack` OFF and both attenuators at 0, the response from
    20 Hz to 20 kHz must be flat within **±0.5 dB**.
    *Figure:* "FREQUENCY RESPONSE: Without limiting: ±0.5 db, from 20 cps to 20 kc". *Source:* the typewritten
    specification page [9]. **Note the conflict**: the brochure page in the same document says ±1 dB [8]. The
    test asserts the tighter figure and 6.1 gives the reason.

27. **Frequency response under 5 dB of limiting.** Same bound, **±0.5 dB**, with the input set for 5 dB of gain
    reduction.
    *Figure:* "With 5 db limiting: ±0.5 db, from 20 cps to 20 kc". *Source:* [9]. This is the only published
    statement about what limiting does to the response and it is worth asserting exactly.

28. **Maximum output and its distortion.** `ua176_attack` OFF, 50 Hz, 1 kHz and 15 kHz at a level giving
    **+24 dBm** at the output: THD must be **below 1 %** at all three.
    *Figure:* "+24 dBm with less than 1 % THD from 50 cps to 15 kc", explicitly measured after the output
    attenuator with limiting switched off. *Source:* [9], whose stated conditions the test reproduces exactly.

29. **THD under gain reduction is recorded, not bounded.** At 10 dB of gain reduction, 1 kHz, record THD and
    the first five harmonics at each ratio. **No pass/fail bound.**
    *Figure:* **none exists.** The only claim anybody makes is the brochure's qualitative "negligible increase
    in distortion at levels above" threshold [8]. *Source:* [8]. **This is exactly the case the repository's
    standard is about**: there is no published number, so the test refuses to invent one and records the value
    for regression instead. A future test may assert stability against a previously recorded value; it may not
    assert a bound as though it came from Universal Audio.

30. **Noise.** Measure signal-to-noise with the input at the limiting threshold. **Record the value; no bound.**
    *Figure:* "SIGNAL TO NOISE RATIO: 80 db" [8] [9], which states neither bandwidth nor reference level, so it
    cannot be asserted against. **Said rather than fudged.**

### 11.6 Structure, numerics and housekeeping

31. **The transformer taps are the tap fractions.** Assert the four `k_r` constants are exactly
    **0.13, 0.32, 0.60, 1.00**.
    *Figure:* drawing A-1050's voltage column: 13 %, 32 %, 60 %, 100 %. *Source:* [7]. A constants test, cheap
    and worth having, because 5.4 shows the whole ratio law rests on those four numbers.

32. **The ratio law reproduces the nameplate.** Assert `R(k) = 1 + 11·k^1.15` returns **12.0, 7.2, 4.0 and
    2.1** for the four taps, each within 15 % of the nameplate 12, 8, 4 and 2.
    *Figure:* the four nameplate ratios on the front panel [29] and the four tap percentages [7]. *Source:*
    [7] [29]. **The constants 11 and 1.15 are mine** (5.4); what the test asserts is that a law built from two
    published sets of numbers lands on the printed ratios, which is the claim section 5.4 makes.

33. **Detector polarity.** With a strongly asymmetric input (a half-wave-rectified sine, or a kick drum), the
    gain reduction with the input inverted must **differ measurably** from the gain reduction with it upright
    in half-wave mode, and must be **identical within 0.05 dB** in the full-wave development mode.
    *Figure:* **none published.** *Source:* the series-connected 6AL5 sections on C-10476 [3], read as
    half-wave (5.1). **This test exists to make my uncertain trace falsifiable**: if a measurement of real
    hardware ever surfaces showing the 176 is polarity-insensitive, this test is where the model changes.

34. **Numerical robustness.** Ten seconds of programme followed by thirty seconds of digital silence: no NaN or
    infinity in any state, the detector voltage reaches exactly zero, no subnormals remain. DC, full-scale
    square waves at 20 Hz and 20 kHz, and impulses at every knob extreme: output finite and bounded by the
    saturation limit; the control voltage never exceeds the value corresponding to `G_max`.

35. **Sample-rate invariance.** Repeat tests 1 to 13 at 44.1, 48, 88.2, 96 and 192 kHz: thresholds within
    0.3 dB and time constants within 5 % across rates.

36. **Bypass.** `ua176_bypass` on: bit-exact pass-through with reported latency.

37. **Performance.** One stereo instance at 4× oversampling and 96 kHz processing must stay inside a fixed
    budget with no allocations on the audio thread. **Estimate: under 3 % of one core on a 2020-class laptop**,
    measured with the benchmark harness rather than a unit test. The 4× figure is higher than the lab's other
    models because the loop, the tube table and the antiderivative all run at the oversampled rate.
