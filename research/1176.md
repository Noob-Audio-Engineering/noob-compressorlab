# The 1176 FET limiting amplifier: research notes for the 1176 side of `noob-compressorlab`

Research document for the 1176 model of the `noob-compressorlab` example plug-in in noob-vst-webgui-framework. The example is
a humorous, affectionate spoof of the UREI / Universal Audio 1176 FET limiting amplifier. It is not a
product and does not use any manufacturer trademark as its name; "1176", "UREI" and "Universal Audio"
appear below only to identify the hardware being discussed and the sources being cited.

Conventions used in this document:

- Bracketed numbers such as [3] refer to the numbered reference list in section 9, which gives a URL for
  every source. Where a claim rests on a single source, the URL is also given inline.
- "Estimate" marks a number that was derived or assumed by me rather than
  measured or published. "Derived" marks an equation or figure worked out here from published circuit
  facts. Nothing labelled as a measurement was invented; where sources disagree, both values are given.
- dBu is 0.775 V RMS; dBm is the same figure into 600 ohms, which is how the original documentation is
  written. dBFS is digital full scale.

## 1. What the 1176 is

### 1.1 Origin

The 1176 was designed by Bill Putnam Sr. at Universal Audio / UREI. His son's letter in the 2009 reissue
manual dates the design to 1966; the first production unit is dated June 20, 1967 [1][3]. It evolved
from the Universal Audio 175 and 176 vacuum-tube limiters and is described by the manufacturer as "the
first true peak limiter with all transistor circuitry" [1, p. 17]. Putnam's notebook experiments with the
then-new field-effect transistor led to the central idea of the design: use a FET as a voltage-controlled
variable resistor forming the bottom half of a voltage divider, put that divider in front of a solid-state
preamplifier and line driver, and derive the control voltage from a level-sensing circuit that monitors the
output [1, p. 29]. The unit was inducted into the TECnology Hall of Fame in 2008 [5].

Universal Audio was sold in 1985 (Wikipedia gives this as the end of UREI production; the last UREI
revision is H) and was re-founded in 1999 by Bill Putnam Jr. and James Putnam; their first product,
released January 4, 2000, was a reissue of the 1176LN patterned on revisions D and E [1, p. 18][5].

### 1.2 Revisions

The manufacturer's own count is "at least 13 revisions" [1, p. 17]. The revision letters that matter to
the sound are summarised below. Dates and serial ranges follow the Mix revision history [3] and the
DIYRE revision guide [4]; where the UA manual differs the difference is noted.

Sonic consequences reported across the sources: the blue-stripe revisions have more harmonic distortion,
more noise and a more "forward" midrange; the C/D/E black-face LN units are cleaner while keeping the
class-A output stage and both transformers; F and later are cleaner still, with the transformer front end
disappearing at G [1][3][4][12][14].

### 1.3 The related 1178

The 1178 is a two-channel unit often described as a stereo 1176. It uses IC op-amps instead of discrete
transistors, a class-AB output stage and a transformerless differential input, and it shares one set of
attack and release controls across both channels [48]. One vendor blog claims the 1178 lacks the
all-buttons behaviour [48]; Pulsar's 1178 emulation nevertheless offers an all-buttons mode and describes
the hardware as "cleaner and more versatile than its older brother" [19]. The two sources disagree and
neither shows a measurement.

### 1.4 Why it is famous

The reissue manual collects the reasons engineers give: extremely fast attack and release, a bright
"spectral tilt" rather than an obvious high-frequency lift, controllable distortion, and the all-button
mode [1, pp. 13 to 16]. Robjohns' 2001 review describes the tonal signature as "a slightly bright
character, more of a subtle spectral tilt than an obvious high-frequency lift" [9]. Senior's survey of
classic compressors reports that the 1176 "topped the polls" for bass processing because "the circuitry's
tonal brightening and subtle FET distortion really help the bass to cut through", provided the time
constants are kept away from the fastest settings [10]. Moore's study lists feedback topology,
program-dependent time constants and ratio, a threshold that moves with the ratio setting, very fast time
constants, and the distortion produced in all-buttons mode as the elements of its "sonic signature" [7].

## 2. Controls and their real ranges

The front panel has two large knobs (Input, Output), two small knobs (Attack, Release), four
push-buttons for ratio, a VU meter and four push-buttons for meter function [1, pp. 8 to 10].

Two facts about the knobs surprise newcomers and are stressed by every source: attack and release get
faster, not slower, as the knobs are turned clockwise [1, p. 9], and there is no threshold control; the
Input knob and the ratio buttons set the threshold together [1, p. 36 glossary].

Manufacturer starting points: both large knobs at about 24 (12 o'clock) for roughly unity gain, attack
and release at about 4 (12 o'clock), select GR metering, then raise Input until the desired gain
reduction appears [1, p. 10]. The widely quoted "Dr. Pepper" vocal setting is attack at 10 o'clock,
release at 2 o'clock, ratio 4:1, with a lot of input level [7][11].

In stereo, two units are linked with the 1176SA adapter, which makes the attack and release controls of
the two units interact and doubles the fastest attack to 40 us [1, p. 27].

## 3. Signal path and circuit behaviour

### 3.1 Block diagram

The reissue manual gives the block diagram as Input (attenuator and transformer) -> GR circuit (FET
divider) -> Preamp -> Output pot -> Output amp (line amplifier and output transformer), with a GR control
circuit fed from the preamp output and driving the FET [1, p. 29, figure 1]. The manual states plainly
that "the 1176LN is a feedback style compressor since the sidechain circuit samples the signal level
after the gain reduction" [1, p. 31].

### 3.2 Input section

A bridged-T attenuator (R1A/R1B 600 ohm, R2/R3 620 ohm) followed by input transformer T1 loaded with
R74 270 ohm [1, p. 30, figure 2]. Its stated purpose is to reduce the signal level so as not to overdrive
the FET stage, and to set the amount of compression. Revisions A to F use this circuit; G replaced it with
a differential op-amp input [1, p. 30].

### 3.3 The FET as a voltage-controlled resistor

The gain-reduction element is a junction FET (Q1, called the "VVR FET" in the manual) working as the
shunt element of a divider whose series element is R5 = 27 kohm [1, p. 31, figure 4]. The manual's
description: "the FET acts like a resistor whose resistance is controlled by the voltage applied to its
gate. The higher the voltage applied to the gate, the smaller the drain-source resistance will be" [1,
p. 31]. Larger input signals produce a larger control voltage, lower channel resistance, and lower gain.

JFET physics relevant to the model (from an applications article on FET voltage-controlled resistors
[43]):

- In the ohmic (triode) region, valid for `Vp <= Vgs <= 0` (n-channel), the drain-source resistance for
  small signals is proportional to `1 / (1 - Vgs/Vp)`; at `Vgs = 0` it equals `Vp / (-2 IDSS)`.
- The resistance depends on the drain-source voltage as well as on the gate voltage, so a signal of more
  than a few tens of millivolts across the channel produces distortion, predominantly second harmonic. The
  article states that with small signals (below about 500 mV peak to peak) distortion can be "reasonably"
  low and quotes below 3 % within plus or minus 250 mV [43].
- Feeding exactly half of the drain-source signal back to the gate (equal resistors from drain and from
  the control voltage to the gate, k = 1/2) cancels the second-order term and leaves a much smaller
  third-order residue [43].

The 1176 uses this half-signal trick: the gate network in the manual's gain-reduction schematic contains
two 2.2 megohm resistors (R6, R7) and a small capacitor between drain, gate and control voltage [1, p. 30,
figure 3]; a GroupDIY analysis of the all-buttons mode refers to "the anti-distortion circuit which feeds
exactly 1/2 the input signal to the JFET gate" [37]. A repair thread on the 1178 adds that both units also
"bootstrap the JFET source to reduce Vds (and thus distortion)", and that in normal operation the residual
harmonics measured "over 60 dB down" [40]. The same thread summarises the character of the family in one
sentence: "the FET introduces some distortion if there's more than a few millivolts of signal across it.
This is the sound of FET compressors" [40].

DIY replicas use the BF245A (cutoff around minus 2 V) [36] or the 2N5457 [35]. Estimate: with R5 = 27 kohm
and an on-resistance of a few hundred ohms, the maximum attenuation of the divider is around 35 to 40 dB.

### 3.4 Preamp, line amplifier and output transformer

After the divider, a solid-state preamp restores level; in revisions B onward it is bipolar (a Darlington
pair, "the famous 1108 circuit") [3]. The output amplifier is "a Darlington pair followed by a class A
stage based on a 2N3053 transistor", essentially the same as the Universal Audio 1108 preamplifier [1,
p. 32]. The custom output transformer is more than an impedance matcher: it has several extra windings
that provide feedback to the output stage, so the transformer is "an integral part of the feedback
network used to stabilize the output stage" [1, pp. 29, 32] and is "enclosed within the negative feedback
of the output amplifier" [9]. Revision F replaced this stage with the push-pull 1109 design [1, p. 18].

The 5002 transformer carries DC (it is "buttstacked"), and a GroupDIY thread reports that being down about
2 dB at 20 Hz is normal for it [42]. The published response is 20 Hz to 20 kHz within plus or minus 1 dB
[1, p. 38].

Gain: the reissue manual specifies 45 dB plus or minus 1 dB [1, p. 38]; the current UA product page says
50 dB [2]. With both large knobs at 24 the unit is approximately at unity gain [1, p. 10].

### 3.5 The gain-reduction control circuit (sidechain)

From the manual's description of figure 5 [1, pp. 32, 33]:

1. The sidechain input is taken from the preamp output "just before the volume control potentiometer
   (R23)". Because this point is after the FET, the loop is closed: this is the feedback.
2. The ratio push-buttons select taps on a resistor ladder (R19 68 k, R20 56 k, R21 56 k, R22 47 k after a
   1 kohm series resistor) and thereby "determine the level of the signal which is sent to the sidechain".
3. Q7 is a phase inverter, Q8 an emitter follower; Q9/Q10 form a second inverter/follower. The two
   antiphase signals feed diodes CR2 and CR3, which together act as a full-wave rectifier.
4. "A bias level is applied to the diodes by the Compression Ratio pushbutton switches. This controls the
   threshold of limiting, and is adjusted for the correct value as determined by the currently selected
   compression ratio selection." The bias ladder in the figure is R75 470, R73 560, R68 1.5 k, R64 1.5 k
   and R76 150 ohm, fed from a minus 10 V rail through R35 10 k, R60 3.9 k and the Q-bias trimmer R59 2 k.
5. The rectifier output is filtered by C22; "R55 controls the compressor's attack time by regulating how
   fast C22 is charged. Likewise, R56 determines the compressor's release time by controlling the rate at
   which C22 discharges." The capacitor voltage is applied to the FET gate.

So the detector is a diode peak detector with a fixed bias (the threshold) and a single timing capacitor
that is charged through the attack resistance whenever the rectified signal exceeds the capacitor
voltage plus the diode bias, and discharged continuously through the release resistance. A GroupDIY
poster notes the consequence: "the attack and release interact in a simple RC sidechain. If you make the
attack pot too big, the release pot is draining the control voltage as you are trying to charge it" [38].

Component values for the timing network are not printed in the reissue manual text, only in its schematic
figures, and they differ between revisions and replicas. Two data points:

- A DIY analysis of the mnats Rev D schematic found a 220 nF capacitor in parallel with the release pot;
  with the 270 kohm to 5.27 megohm release path this gives RC products of 59 ms to 1159 ms, matching the
  published 50 ms to 1.1 s range. The same poster could not reproduce the 800 us attack figure from the
  25 kohm attack pot and 220 nF with a plain RC formula and noted that a 33 nF capacitor would be needed
  to get 825 us [38].
- The schematic reproduced in the reissue manual shows C27 0.022 uF next to the attack control together
  with R54 470 ohm and R55 25 k [1, p. 32]. Derived: 470 ohm x 22 nF = 10 us and 25.5 kohm x 22 nF =
  560 us, which brackets the published 20 to 800 us range within a factor of two.

Conclusion for modelling (estimate): the published release range corresponds closely to a plain RC time
constant of the timing capacitor, whereas the published attack range is of the same order as, but not
identical to, an RC time constant. The manufacturer does not define the percentage of final gain
reduction to which the attack and release figures refer, in either the original or the reissue
documentation [1, p. 38].

### 3.6 Ratio buttons, threshold and the Q-bias

Two things change with each ratio button: the fraction of the preamp output that reaches the sidechain
amplifier (the signal ladder) and the DC bias on the rectifier diodes (the threshold ladder). A GroupDIY
thread that worked out the resistor values for adding the AE's 2:1 ratio describes the pair as "the left
sets a voltage divider to reduce the signal at higher ratios; the right changes the threshold to change
the point where compression kicks in", and notes that the circuit "changes the threshold with the ratio
to maintain about the same level of compression" [39]. The manual states the result: "selecting higher
ratios also raises the threshold level" [1, p. 21], and the UREI transfer characteristic reproduced by
Moore shows the four curves leaving the unity line at different input levels, with a harder knee at the
higher ratios [7, figure 1].

The Q-bias trimmer (R59) sets the resting gate voltage and therefore "the compression threshold" [1,
p. 26]. The factory procedure is to apply a sine, set unity output with the attack control off, then turn
R59 until the output drops by exactly 1.0 dB [1, p. 26]; Hairball's guide gives the same procedure with
+11 dBu falling to +10 dBu and explains that it "places your gain reduction FET Q1 slightly into
conduction" [35]; Gyraf calls it "the FET start point in the beginning of its conducting range" [36].
Because the FET's resistance-versus-gate-voltage law is steepest near pinch-off, this resting point makes
the first fraction of a volt of control voltage very effective, which is why the circuit can react to a
small overshoot with a large gain change.

### 3.7 Why feedback matters: loop gain sets the ratio (derived)

In a feedback compressor the ratio is not set by a gain computer; it emerges from the loop gain. Write the
sidechain tap level in dB as `Y`, the gain reduction in dB as `G` (negative), and let the sidechain produce
`G = -F(k * y - V_T)` where `y` is the tap amplitude in volts, `k` the signal-ladder gain, `V_T` the diode
bias (threshold) and `F` the FET's control-voltage-to-dB law. Linearising around an operating point, the
loop gain in dB per dB is

```
L = dG/dY = F'(e) * d(k*y)/dY = F'(e) * (ln 10 / 20) * (V_T + e),   e = k*y - V_T
```

and since the output tracks `Y = X + G`, the static slope is `dY/dX = 1 / (1 + L)`, i.e. the effective
ratio is `R = 1 + L`. Three consequences follow, all consistent with the published behaviour:

- The ratio near threshold is `1 + 0.115 * F'(0) * V_T`. Raising the diode bias raises both the threshold
  and the loop gain, which is why higher ratios come with higher thresholds and harder knees.
- Above threshold, `L` grows with the overdrive `e` as long as `F'` stays roughly constant, so the curve
  keeps bending toward limiting; when the FET law saturates (`F'` falls), the ratio stops increasing and
  the curve plateaus. This is the soft-knee-then-plateau shape of the UREI curves.
- Attack and release become level dependent: the timing capacitor charges from the overdrive `e`, which
  shrinks as gain reduction builds, so a large transient is caught faster than a small one and the last
  few dB of gain reduction arrive slowly. Will Shanks' description quoted by Moore matches: "the 1176
  will faithfully compress or limit at the selected ratio for transients, but the ratio will always
  increase a bit after the transient" [7].

A digital feedback compressor with an ordinary gain computer of slope `1 - 1/R'` in the loop has
`L = 1 - 1/R'` and therefore an effective ratio of `2 - 1/R'`, which can never exceed 2:1 (derived; the
same "ratio collapse" is discussed by plug-in developers in [34], where the suggested remedies are to
square the gain factor and double the time constants, or to multiply successive gain corrections with a
one-sample delay in the loop). Giannoulis, Massberg and Reiss advocate feed-forward designs for their
stability and predictability and place a log-domain detector after the gain computer to get "a smooth
envelope, no attack lag, and a variable knee width" [26]. An 1176 model therefore has a choice: a feedback
loop in the voltage domain with enough loop gain, which reproduces the program dependence naturally, or a
feed-forward compressor whose static curve and time constants are fitted to the feedback behaviour.
Section 7 recommends the first.

### 3.8 The LN circuit

The LN circuit "was designed to reduce the distortion that the FET introduced in the gain reduction
stage ... to ensure that the FET stayed as much within a linear region as possible" [1, p. 31]. UA kept it
secret and built it as a potted module on the Rev B board (Rev C), then integrated it on the board (Rev D)
[1, p. 31]. DIYRE summarises it as "reduced voltage to gain-reduction FET; incorporated Q-bias pot" [4].

### 4.1 Specifications

From the reissue manual [1, p. 38] unless noted:

A commercial 1176-style 500-series unit built around the same shunt-FET idea quotes THD below 0.05 % with
no gain reduction and below 0.1 % with gain reduction, rising to 3 % second harmonic when a
"Blue Stripe" style local feedback is selected [44]. Those are that product's figures, not UREI's, but they
indicate the order of magnitude that separates black-face and blue-stripe behaviour.

### 4.2 Static compression curves and thresholds

The transfer characteristic printed in the 1989 UREI manual (reproduced as figure 1 in Moore [7]) plots
output versus input in dB for the four ratios. Reading the figure: the curves leave the unity line between
roughly minus 30 dB (4:1) and minus 25 dB (20:1) input, the 20:1 curve is essentially flat above its knee
and the 4:1 curve rises gently; the knees are soft and become harder with ratio. Moore's reading of it:
"the threshold changes depending on the ratio ... At higher ratio/thresholds the knee is harder ... while
lower ratio/thresholds have a softer knee" [7].

Robjohns gives numbers: the threshold is "minus 25 dBm for a 20:1 ratio, falling to minus 32 dBm at a 4:1
ratio" [9], a spread of 7 dB. Waves' manual gives its model's thresholds as minus 12 dBFS at 20:1 and
minus 18 dBFS at 4:1 (6 dB spread) at the model's calibration of minus 18 dBFS = +4 dBu [14]. The UREI
figure suggests about 5 dB. Estimate for the model: a 5 to 7 dB rise in threshold from 4:1 to 20:1.

The manual describes the knee as soft: "The 1176LN utilizes soft knee compression and limiting" [1,
p. 21]. Moore, listening to a room mic at 4:1, 8:1, 12:1 and 20:1 with the same gain reduction, "struggles
to hear a difference" between the three highest ratios [7], which is consistent with the curves converging
into a plateau at high ratio.

### 4.3 Attack and release

No source found gives an independent oscilloscope measurement of the 1176's attack and release against
a stated criterion (for example time to 63 % or 90 % of final gain reduction). What exists:

- The manufacturer's 20 to 800 us and 50 ms to 1.1 s figures [1, p. 38].
- The RC analysis in section 3.5: the release range corresponds closely to the RC time constant of the
  timing capacitor; the attack range is of the same order as the RC of the attack path [38][1, p. 32].
- Emulation vendors' statements: Softube gives "about 20 us at the fastest setting to about 800 us on its
  slowest setting" and "between 50 ms and 1.1 s", both "program-dependent" [16]. Waves gives attack from
  1 ms (slowest) to "less than 50 microseconds" (fastest) and release from 1 s to 50 ms, and elsewhere
  says the hardware "is known for its superfast attack time of 50 microseconds" [14]. Arturia quotes
  "ultra fast attack & release parameters down to 20 microseconds" [15]. Analog Obsession's free FETish
  quotes 20 to 800 us and 50 ms to 1.1 s [25].
- Program dependence is asserted by every source but quantified by none. Wikipedia summarises the common
  description: release is "faster after transients to obtain a more consistent level, but slows down
  after sustained and heavy compression to reduce pumping effects" [5]. The manual is more careful: "The
  1176LN compression or limiting is, to some degree, program-dependent" [1, p. 15]. Moore lists "program
  dependent time constants" and "program dependent ratio" among the defining characteristics [7]. The
  feedback-loop analysis of section 3.7 gives the mechanism.
- Moore's thesis reports tone-burst analysis of a vintage black-face unit and the reissue but the
  numerical results are inside the thesis PDF, which could not be retrieved for this document [8].

Note on Moore [7]: the paper's text says attack times are "between 200-800 microseconds"; the manual it
cites says 20 to 800 us. The 200 is presumably a typographical error in the paper.

### 4.4 Distortion and frequency response

- THD is specified at or below 0.5 % with limiting at the slowest release [1, p. 38].
- Fast time constants let the gain follow individual cycles of low-frequency waveforms; the manual
  presents this as a feature: "since the attack and release can happen so fast, set at their fastest
  values, they impart minute level fluctuations over the audio. The result is a special kind of
  distortion" [1, p. 16]. Moore reproduces the mechanism from Izhaki and Case: distortion occurs when "the
  period of low frequencies is long enough for the compressor to act within each cycle rather than on the
  overall dynamic envelope" and shows spectrograms of a picked bass with the fastest settings where the
  50 to 250 Hz region is visibly altered [7, figure 6].
- Moore's thesis concludes that the 1176 "produced significant amounts of distortion when used in its
  all-buttons mode and with fast attack and release configurations" [8].
- Robjohns describes the frequency balance as a subtle spectral tilt toward brightness [9]; the reissue
  manual quotes engineers calling the effect "an equalizer kind of effect, adding a coloration that's
  bright and clear" [1, p. 13].
- Low-frequency response of the class-A stage is limited by the output transformer; about 2 dB down at
  20 Hz is reported as normal [42].

### 4.5 All buttons in ("British mode")

The manufacturer's description [1, p. 22]: "distortion increases radically due to a lag time on the attack
of initial transients (a phenomenon which might be described as a 'reverse look-ahead'). The ratio goes to
somewhere between 12:1 and 20:1, and the bias points change all over the circuit, thus changing the
attack and release times as well. The unique and constantly shifting compression curve that results yields
a trademark overdriven tone." Will Shanks (UA), as quoted by Wikipedia and Moore, likens the curve to
a "plateau" rather than a slope [5][7]. The manual adds that "the perception of distortion is increased
with lower frequencies in All-Button mode" and that the meter "will go wild, often resting at maximum"
[1, pp. 9, 14].

Electrically, a GroupDIY analysis of a Rev D-type circuit found [37]:

- pressing all four buttons short-circuits three of the five resistors in the bias ladder, raising the
  current through it from 1.62 mA to 2.98 mA;
- this loads the bias divider so that the quiescent control voltage moves from minus 2.0 V to minus
  3.2 V, i.e. the FET gate rests further from conduction. More rectified signal is needed before any gain
  reduction occurs, which produces the attack lag and a raised threshold, although the control voltage
  eventually reaches higher values;
- the half-signal anti-distortion feed to the gate is disturbed by the extra load on the DC bias point,
  so the cancellation no longer works and distortion rises. The poster compares it to "sag" in a guitar
  amplifier and argues that the character comes from distortion rather than from a higher ratio.

Gyraf's summary: "when pushing all ratio-buttons at once, you'd get a wrong bias and threshold, as well
as 20:1 compression" [36]. Users report that button combinations stack, with 4 + 20 equal to all four
[11][38]. IK Multimedia's description of its model is "a ratio between 12 and 20 with much more aggressive
slope and overdriven sound" and distortion that "drastically increases due to continuous and radical
time constants shifts" [18]. Waves attributes it to "the changing bias of the FET component" [14].

Moore's listening test on a mono drum room mic at 3 to 10 dB of gain reduction: with all buttons in "the
transients are still very pronounced with the occasional hit overshooting ... The texture is significantly
thicker and it sounds almost as if there is some low frequency distortion from the bass drum hits", and
the spectrogram shows changes at the bottom of the spectrum [7, figure 8].

### 4.6 Noise

Signal to noise is specified as better than 81 dB (manual) or 70 dB at +10 dBm (product page) [1][2].
Blue-stripe units are noisier; a DIY comparison found a Rev D build 3.4 dB quieter than a Rev A build
[4]. Waves modelled "the original pre-amp noise" and offers 50 Hz or 60 Hz hum variants [14].

## 5. Sound character, and what makes emulations sound right or wrong

Descriptions collected from engineers in the reissue manual and Moore's paper [1, pp. 13 to 16][7]:
"bright character ... nice bite", "brings out the brightness and presence", "an equalizer kind of effect",
"thick texture" on vocals, "helps the bass cut through", "makes drums bigger and more present", and, in
all-button mode, "puts the unit into overdrive". Moore rated the reissue's effect on a heavily driven rock
vocal as 5 out of 5 for added texture and observed that the high-frequency content of the vocal became
"much more apparent" [7, figure 4].

What listeners compare when judging emulations (from a hardware-versus-plug-in vocal comparison [50]):
the compression itself was "nearly identical" between one popular emulation and the hardware when
soloed, but in the mix the hardware had extra "sizzle" and high-frequency saturation that the emulation
lacked; another emulation with more saturation modelling was judged closer; a third had "crispy"
saturation but its gain control felt "less effective" despite a pinned meter. The recurring theme in
user comparisons is that the ballistics are easy to approximate and that the differences lie in the
level-dependent distortion of the FET and amplifier stages and in how the two interact during fast gain
changes.

Summarising the sources, an emulation is judged "right" when it has:

1. the fixed-threshold, input-driven workflow with thresholds that rise with ratio and a soft knee that
   hardens with ratio [1][7][9];
2. genuinely fast attack that can track waveform cycles at low frequencies, with the resulting
   distortion, and time constants that shorten on large transients [1][7][16];
3. level-dependent harmonic distortion from the FET (second harmonic dominant in blue-stripe behaviour,
   much lower in LN behaviour) and from the class-A output stage and transformer [4][40][43][44];
4. an all-buttons mode with a raised threshold, an attack lag, a plateau-like curve and markedly more
   distortion, particularly at low frequencies [1][37];
5. a bright spectral tilt rather than a flat response [9];
6. a GR meter with VU ballistics that reads gain reduction in dB [1].

It is judged "wrong" when the compression is a generic feed-forward peak compressor with the 1176's knob
labels, when the fast settings alias or click instead of distorting like the hardware, when the
all-buttons mode is merely "20:1 with more drive", or when the meter does not behave like a VU meter.

## 6. How the 1176 is simulated

### 6.1 Digital compressor design literature

- Giannoulis, Massberg and Reiss, "Digital Dynamic Range Compressor Design, A Tutorial and Analysis",
  JAES 60(6), 2012 [26]. The paper surveys designs (RMS and peak detection, feed-forward and feedback,
  linear and log domain), and recommends a feed-forward design with the detector in the log domain
  placed after the gain computer, because it gives a smooth envelope, no attack lag and a variable knee
  width [26, abstract]. The standard forms used in that tutorial, restated here (notation: `x_G` sidechain
  level in dB, `T` threshold, `R` ratio, `W` knee width, `tau` time constant, `fs` sample rate):

  ```
  soft-knee gain computer
    y_G = x_G                                   if 2 (x_G - T) < -W
    y_G = x_G + (1/R - 1) (x_G - T + W/2)^2 / (2 W)   if 2 |x_G - T| <= W
    y_G = T + (x_G - T) / R                    if 2 (x_G - T) > W
  smoothing coefficient
    alpha = exp(-1 / (tau * fs))
  branching peak detector
    y_L[n] = alpha_A y_L[n-1] + (1 - alpha_A) x_L[n]   if x_L[n] > y_L[n-1]
    y_L[n] = alpha_R y_L[n-1] + (1 - alpha_R) x_L[n]   otherwise
  smooth decoupled peak detector
    y_1[n] = max(x_L[n], alpha_R y_1[n-1] + (1 - alpha_R) x_L[n])
    y_L[n] = alpha_A y_L[n-1] + (1 - alpha_A) y_1[n]
  ```

  These are given because a feed-forward variant of the design in section 7 would use them; the paper's
  feedback discussion is summarised in section 3.7.
- Eichas, Gerat and Zölzer, "Virtual Analog Modeling of Dynamic Range Compression Systems", AES 142,
  2017 [27]: a method that uses only input/output measurements of an analog compressor, extracts its
  linear characteristics and static curves, and feeds them into an iterative optimisation that adapts the
  parameters of a generic block-based compressor model.
- Gerat, Eichas and Zölzer, "Virtual Analog Modeling of a UREI 1176LN Dynamic Range Control System",
  AES 143, 2017 [28]: the same gray-box, block-oriented method applied to a 1176LN clone. A parametric
  block-oriented model was "designed, improved, and tuned using the Levenberg-Marquardt iterative error
  minimization algorithm" from input/output measurements only, then evaluated with objective scores and
  a listening test that the authors describe as "very convincing".
- Eichas, "System Identification of Nonlinear Audio Circuits", dissertation, Helmut-Schmidt-Universität
  Hamburg [29]: collects the above; dynamic range compressors including the 1176LN are treated with "a
  block-based model describing a generic dynamic range compression system and an automated routine to
  adjust it". The related DAFx-17 paper on gray-box guitar-amplifier modelling [30] describes the general
  block structure (LTI filters plus nonlinear blocks with memory, fitted by iterative optimisation).
- Neural approaches: Hawley et al., "SignalTrain" [32] and Steinmetz and Reiss, "Efficient neural
  networks for real-time modeling of analog dynamic range compression" [31] (a temporal convolutional
  network modelling the LA-2A, real-time on CPU). A DAFx 2026 paper proposes evaluating compressor
  models against the hardware's measured gain-control voltage rather than only against output audio,
  showing that waveform metrics can fail to separate models that the control-voltage metric separates
  clearly [33]. That measurement idea (record the control voltage, compare gain trajectories) is the most
  useful methodology for the test plan in section 8.
- Developer discussion of feedback topologies in software [34]: the effective-ratio limitation of naive
  digital feedback loops and practical workarounds (squared gain and doubled time constants; multiplying
  gain corrections with a one-sample loop delay; viewing the loop as a control problem).

### 6.3 Oversampling

Two sources of aliasing exist in an 1176 model: the memoryless nonlinearities (FET, amplifier,
transformer) and the gain multiplication itself, which is amplitude modulation whose sidebands extend
beyond Nyquist when the gain moves within a few samples [51]. At 20 us attack, the control signal has a
bandwidth of the order of 1/(2 pi 20 us), about 8 kHz, and at low frequencies it follows the rectified
waveform. Developer discussions conclude that a compressor with sub-millisecond attack needs at least 2x
oversampling around the gain multiplication and the waveshapers [51]. Several vendors expose an
oversampling switch (Pulsar, Overloud) [19][22].

## 7. Recommended DSP design for noob-vst-webgui-framework

The goal is an implementation in Rust that runs in real time at 44.1 to 96 kHz, behaves like the
sources describe, and is testable. The recommended architecture is a voltage-domain feedback loop with a
single-capacitor diode detector and a FET-like control law, because that structure produces the fixed
threshold, ratio-dependent threshold and knee, program-dependent ballistics and all-buttons behaviour
with very few tunable constants. All numeric defaults below are estimates unless a source is cited; they
should be tuned against the test plan in section 8.

### 7.1 Block diagram (in words)

Per channel, at the processing rate (2x oversampled below 88.2 kHz):

1. Input trim: `x1 = x * 10^(A_in / 20)` where `A_in` is the Input knob attenuation in dB (0 at full
   clockwise, minus infinity at full counter-clockwise).
2. Input transformer: second-order high-pass at about 15 Hz (Q 0.6) and a very gentle low-pass above
   50 kHz; optional light saturation. Estimate.
3. FET divider: `x2 = g_inst[n] * x1` where `g_inst` is the instantaneous divider gain from section 7.4,
   depending on the control voltage `v[n]` and, through the FET nonlinearity, on the signal across the
   FET.
4. Preamp: fixed gain `G_pre` (dB) with soft saturation (tanh-like, symmetric); this is the sidechain tap
   point `y_tap = pre(x2)`.
5. Output pot: `x3 = y_tap * 10^(A_out / 20)`.
6. Line amplifier and output transformer: fixed gain `G_line`, class-A saturation with a small
   asymmetric (second-harmonic) term, transformer high-pass (second order, about 20 Hz, so that the
   response is about 2 dB down at 20 Hz [42]), level-dependent low-frequency saturation, and a gentle
   spectral tilt (estimate: +0.5 dB at 10 kHz, minus 0.5 dB at 100 Hz, a single first-order tilt) to
   represent the "spectral tilt" reported in [9].
7. Output.

Sidechain (feedback), from `y_tap`:

1. Ratio ladder: `s = k_r * y_tap`.
2. Full-wave rectifier with diode bias: `e = max(0, |s| - V_T,r)`.
3. Single capacitor charged through the attack resistance whenever `e` exceeds the capacitor voltage,
   discharged continuously through the release resistance (section 7.3).
4. FET control law `G_dB = F(v)` and, through it, `g_inst` (section 7.4).
5. Meter driver: `G_dB` feeds the GR meter model (section 7.8).

Fixed internal gain: choose `G_pre + G_line` so that Input and Output at their "24" marks give exactly
0 dB overall (i.e. 48 dB before the two 24 dB attenuations). The hardware figure is 45 dB plus or minus
1 dB [1] or 50 dB [2]; unity at 24/24 is the manufacturer's own "approximately unity" advice [1, p. 10].
Reference calibration: 0 dBFS corresponds to +22 dBu, which makes minus 18 dBFS equal to +4 dBu (the
calibration Waves states for its model [14]) and puts the hardware's +22 dBm maximum output at digital
full scale.

### 7.2 Control mappings

Input and Output: the panel marks are attenuation in dB but the pot taper is not linear in angle
[14]. Use a piecewise-linear table from knob rotation `theta` in [0, 1] to dB through the panel marks
(0.0 -> minus infinity, 0.08 -> minus 48, 0.20 -> minus 36, 0.33 -> minus 30, 0.50 -> minus 24, 0.62 ->
minus 18, 0.74 -> minus 12, 0.86 -> minus 6, 1.0 -> 0). Estimate based on the printed dial positions;
the exact hardware taper is not published.

Attack knob `p_a` in [1, 7], plus an OFF detent below 1:

```
tau_att(p_a) = 800 us * (20 / 800)^((p_a - 1) / 6)     geometric mapping (estimate)
```

giving 800, 434, 236, 128, 69, 37, 20 us at the integer marks. The hardware uses a resistive pot in an RC
network, so a linear taper would instead give `tau = 20 us + 780 us * (7 - p_a) / 6`. The taper is not
documented; the geometric mapping is recommended because it spreads the audible range across the dial
and is what the "Dr. Pepper" settings imply (10 o'clock is a slow attack, 2 o'clock a medium release).

Release knob `p_r` in [1, 7]:

```
tau_rel(p_r) = 1100 ms * (50 / 1100)^((p_r - 1) / 6)   geometric mapping (estimate)
```

giving 1100, 657, 393, 235, 140, 84, 50 ms. Stereo linking doubles the fastest attack to 40 us [1,
p. 27]; the plug-in may or may not reproduce that quirk.

Ratio buttons: `k_r` (signal ladder gain) and `V_T,r` (diode bias) per button, all-buttons as a fifth
state (section 7.6). Attack OFF: `g_inst = 1` with the amplifier and transformer stages still active
(the hardware's "colour without compression" [1, p. 8]).

Meter switch: GR, +4, +8, OFF (OFF may be treated as bypass, as several plug-ins do [11]).

### 7.3 Detector equations (voltage domain, feedback)

With `a_att = 1 - exp(-1 / (tau_att * fs_proc))` and `a_rel = 1 - exp(-1 / (tau_rel * fs_proc))`:

```
s[n]  = k_r * y_tap[n-1]                       (one-sample loop delay; runs at the oversampled rate)
e[n]  = max(0, |s[n]| - V_T,r)                 (full-wave rectifier with diode bias = threshold)
v[n]  = v[n-1] + a_att * max(0, e[n] - v[n-1]) - a_rel * v[n-1]
```

The `max` in the charge term is the diode: the capacitor charges only when the rectified overdrive
exceeds its current voltage; the release term acts at all times, as in the hardware's RC [1, p. 33][38].
At 44.1 kHz and 20 us the coefficient `a_att` is about 0.68 per sample at 1x, and about 0.4 per sample at
2x oversampling; the fastest attack is effectively "within a few samples", which is one more reason to run
the sidechain at the oversampled rate. Use `f64` or `f32` with denormal flushing; `v` decays
exponentially toward zero and must be clamped to zero below about 1e-9.

Because the rectifier output ripples at twice the signal frequency and the capacitor follows it when
`tau_att` and `tau_rel` are short compared with the period, this detector reproduces the fast-setting
low-frequency distortion described in [1][7] without any extra mechanism.

### 7.4 FET control law and static curve

Model the FET gain in dB as a linear-then-saturating function of control voltage:

```
G_dB(v) = -G_max * (1 - exp(-S * v / G_max))
```

with `S` the initial slope in dB per volt and `G_max` the deepest attainable gain reduction. Defaults
(estimates): `G_max = 40 dB` (limited by R5 and the FET on-resistance, section 3.3), `S = 40 dB/V`,
control voltages expressed in volts of the modelled sidechain so that thresholds can be quoted as a
diode bias `V_T,r` in volts. The linear region reproduces the near-constant dB-per-volt behaviour of a
JFET near pinch-off; the saturation reproduces the plateau at deep gain reduction.

Effective ratio at threshold, from section 3.7: `R = 1 + 0.115 * S * V_T,r`. Solving for the diode bias
that yields the nominal ratios with `S = 40`:

| Button | Target ratio at onset | `V_T,r` (V) | `k_r` relative to 4:1 (dB) so that the tap threshold rises by 6 dB across the range |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4:1 | 4 | 0.65 | 0 |
| 8:1 | 8 | 1.52 | +5.4 |
| 12:1 | 12 | 2.39 | +9.3 |
| 20:1 | 20 | 4.13 | +10.1 |

Derived, not measured: the diode bias sets the ratio, and the signal-ladder gain `k_r` is chosen so that
the threshold at the tap, `V_T,r / k_r`, rises by only about 6 dB from 4:1 to 20:1 (the 5 to 7 dB spread
of section 4.2) rather than the 16 dB the bias alone would give. Above threshold the loop gain grows with
overdrive, so the measured slope 10 dB above threshold will be higher than the onset ratio; tune `S`,
`G_max` and the four `V_T,r` against the static-curve test (section 8.1) so that the slope measured
between 6 and 16 dB above threshold matches 4, 8, 12 and 20 within about 20 %, and so that the 20:1 curve
is essentially flat.

Absolute threshold placement: choose the sidechain scaling so that at 4:1 with Input at 24 the threshold
falls near minus 26 dBFS at the plug-in input (estimate, consistent with the Waves figures of minus
18 dBFS at an Input setting of 30 [14] and with Robjohns' minus 32 dBm at 4:1 [9] when the calibration
of section 7.1 is applied). This puts typical minus 18 dBFS program into gentle compression at 24 and
heavy compression at 30 to 36, matching the manufacturer's usage advice [1, p. 10].

Instantaneous divider gain with FET nonlinearity: the FET's resistance depends on the signal across it,
so the divider's gain is not constant within a cycle. A cheap, stable form is

```
g_lin[n]   = 10^(G_dB(v[n]) / 20)
w[n]       = (1 / g_lin[n] - 1)                      (proportional to the FET conductance)
g_inst[n]  = 1 / (1 + w[n] * (1 + a2 * x2[n-1] / X0 + a3 * (x2[n-1] / X0)^2))
x2[n]      = g_inst[n] * x1[n]
```

where `x2[n-1]` is the previous output of the divider (the signal across the FET), `X0` is a reference
amplitude (the level at which distortion becomes noticeable; estimate: the equivalent of 250 mV across
the FET, mapped to about minus 14 dBFS at the divider input with the defaults above), `a2` an even-order
coefficient and `a3` an odd-order coefficient. LN behaviour: `a2 = 0.02`, `a3 = 0.05`; blue-stripe
behaviour: `a2 = 0.15`, `a3 = 0.05`; all-buttons adds to `a2` (section 7.6). Estimates; tune them so
that THD with 10 dB of gain reduction at 1 kHz stays under 0.5 % in LN mode (the specification [1]) and
reaches a few percent, second harmonic dominant, in blue-stripe mode (the 3 % figure that a hardware
clone quotes for its blue-stripe setting [44]). The distortion is largest at moderate gain reduction,
where `w` is neither tiny nor dominant and the signal across the FET is still large, which agrees with
the observation that the effect grows with input level [1, p. 16].

### 7.5 Amplifier and transformer models

- Preamp saturation: `y = tanh(x / X_pre) * X_pre` with `X_pre` set so that a +22 dBu output (0 dBFS)
  is about 1 dB into compression of the curve. Estimate.
- Line amp: same shape with a small asymmetry, `y = tanh(u) + c * (tanh(u)^2 - mean)` with `c` about
  0.03, giving a second harmonic near minus 50 dB at nominal level, rising toward the +22 dBu limit.
  Estimate. The class-A stage has no crossover distortion [1, p. 22], so no dead-zone term.
- Output transformer: second-order high-pass with corner near 20 Hz (about 2 dB down at 20 Hz [42]); a
  first-order low-pass near 60 kHz (inaudible, keeps the response within the published plus or minus
  1 dB [1]); low-frequency core saturation approximated by applying an extra soft clipper to a
  low-passed (200 Hz) copy of the signal and adding back the difference, with drive that grows with
  level. Estimate; this is the least important block for the compression behaviour and the most
  important for the "colour with attack OFF" behaviour.
- Spectral tilt: first-order tilt of plus or minus 0.5 dB between 100 Hz and 10 kHz. Estimate, from the
  qualitative description in [9]; measure a real unit before relying on this.

### 7.6 All-buttons mode

Following the electrical analysis in [37] and the manufacturer description [1, p. 22], all-buttons is a
different operating point, not a fifth ratio:

1. Bias shift: add an offset `V_off` (default 1.2 V, from the quiescent control voltage moving from minus
   2.0 V to minus 3.2 V [37]) that the capacitor must charge through before the FET law starts:
   `G_dB = F(max(0, v - V_off))`. This produces the raised threshold and the attack lag ("reverse
   look-ahead"); a small transient produces no gain reduction, a large one is caught after a delay of
   roughly `tau_att * ln(e / (e - V_off))`.
2. Loop gain: use the 20:1 signal ladder (all ladder sections shorted) and a diode bias between the
   12:1 and 20:1 values (estimate: `V_T = 3.0 V`), so that the onset ratio is between 12 and 20 [1].
3. Plateau: lower `G_max` slightly (estimate: 32 dB) or raise `S` so that the curve saturates earlier;
   Shanks' "plateau" description [5][7].
4. Distortion: the half-signal cancellation is disturbed [37]; multiply `a2` by about 5 and make it
   depend on the control voltage (`a2_eff = a2 * (1 + v / 2)`) so distortion grows with gain reduction
   and is emphasised at low frequencies, where the detector ripple is largest [1, p. 14].
5. Sag: modulate the effective bias offset by a slow low-passed version of the control voltage (time
   constant 100 ms) so that sustained compression shifts the operating point, reproducing the "constantly
   shifting compression curve" [1, p. 22] and the "sag" analogy in [37].
6. Meter: the hardware meter "goes wild, often resting at maximum" [1, p. 9]; a faithful spoof feeds the
   meter driver from the raw capacitor voltage (before the `V_off` subtraction) in this mode.

All of the numbers in this subsection are estimates to be tuned by ear and against section 8.3.

### 7.7 Oversampling and sample-rate handling

Run blocks 2 to 6 of the signal path and the whole sidechain at 2x when the host rate is below 88.2 kHz,
and at 1x at 88.2 kHz and above. Use a linear-phase or minimum-phase half-band FIR (e.g. 32 taps) for the
up- and down-sampling; latency under 1 ms is acceptable for a compressor without lookahead but should be
reported to the host. Time constants must be computed from the processing rate so that behaviour is
sample-rate independent (section 8.9). The reasons: the gain multiplication at 20 us attack and the FET
and amplifier waveshapers all generate content above Nyquist [51]; several commercial emulations expose
an oversampling switch for the same reason [19][22].

### 7.8 Meter

Ballistics: a VU meter reaches 99 % of its deflection 300 ms after a step, overshoots by between 1 % and
1.5 %, falls in the same 300 ms, is driven by a full-wave average rectifier, and reads 0 VU for 1.228 V
RMS (+4 dBu) [49]. Model it as a second-order low-pass on the meter drive with natural frequency
`omega_0` about 13 rad/s (about 2.1 Hz) and damping ratio `zeta` about 0.80 (derived: this combination
gives 1.5 % overshoot and reaches 99 % at about 300 ms; `zeta` of 0.83 gives 1 % overshoot). Run it at the
control rate (for example every 32 samples) in `f64`.

- GR mode: drive the ballistic filter with `G_dB` (0 at rest, negative under compression) and display
  the needle at the VU scale position for that many dB below 0 VU. The hardware meter is a control-voltage
  mirror calibrated by trimmers so that 10 dB of measured gain reduction reads minus 10 [35] (a DIY
  procedure calibrates 6 dB to read minus 6 [36]); it reads 0 dB with no input or no compression [1,
  p. 22].
- +4 and +8 modes: drive the ballistic filter with the mean absolute value of the output signal and
  display `20 log10(mean|y| / (0.637 * A_ref))` VU, where `A_ref` is the peak amplitude of a sine at the
  reference level (+4 dBu is minus 18 dBFS RMS in the calibration of section 7.1; +8 dBu is minus
  14 dBFS RMS).
- The web UI should receive the smoothed value at 30 to 60 Hz; the ballistics live in the audio thread so
  that the meter is independent of UI frame rate.

### 7.9 Stereo linking

The hardware links two units by connecting their sidechains through the 1176SA so that one control
voltage serves both, with the side effects of interacting attack/release controls and a 40 us minimum
attack [1, p. 27]. Waves' stereo component uses "one detector for both channel paths" [14]. Recommended:
when linked, compute `e[n]` from `max(|s_L|, |s_R|)` (or the sum of the two rectified signals, which is
closer to summing two sidechains) and run one capacitor; apply the same `g_inst` law per channel with
each channel's own FET nonlinearity so that the distortion stays channel-specific. Unlinked: two
independent sidechains. A mid/side detection option is a modern addition found in several emulations
[15][19] and is not part of the hardware.

### 7.10 Defaults and parameter list

| Parameter | Range | Default | Source of default |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input | minus infinity to 0 dB via panel-mark taper | 24 (minus 24 dB) | [1, p. 10] |
| Output | same | 24 | [1, p. 10] |
| Attack | OFF, 1 to 7 (800 to 20 us) | 4 (about 128 us with the geometric map) | [1, p. 10] |
| Release | 1 to 7 (1100 to 50 ms) | 4 (about 235 ms) | [1, p. 10] |
| Ratio | 4, 8, 12, 20, ALL | 4:1 | [1, p. 7] |
| Meter | GR, +4, +8, OFF | GR | [1, p. 10] |
| Revision character | LN, Blue | LN | [1][14] |

## 8. Test plan

The tests below are meant as unit and integration tests for the Rust implementation. Tolerances are
proposals; tighten them once the model has been tuned. Where possible, compare against the hardware's
control-voltage behaviour rather than only against output audio, following [33].

### 8.1 Static gain reduction per ratio

For each ratio and for input levels from minus 60 to 0 dBFS in 1 dB steps, feed a 1 kHz sine for 2 s
(long enough for the detector to settle at the slowest release), measure the steady-state output level
over the last 200 ms, and check:

- below threshold the output tracks the input within 0.1 dB (plus the fixed gain);
- the threshold (defined as the input level producing 1 dB of gain reduction) increases monotonically
  from 4:1 to 20:1 with a total spread between 5 and 7 dB [7][9][14];
- the slope of the output-versus-input curve measured between 6 and 16 dB above threshold is within
  20 % of 4, 8, 12 and 20 for the four buttons, and the 20:1 curve rises by less than 1 dB over that
  range;
- the curves are soft-kneed: the slope over the first 3 dB above threshold is at least 30 % lower than
  the slope 10 dB above threshold;
- the same test at 100 Hz and 10 kHz gives thresholds within 1 dB of the 1 kHz values (the detector is a
  peak detector and the sidechain is flat).

### 8.2 Attack and release timing

Tone burst: 1 kHz (and 10 kHz, which has less detector ripple) stepping from minus 40 dBFS to minus
6 dBFS at 20:1 with Input at 30, held for 1 s, then back. Extract the gain trajectory by dividing the
output envelope by the input envelope.

- Attack: time from the step to 63 % of the final gain reduction (in dB) is monotonic in the knob and
  within a factor of 1.5 of the mapped `tau_att` at the integer marks; at 7 it is below 60 us; at 1 it is
  between 400 us and 1.2 ms. The hardware definition is unpublished, so the test pins the model's own
  definition rather than a hardware truth.
- Release: time from the step down to a 63 % recovery of the gain reduction (in dB) is within 25 % of the
  mapped `tau_rel` (this is the criterion that the RC analysis in [38] supports); at 7 it is 40 to 65 ms,
  at 1 it is 0.9 to 1.4 s.
- Program dependence: a step to minus 6 dBFS reaches 90 % of its final gain reduction faster than a step
  to minus 18 dBFS does (feedback-loop property, section 3.7).

### 8.3 All-buttons behaviour

With the same bursts:

- threshold (1 dB gain reduction) is higher than at 20:1 (raised threshold [1][37]);
- time to the first 1 dB of gain reduction is longer than at 20:1 for the same burst (attack lag [1]);
- slope 10 dB above threshold is between 10 and 25 (between 12 and 20 with tolerance [1]);
- THD at 100 Hz with 10 dB of gain reduction is at least three times the THD at 20:1 with the same gain
  reduction [1][37][8];
- with a sustained tone the gain reduction drifts by more than 0.5 dB over the first second (sag)
  and settles.

### 8.4 Distortion

- Attack OFF, Input and Output at 24, 1 kHz at minus 18 dBFS: THD below 0.1 % in LN mode (the
  hardware specification is under 0.5 % with limiting [1]; a modern clone quotes under 0.05 % without
  gain reduction [44]).
- 4:1, 10 dB of gain reduction, 1 kHz, release at 1: THD below 0.5 % in LN mode [1]; between 1 % and 5 %
  in Blue mode with the second harmonic dominant [44].
- 4:1, 10 dB of gain reduction, 50 Hz, attack and release at 7: THD is at least ten times the release-1
  value (cycle-tracking distortion [1][7]).
- Aliasing: with a 15 kHz tone at minus 6 dBFS and the fastest settings at 44.1 kHz, no aliased
  component above minus 80 dBFS below 10 kHz; compare with the same test at 96 kHz.

### 8.5 Numerical robustness

- Feed 10 s of a burst followed by 30 s of digital silence: no NaN or infinity anywhere in the state; the
  detector voltage reaches exactly zero; no subnormal values remain (check with a flush-to-zero assertion
  or by inspecting the state).
- Feed DC, full-scale square waves at 20 Hz and 20 kHz, and impulses at every knob extreme: output stays
  finite and bounded by the saturation limit; the detector does not wind up beyond `G_max`.
- Alternate ratio buttons every 100 samples: no discontinuity larger than the gain change implied by the
  new threshold (smoothing test).

### 8.6 Bypass transparency

- Attack OFF and "colour" stages disabled (a test hook): output equals input times the fixed gain within
  0.01 dB and sample-aligned after latency compensation.
- Attack OFF with colour enabled: response within plus or minus 1 dB from 20 Hz to 20 kHz [1] except for
  the transformer high-pass (about 2 dB down at 20 Hz [42]); THD below 0.1 % at minus 18 dBFS.
- Plug-in bypass: bit-exact pass-through with reported latency.

### 8.7 Meter

- GR meter: apply a 1 kHz tone that produces 10 dB of steady-state gain reduction; the meter reads minus
  10 VU plus or minus 0.5 dB after settling [35]; time to 99 % of deflection is 300 ms plus or minus
  10 %; overshoot between 0.5 % and 2 % [49].
- +4 meter: a sine at minus 18 dBFS RMS reads 0 VU plus or minus 0.2 dB; +8 meter: minus 14 dBFS reads
  0 VU [1, p. 10][14].
- The meter returns to 0 in GR mode within 400 ms of silence.

### 8.8 Stereo

- Linked: a burst in the left channel only produces identical gain trajectories in both channels; equal
  inputs give equal outputs to within floating-point tolerance.
- Unlinked: the right channel shows no gain reduction when only the left is driven.

### 8.9 Sample-rate invariance

Run 8.1, 8.2 and 8.4 at 44.1, 48, 88.2 and 96 kHz; thresholds within 0.3 dB and time constants within
5 % across rates; THD figures within a factor of 1.5 (differences come from the oversampling filters).

### 8.10 Performance

One stereo instance at 2x oversampling and 96 kHz processing rate must stay under a small fixed budget
(estimate: below 2 % of one core on a 2020-class laptop) with no allocations on the audio thread; measure
with a benchmark harness rather than a unit test.
